Classification of Elements and Periodicity in Properties

Classification of Elements and Periodicity in Properties
S.No. Concept Formula Other Information
1 Dobereiner’s Triads Middle element atomic weight = (At. wt. of 1st + At. wt. of 3rd) / 2 Example: Na = (Li + K) / 2 = (7 + 39) / 2 = 23 Proposed by German chemist Johann Dobereiner (~1829) Groups of 3 elements with similar properties Middle element’s properties lie between the other two Law of Triads — limited applicability
2 Newlands’ Law of Octaves Every 8th element has properties similar to the 1st Pattern: Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F | Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl Proposed by John Alexander Newlands (1865) Elements arranged in increasing atomic weight Analogous to musical octaves Worked only up to Calcium Awarded Davy Medal (1887)
3 Mendeleev’s Periodic Law “Properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic weights.'' Elements arranged in increasing order of atomic weights Proposed by Dmitri Mendeleev and Lothar Meyer (1869) Elements with similar properties in same vertical column (group) Left gaps for undiscovered elements (e.g., Eka-Al = Ga, Eka-Si = Ge) Iodine placed before tellurium despite higher atomic weight
4 Modern Periodic Law “Physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.'' Based on Moseley’s work (1913) on characteristic X–ray spectra Atomic number (Z) = number of protons = nuclear charge Plot of sqrt(v) vs Z gives straight line (v = X-ray frequency) Periodic Law is a consequence of periodic variation in electronic configurations

6 Periods of Periodic Table Period 1 (n = 1): 2 elements - H, He Period 2 (n = 2): 8 elements - Li to Ne (2s, 2p filled) Period 3 (n = 3): 8 elements - Na to Ar (3s, 3p filled) Period 4 (n = 4): 18 elements - K to Kr (4s, 3d, 4p filled) Period 5 (n = 5): 18 elements - Rb to Xe (5s, 4d, 5p filled) Period 6 (n = 6): 32 elements - Cs to Rn (6s, 4f, 5d, 6p filled) Period 7 (n = 7): 32 elements - Fr to Og (7s, 5f, 6d, 7p filled) (number of available orbitals in energy level) Period number = highest principal quantum number (n) of elements in that period Lanthanoids (4f): Ce(58) – Lu(71), placed separately at bottom Actinoids (5f): Th(90) – Lr(103), placed separately at bottom
7 s–Block Elements Groups 1 & 2 (Alkali and Alkaline Earth Metals) 2) Group 1 (Alkali metals): Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr Group 2 (Alkaline earth metals): Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra Highly reactive metals with low ionization enthalpies Lose outermost electron(s) to form +1 or +2 ions Metallic character and reactivity increase down the group Never found in pure state in nature (too reactive) Compounds predominantly ionic (except Li and Be — more covalent)
8 p–Block Elements Groups 13 to 18 (together with s-block = Main Group / Representative Elements) Non–metallic character increases left to right across a period Metallic character increases down a group Noble gases: completely filled valence shell — very low chemical reactivity Halogens and chalcogens: highly negative electron gain enthalpies 18) due to noble gas properties
9 d–Block Elements (Transition Elements) Groups 3 to 12 (centre of Periodic Table) All are metals Form coloured ions Exhibit variable valence (oxidation states) Show paramagnetism Often used as catalysts typical transition element properties Bridge between chemically active s–block metals and less active p–block elements
10 f–Block Elements (Inner Transition Elements) Lanthanoids: Ce(58) to Lu(71) [4f series] Actinoids: Th(90) to Lr(103) [5f series] Elements after uranium (Z > 92) = Transuranium Elements All are metals Properties similar within each series Actinoids: more complicated chemistry than lanthanoids (many oxidation states) Actinoids are radioactive Many actinoids made only in nanogram quantities by nuclear reactions Placed separately to maintain periodic table structure
11 Metals, Non– metals & Metalloids Metals: > 78% of all known elements, left side of PT Non–metals: top right of PT, < 20 in number Metalloids (semi-metals): Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te - border elements Metals: solids at RT (except Hg), good conductors, malleable, ductile Non–metals: solids/gases at RT (except Br), poor conductors, brittle Metallic character increases down a group and decreases left to right across a period Non–metallic character increases left to right and decreases down a group Metalloids show properties of both metals and non–metals
12 Atomic Radius Covalent Radius = half the bond distance in a covalent molecule atomic radius = 99 pm Metallic Radius = half the internuclear distance in metallic crystal Example (Cu): internuclear distance = 256 pm Atomic radius decreases across a period (left to right) — increasing nuclear charge, same shell Atomic radius increases down a group — increasing principal quantum number (n), more shielding Noble gas radii (van der Waals) are very large — not compared with covalent radii
13 Ionic Radius Cation radius < Parent atom radius (fewer electrons, same nuclear charge) Anion radius > Parent atom radius (more electrons, increased repulsion) Example: F atomic = 64 pm; F- ionic = 136 pm 95 pm Isoelectronic species: same number of electrons electrons Among isoelectronic species: higher Ionic radii follow same periodic trends as atomic radii
14 Ionization iH Always positive (energy required to remove electron) enthalpies increase) Increases across a period (increasing nuclear charge, decreasing atomic size) Decreases down a group (increasing shielding, farther nucleus) Anomaly: IE(B) < IE(Be) — 2p electron more shielded than 2s Anomaly: IE(O) < IE(N) — paired 2p electron in O has extra repulsion (Hund’s rule) Maxima at noble gases (stable filled shells); Minima at alkali metals Used to determine group of element from big jump in successive ionization values

16 Electronegativity Pauling scale: F = 4.0 (highest, reference value) Period II: Li = 1.0, Be = 1.5, B = 2.0, C = 2.5, N = 3.0, O = 3.5, F = 4.0 Period III: Na = 0.9, Mg = 1.2, Al = 1.5, Si = 1.8, P = 2.1, S = 2.5, Cl = 3.0 Group 1: Li = 1.0, Na = 0.9, K = 0.8, Rb = 0.8, Cs = 0.7 Group 17: F = 4.0, Cl = 3.0, Br = 2.8, I = 2.5, At = 2.2 Qualitative measure of ability of atom in compound to attract shared electrons Not a measurable quantity (unlike ionization enthalpy) Increases across a period (left to right) — decreasing atomic radius Decreases down a group — increasing atomic radius Trend similar to ionization enthalpy Directly related to non–metallic properties; inversely to metallic properties Not constant for an element — varies with bonding partner
17 Valence / Oxidation State Group: 1, 2, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Valence e-: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Valence = number of outermost electrons OR 8 minus outermost electrons Oxidation state of F always -1 (most electronegative) Transition elements show variable valence (many oxidation states) Actinoids especially show large number of oxidation states Oxidation state = charge acquired by atom based on electronegativity More electronegative atom is given negative oxidation state
18 Anomalous Properties of 2nd Period Elements 1st member of each group differs from rest due to: • Small size and large charge/radius ratio covalency = 4 • Other members have 9 valence orbitals (3s, 3p, Example: max 4 bonds) vs expands to 6 bonds) Li and Be form covalent compounds (unlike ionic Na, Mg) Diagonal Relationship: Li resembles Mg; Be resembles Al p–block 1st members form pp–pp multiple bonds (C = C, N = N, C = O, etc.) Later members prefer single bonds (larger atoms, weaker pi overlap)
19 Periodic Trends in Chemical Reactivity Reactivity highest at left extreme (alkali metals - lose e- easily) Reactivity highest at right extreme (halogens - gain e- easily) Reactivity lowest at centre of period Oxide nature: Left extreme → basic ; Right extreme → acidic + → (basic) + → (acidic) Centre oxides: amphoteric , or neutral , , Highly reactive elements do not occur in nature in free state Metallic character highest at extreme left and decreases across period Non–metallic character increases left to right across a period Within transition metals (3d), change in atomic radii is much smaller 4f (lanthanoid) series: change in atomic radii even smaller (lanthanoid contraction) Transition metals less electropositive than Group 1 & 2 metals
