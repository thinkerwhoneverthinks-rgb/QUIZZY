BIOLOGICAL CLASSIFICATION

TOPIC1
From gross morphology to cellular, reproductive and phylogenetic criteria

BASIS OF CLASSIFICATION

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Biological classification = arranging organisms into groups based on shared characters that reflect natural (evolutionary) relationships.
  • Early systems used visible morphology; modern systems add cellular, physiological, reproductive and phylogenetic evidence.
  • A good classification should be objective, stable, predictive and place organisms with common ancestry together.
  • As scientific knowledge improved (microscopy, biochemistry, molecular data), criteria changed and systems were revised.
  • Two-kingdom classification was easy but inadequate for microbes, fungi, and many unicellular forms.

🖼️ Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🌿Aristotle: classified plants as trees/shrubs/herbs; animals by red blood vs no red blood.
  • 👑Linnaeus: Two Kingdom system → Plantae and Animalia.
  • 🔬Limitation: two-kingdom system did not separate prokaryotes vs eukaryotes and uni- vs multicellular.
  • 🍄Fungi were wrongly kept with plants due to cell wall, despite heterotrophic nutrition and chitin wall.
  • 🧫Modern basis: cell structure, cell wall nature, body organisation, nutrition, reproduction, phylogeny.
  • 🧭Whittaker (1969): five kingdoms using multiple criteria; aimed for a more phylogenetic classification.

🔎WHY TWO-KINGDOM CLASSIFICATION BECAME INADEQUATE

  • Could not separate prokaryotes from eukaryotes (both mixed together).
  • Could not separate unicellular from multicellular organisms clearly.
  • Grouped photosynthetic algae and non-photosynthetic fungi together under plants.
  • Used cell wall presence as a unifying plant trait, but wall composition differs (cellulose vs chitin).
  • Many organisms did not fit neatly as ‘plant’ or ‘animal’ (especially microbes and protists).

⚙️MODERN CRITERIA (BASIS) USED IN CLASSIFICATION

  • Cell structure: prokaryotic vs eukaryotic; nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
  • Nature of cell wall: cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), peptidoglycan-like (many prokaryotes), absent (animals).
  • Body organisation: cellular level to tissue/organ/organ-system level.
  • Mode of nutrition: autotrophic (photo/chemo) vs heterotrophic (saprophytic/parasitic/holozoic).
  • Reproduction: asexual/sexual patterns, spores, gametes, life-cycle features.

TOPIC2
Scientific classification using cell structure, organisation, nutrition, reproduction & phylogeny

FIVE KINGDOM SYSTEM (WHITTAKER, 1969)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • R.H. Whittaker (1969) proposed 5 kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia.
  • It uses multiple criteria: cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction, phylogenetic relationships.
  • It separates prokaryotes vs eukaryotes and unicellular vs multicellular organisms clearly.
  • It recognises Fungi as a separate kingdom (heterotrophic absorptive, chitin wall) from green plants.
  • It places all unicellular eukaryotes together in Protista, forming a link among plant/animal/fungal lines.

🖼️Diagram

Flow: criteria used by Whittaker lead to separation into five kingdoms.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧫Monera: all prokaryotes; no nuclear membrane; wall usually non-cellulosic (Mycoplasma lacks wall).
  • 🦠Protista: unicellular eukaryotes; diverse nutrition; mostly aquatic; boundaries not sharply defined.
  • 🍄Fungi: eukaryotic; mostly multicellular; chitin wall; absorptive heterotrophs; yeast is unicellular.
  • 🌿Plantae: eukaryotic; cellulose wall; mainly photosynthetic autotrophs.
  • 🐾Animalia: eukaryotic; no cell wall; ingestive (holozoic) nutrition; organ systems common.
  • 🧠Phylogenetic aim: classification reflects evolutionary relationships, not just appearance.

🔎WHY FIVE KINGDOMS? (LIMITATIONS RESOLVED)

  • Two-kingdom system could not separate prokaryotes from eukaryotes.
  • It mixed unicellular and multicellular forms within the same kingdom (e.g., algae).
  • It wrongly grouped fungi with plants just because both had cell walls.
  • It ignored key differences in wall: fungi chitin vs plants cellulose.
  • Five-kingdom system placed all prokaryotes → Monera and unicellular eukaryotes → Protista.

⚙️CRITERIA USED (EXAM FOCUS)

  • Cell structure: prokaryotic vs eukaryotic; presence/absence of nuclear membrane.
  • Body organisation: cellular, tissue, organ, organ system levels.
  • Nutrition: autotrophic (photo/chemo) vs heterotrophic (absorptive/ingestive).
  • Reproduction: asexual/sexual patterns considered along with life cycles.
  • Phylogenetic relationships: attempts to reflect evolutionary relatedness.

TOPIC3
Bacteria as prokaryotes: everywhere, many shapes, and extreme metabolic diversity

KINGDOM MONERA OVERVIEW

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Kingdom Monera includes all prokaryotic organisms; bacteria are its sole members (Whittaker).
  • Cells lack a true nucleus and nuclear membrane; genetic material is in a nucleoid region.
  • They are the most abundant micro-organisms and are cosmopolitan in distribution.
  • Found in soil, water, air, inside/on organisms, and in extreme habitats (hot springs, deserts, snow, deep oceans).
  • As a group, bacteria show the most extensive metabolic diversity among organisms.
  • Nutrition broadly: autotrophic (photosynthetic/chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (depend on others/dead matter).

🖼️ Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔬Prokaryotic cell plan: no nuclear membrane; generally simple structure but complex behaviour
  • 🌍Ubiquitous: from a handful of soil to deep oceans; also parasitic on other organisms
  • 🧩Shape-based groups: coccus, bacillus, vibrio, spirillum
  • 🌿Autotrophs: photosynthetic (use light) and chemosynthetic (oxidise inorganic substrates)
  • 🍂Heterotrophs: vast majority; many act as decomposers on dead organic matter

🔎SHAPES OF BACTERIA

  • Coccus (cocci): spherical cells; may occur singly or in groups.
  • Bacillus (bacilli): rod-shaped; often drawn as elongated rectangles with rounded ends.
  • Vibrio (vibrio): comma-shaped curved rod.
  • Spirillum (spirilla): spiral/helical form; appears as wavy coils.
  • Shape grouping is morphological; it does not by itself indicate nutrition or habitat.

⚙️METABOLIC DIVERSITY: AUTOTROPHS VS HETEROTROPHS

  • Autotrophic bacteria synthesise food from inorganic substrates.
  • Photosynthetic autotrophs: use light energy; e.g. cyanobacteria with chlorophyll a.
  • Chemosynthetic autotrophs: oxidise inorganic substances (e.g. ammonia, nitrites) to release energy for ATP.
  • Chemosynthetic bacteria are important in nutrient recycling (notably nitrogen cycle).
  • Heterotrophic bacteria are most abundant; depend on other organisms or dead organic matter.

TOPIC4
Harsh-habitat prokaryotes with a different cell wall; methanogens produce methane (biogas) from dung

ARCHAEBACTERIA

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Archaebacteria are prokaryotic microbes adapted to harsh habitats due to a different cell wall structure than other bacteria.
  • They occur in extreme salty areas, hot springs, and anaerobic marshy habitats.
  • Major groups (by habitat): halophiles, thermoacidophiles, and methanogens.
  • Their different cell wall structure is responsible for survival in extreme conditions.
  • Methanogens occur in the ruminant gut and anaerobic dung pits; they produce methane that contributes to biogas.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧱Cell wall structure is different from other bacteria; this supports extreme survival.
  • 🧂Halophiles thrive in extremely saline areas like salt lakes and salt pans.
  • ♨️Thermoacidophiles live in hot springs; tolerate high temperature (often with low pH).
  • 🌿Methanogens are anaerobes in marshes, rumen, and dung; release CH4.
  • 🐄Methanogens in cows/buffaloes contribute to methane formation during digestion in the rumen.

🔎EXTREME HABITATS (TYPES) + EXAMPLES

  • Halophiles: extremely saline environments (salt pans, salt lakes).
  • Thermoacidophiles: hot springs; tolerate very high temperature (often acidic).
  • Methanogens: anaerobic marshes, sewage, dung pits, and ruminant rumen.
  • Classification note (exam-safe): earlier placed under Monera in five-kingdom; in the three-domain view, archaebacteria belong to Archaea.

⚙️METHANOGENS & BIOGAS

  • Methanogens are anaerobic and produce methane (CH4) during breakdown of organic matter.
  • In cows/buffaloes, they occur in the anaerobic rumen and are responsible for methane production from digested material.
  • In biogas plants, dung + water slurry is kept anaerobic; methane-rich gas is collected as fuel.
  • Biogas is methane-rich and commonly contains CO2 along with small amounts of other gases (composition varies with feed and conditions).

TOPIC5
True bacteria: rigid wall + diverse nutrition; plus wall-less smallest cells

EUBACTERIA AND MYCOPLASMA

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Eubacteria are "true bacteria" with a rigid cell wall; if motile, they bear flagella.
  • Show widest metabolic diversity: photosynthetic, chemosynthetic autotrophs and abundant heterotrophs.
  • Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) are photosynthetic prokaryotes with chlorophyll a.
  • Some cyanobacteria fix atmospheric N2 in specialised cells called heterocysts (e.g., Nostoc, Anabaena).
  • Bacteria reproduce mainly by binary fission; many form spores in unfavourable conditions.
  • Mycoplasma are the smallest living cells and completely lack a cell wall; can survive without oxygen; many are pathogenic.

🖼️ Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧱Eubacterial cell wall is rigid (non-cellulosic); helps maintain shape and protection.
  • 🌀Motility: many eubacteria move using flagella.
  • 🌿Cyanobacteria: unicellular/colonial/filamentous; colonies often have gelatinous sheath.
  • 🧪Chemosynthetic bacteria oxidise NH3, NO2−, NO3− etc. to make ATP; key in nutrient cycling.
  • 🍂Heterotrophic bacteria are major decomposers; some useful (curd, antibiotics), some pathogenic.
  • 🧬Bacteria can show primitive DNA transfer (genetic recombination), besides asexual division.

🔎CYANOBACTERIA (BLUE-GREEN ALGAE)

  • Photosynthetic prokaryotes with chlorophyll a; release O2 during photosynthesis.
  • Forms: unicellular, colonial, or filamentous; freshwater, marine, and terrestrial.
  • Colonies often have a gelatinous sheath; can form blooms in polluted water bodies.
  • N2 fixation occurs in heterocysts (thick-walled cells providing low O2 microenvironment).
  • Examples: Nostoc, Anabaena (heterocystous, nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria).

⚙️NUTRITION TYPES IN EUBACTERIA + ECOLOGICAL ROLES

  • Photosynthetic autotrophs: mainly cyanobacteria; primary producers in many habitats.
  • Chemosynthetic autotrophs: oxidise inorganic substrates (NH3, NO2−, H2S, Fe2+) for energy.
  • Chemosynthetic bacteria recycle key elements: N, P, Fe, S; maintain soil and water fertility.
  • Heterotrophs (most abundant): decomposers of dead organic matter; mineralise nutrients.
  • Human relevance: useful (curd, antibiotics, symbiotic N2 fixation) and harmful (pathogens).

TOPIC6
Unicellular eukaryotes, mostly aquatic; diverse nutrition and reproduction

KINGDOM PROTISTA OVERVIEW

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Protista includes all single-celled eukaryotes; boundaries are not well defined.
  • Primarily aquatic; many form plankton and thrive in moist habitats.
  • A link kingdom showing traits overlapping plants, animals and fungi.
  • Cells have true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
  • Locomotion in many via flagella, cilia, or pseudopodia (in some groups).
  • Reproduction occurs asexually and sexually; sexual process involves cell fusion and zygote formation.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧫Habitat: mostly water-based (freshwater/marine); some in moist soil.
  • 🧠Eukaryotic cell plan: nuclear membrane present; organelles present.
  • 🧩Boundaries blur: photosynthetic protists may resemble plants; protozoans resemble animals.
  • 🧬Asexual reproduction common (e.g., fission); sexual reproduction forms a zygote.
  • 🧭Movement structures: flagella/cilia in many; enables active swimming/feeding.

🔎CELL FEATURES (EXAM LENS)

  • Eukaryotic: nuclear membrane present; genetic material in a true nucleus.
  • Membrane-bound organelles present (e.g., mitochondria; chloroplasts in photosynthetic forms).
  • Covering may vary: some have cell wall/pellicle; many protozoans lack a typical wall.
  • Locomotion/feeding aided by cilia, flagella, or pseudopodia in different protists.

⚙️REPRODUCTION PATTERNS

  • Asexual: common and rapid; usually by cell division (binary fission in many).
  • Sexual: involves cell fusion (gamete/cell fusion) followed by zygote formation.
  • Sexual reproduction increases variation; often triggered by changing conditions.

TOPIC7
Major protistan groups: key traits, habitat, nutrition, and exam-favourite examples

PROTISTA GROUPS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Protista = mostly unicellular eukaryotes with nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
  • Primarily aquatic; many are planktonic; some have cilia/flagella/pseudopodia.
  • Acts as a link between plants, animals and fungi (mix of autotrophy and heterotrophy).
  • Reproduction: asexual (cell division) and sexual (cell fusion → zygote).
  • Major groups (NCERT): chrysophytes, dinoflagellates, euglenoids, slime moulds, protozoans.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🌿Chrysophytes (diatoms/desmids): photosynthetic plankton; diatoms have silica walls.
  • 🌊Diatomaceous earth: fossil silica deposits; used in polishing and filtration.
  • 🧭Dinoflagellates: stiff cellulose plates on outer surface; two flagella (transverse + longitudinal).
  • 🟥Red tides: blooms of red dinoflagellates (e.g. Gonyaulax) releasing toxins.
  • 🟢Euglenoids: no cell wall; pellicle gives flexibility; mixotrophic (auto + hetero).
  • 🍂Slime moulds: saprophytic; form multinucleate plasmodium; spores resistant.

🔎GROUP-WISE HIGH-YIELD CHARACTERISTICS + EXAMPLES

  • Chrysophytes: diatoms + golden algae (desmids); freshwater/marine; planktonic; mostly photosynthetic.
  • Diatoms: two overlapping shells like a soap box; wall embedded with silica → very resistant.
  • Diatoms leave silica deposits → diatomaceous earth; used in polishing, oil/syrup filtration.
  • Dinoflagellates: mostly marine; pigment diversity (yellow/green/brown/blue/red).
  • Cell wall: stiff cellulose plates on outer surface; 2 flagella—one transverse in furrow, one longitudinal.

⚙️PROTOZOAN GROUPS (4) + DISEASE LINKS

  • Amoeboid: pseudopodia for movement/feeding; example Amoeba; parasite example Entamoeba.
  • Marine amoeboids may have silica shells on surface (exam trap: not a diatom feature).
  • Flagellated: have flagella; free-living or parasitic; example Trypanosoma (sleeping sickness).
  • Ciliated: thousands of cilia; gullet for ingestion; example Paramoecium.
  • Sporozoans: infectious spore-like stage; example Plasmodium causes malaria.

TOPIC8
Heterotrophic, hyphal eukaryotes with chitinous walls; sexual cycle steps: plasmogamy → karyogamy → meiosis

KINGDOM FUNGI BASICS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Fungi are eukaryotic, mostly filamentous heterotrophs with chitinous cell walls.
  • Body is made of thread-like hyphae; a mass/network of hyphae is mycelium.
  • Hyphae may be septate (with cross-walls) or coenocytic (aseptate, multinucleate).
  • Nutrition is absorptive: enzymes digest externally, soluble products absorbed.
  • Reproduction occurs by vegetative, asexual spores, and sexual methods.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧫Cosmopolitan: air, water, soil, plants/animals; thrive in warm, humid places.
  • 🧱Cell wall mainly chitin + polysaccharides (plants mainly have cellulose).
  • 🧵Hyphae form mycelium; yeast is the key unicellular exception.
  • 🧬Sexual cycle sequence: plasmogamy → karyogamy → meiosis.
  • ♻️Major decomposers: many are saprophytes driving mineral cycling.
  • 🤝Symbiosis: lichen (fungus+alga) and mycorrhiza (fungus+roots).

🔎NUTRITIONAL MODES (WHAT THEY FEED ON)

  • Saprophytes: absorb nutrients from dead/decaying organic matter (decomposers).
  • Parasites: obtain food from living hosts; often cause plant/animal diseases.
  • Symbionts: mutually beneficial associations (lichen, mycorrhiza).
  • Key idea: fungi are absorptive; no ingestion like animals, no photosynthesis like plants.

⚙️REPRODUCTION: VEGETATIVE, ASEXUAL, SEXUAL

  • Vegetative: fragmentation, fission, budding (budding common in yeast).
  • Asexual spores: conidia, sporangiospores, zoospores; formed in spore-bearing structures.
  • Sexual spores: oospores, ascospores, basidiospores; formed in specialised reproductive structures (often in fruiting bodies in many groups).
  • Sexual cycle steps: plasmogamy (cytoplasm fusion) → karyogamy (nuclear fusion) → meiosis (haploid spores).
  • In many ascomycetes/basidiomycetes: a prolonged dikaryophase (n + n) occurs before karyogamy.

⚖️SEPTATE vs COENOCYTIC HYPHAE

TOPIC9
Classify fungi by mycelium (septate/coenocytic) and spores/fruiting bodies

FUNGAL CLASSES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Fungi are classified into phycomycetes, ascomycetes, basidiomycetes and deuteromycetes mainly by mycelium type, spore formation and fruiting bodies.
  • Mycelium may be coenocytic (aseptate) or septate; this is a key class-level character.
  • Asexual spores include zoospores, sporangiospores, and conidia (depending on group).
  • Sexual spores include oospores, ascospores, and basidiospores; produced in distinct structures (phycomycetes commonly form zygospore; some form oospore).
  • Fruiting bodies: ascocarp (ascomycetes) and basidiocarp (basidiomycetes); deuteromycetes lack known sexual stage.

🖼️Diagram

Four classes of fungi distinguished by mycelium and spore/fruiting structures.

🎯Key Concepts

  • MyMycelium: phycomycetes coenocytic; others usually septate and branched.
  • SpSporangium makes spores endogenously (inside), typical in phycomycetes.
  • CoConidia are exogenous asexual spores on conidiophores (ascomycetes, deuteromycetes).
  • AsAscus (asci) contains ascospores; asci are packed into an ascocarp.
  • BaBasidium bears typically 4 basidiospores externally; basidia are arranged in a basidiocarp.
  • DuDeuteromycetes (imperfect fungi): only asexual/vegetative phase known; sexual stage not observed.

🔎PHYCOMYCETES (lower fungi)

  • Habitat: aquatic; moist, damp places; decaying wood; many obligate plant parasites.
  • Mycelium: aseptate, coenocytic (multinucleate continuous tubes).
  • Asexual spores: zoospores (motile) or aplanospores (non-motile).
  • Spores formed endogenously inside a sporangium.
  • Sexual spores: commonly zygospore; some phycomycetes (oomycetes) form oospore.

⚙️ASCOMYCETES, BASIDIOMYCETES, DEUTEROMYCETES (quick traits)

  • Ascomycetes (sac fungi): septate branched mycelium; conidia on conidiophores; ascospores in asci within ascocarps; examples Aspergillus, Penicillium, Neurospora, yeast Saccharomyces.
  • Basidiomycetes: septate branched mycelium; asexual spores generally absent; plasmogamy often via somatic fusion; basidium forms typically 4 basidiospores exogenously; basidiocarp; examples Agaricus, Puccinia (rust), Ustilago (smut).
  • Deuteromycetes (imperfect fungi): sexual stage not observed; septate branched mycelium; reproduce by conidia; many are decomposers; examples Alternaria, Colletotrichum, Trichoderma.

TOPIC10
Quick comparison: cell wall, nutrition, and alternation of generations in plants

PLANTAE AND ANIMALIA

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Kingdom Plantae includes eukaryotic organisms with a mainly cellulose cell wall; most are photosynthetic.
  • Most plants are autotrophic (photosynthetic). Some plants are heterotrophic (especially parasitic forms).
  • Alternation of generations: plants show alternating sporophyte (2n) and gametophyte (n) phases.
  • Kingdom Animalia includes multicellular eukaryotes that lack a cell wall and are heterotrophic.
  • Many animals show holozoic nutrition: ingestion → digestion (in alimentary canal/gastrovascular cavity) → absorption; reserves commonly as glycogen/fat.

🖼️Diagram

Plantae vs Animalia core traits + plant life cycle: sporophyte (2n) → meiosis → spores (n) → gametophyte (n) → fertilisation → zygote (2n) → sporophyte.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧱Plantae: cell wall present (mainly cellulose); Animalia: cell wall absent.
  • 🌞Most plants have chloroplasts with chlorophyll and carry out photosynthesis (primary producers).
  • 🪰Insectivorous plants are photosynthetic; insects mainly supply nitrogen/minerals (e.g., Venus flytrap, bladderwort).
  • 🧵Cuscuta is a parasitic plant: derives food from host via haustoria (often essentially non-photosynthetic).
  • 🍽Animalia: heterotrophic, usually holozoic; directly/indirectly depend on plants for food.
  • 🔁Alternation of generations: sporophyte (2n) makes spores (n) by meiosis; spores form gametophyte (n); gametophyte makes gametes.

🔎KINGDOM PLANTAE:

  • Eukaryotic; mostly chlorophyll-containing and photosynthetic.
  • Cell wall mainly of cellulose; large central vacuole common.
  • Includes algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms, angiosperms.
  • Mostly autotrophic; exceptions include parasitic plants and insectivorous plants (still photosynthetic).
  • Insectivorous plants trap insects mainly for nitrogen/minerals; carbon comes mainly from photosynthesis.

⚙️ALTERNATION OF GENERATIONS (AOG): CORE FLOW

  • Sporophyte (2n) produces spores (n) by meiosis in sporangia.
  • Spores (n) germinate and form gametophyte (n) by mitosis (free-living or dependent varies).
  • Gametophyte (n) produces gametes (n) by mitosis.
  • Fertilisation: gamete fusion → zygote (2n) → embryo → sporophyte (2n).
  • Relative dominance varies: bryophytes (gametophyte dominant), pteridophytes+seed plants (sporophyte dominant).

Acellular infectious agents + lichen symbiosis: structure, genetic material, examples

VIRUSES, VIROIDS, PRIONS & LICHENS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Viruses are non-cellular obligate intracellular parasites: inert outside host, replicate inside.
  • A virus is a nucleoprotein: nucleic acid + protein coat (capsid) made of capsomeres.
  • Viral genome is either RNA or DNA; never both in the same virus.
  • Viroids are infectious free RNA molecules without protein coat.
  • Prions are infectious agents of abnormally folded proteins (no nucleic acid).
  • Lichens are mutualistic associations of algae/cyanobacteria and fungi.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧬Viral genetic material is infectious; capsid protects genome and aids host attachment.
  • 🧫Outside host cell: viruses can be crystallised; metabolically inert.
  • 🧷Bacteriophages: typically dsDNA; head–tail structure; infect bacteria.
  • 🥔Viroid example: potato spindle tuber disease; discovered by T.O. Diener (1971).
  • 🧠Prion diseases: BSE (mad cow) in cattle; variant CJD in humans.
  • 🌿Lichens: phycobiont makes food; mycobiont provides shelter, water, minerals.

🔎VIRUSES: STRUCTURE, GENOME & KEY HISTORY

  • Capsid = protein coat; subunits = capsomeres; symmetry: helical or polyhedral.
  • Genome: RNA or DNA only; plant viruses often ssRNA (e.g., TMV).
  • Animal viruses: ssRNA/dsRNA or dsDNA (varies with virus).
  • Bacteriophage (infects bacteria): usually dsDNA; head, tail, tail fibres for attachment.
  • Ivanowsky (1892): tobacco mosaic agent passed bacteria-proof filters.

⚙️DISEASES & SIGNIFICANCE

  • Human viral diseases: influenza, mumps, smallpox, herpes; AIDS caused by HIV.
  • Plant viral symptoms: mosaic, leaf rolling/curling, yellowing, vein clearing, dwarfing.
  • Viroids: smallest known infectious agents; RNA of low molecular weight; no capsid.
  • Prions: transmit neurodegeneration; remarkable because infectious agent is protein only.
  • Lichens: mycobiont absorbs water/minerals + protection; phycobiont synthesises food.
