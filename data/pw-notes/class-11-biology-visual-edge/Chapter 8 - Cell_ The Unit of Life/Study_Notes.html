Chapter 8: Cell — The Unit of Life

TOPIC1
Cell = basic unit of life; modern cell theory: cellular unity + continuity

CELL AND CELL THEORY

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms.
  • Unicellular organisms are independent because a single cell performs all essential life functions.
  • Cell theory explains the unity behind the diversity of living organisms: all have cellular organisation.
  • Integrity of cellular organisation is necessary for living phenomena (physiological/behavioural) to be shown.
  • Modern cell theory states: organisms are made of cells and products of cells, and new cells arise from pre-existing cells.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧑‍🔬Leeuwenhoek: first to see and describe a live cell.
  • 🔎Robert Brown: discovered the nucleus.
  • 🌿Schleiden (1838): all plants are composed of cells forming tissues.
  • 🐾Schwann (1839): animals are composed of cells and products of cells; noted plasma membrane.
  • ➗Virchow (1855): Omnis cellula-e cellula = every cell from a pre-existing cell.
  • 🧩Early theory limitation: did not explain how new cells are formed; Virchow fixed this.

🔎MODERN CELL THEORY

  • All living organisms are composed of cells and products of cells.
  • All cells arise from pre-existing cells (by division).
  • These two points together explain unity and continuity of life.
  • The phrase Omnis cellula-e cellula is Virchow’s named principle.

⚙️UNICELLULAR vs MULTICELLULAR (WHY ‘CELL’ IS THE UNIT)

  • Unicellular: one cell performs nutrition, respiration, excretion, reproduction, etc.
  • Anything less than a complete cell cannot ensure independent living.
  • Multicellular: many cells show division of labour; organism still depends on cells.
  • Living organisms show cellular organisation; acellular entities like viruses are excluded from cell theory.

⚖️Unicellular vs Multicellular Organisms

TOPIC2
Plant vs animal cell boundaries, nucleus→chromosome→DNA, cytoplasm, and prokaryote vs eukaryote basics

CELL OVERVIEW AND TYPES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • A cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms.
  • Plant cells (e.g., onion peel) show a cell wall outside the plasma membrane; animal cells (e.g., cheek cell) lack cell wall.
  • A nucleus is a dense, membrane-bound structure in eukaryotes; it contains chromosomes made of DNA (genetic material).
  • Cytoplasm is the semi-fluid matrix filling the cell; it is the main arena of cellular activities.
  • Cells are broadly of two types: prokaryotic (no membrane-bound nucleus) and eukaryotic (membrane-bound nucleus + organelles).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧅Onion peel (plant): distinct cell wall outer boundary; membrane lies just inside.
  • 👄Cheek cell (animal): outer boundary is only plasma membrane (no wall).
  • 🧬Information hierarchy: nucleus → chromosomes → DNA.
  • 🫧Cytoplasm supports organelles and hosts most metabolic reactions.
  • 🏭Eukaryotes show compartmentalisation due to membrane-bound organelles.
  • ⚙️Ribosomes are non-membrane-bound and present in all cells.

🔎NUCLEUS–CHROMOSOME–DNA:

  • Nucleus is membrane-bound in eukaryotes; absent as a membrane-bound body in prokaryotes.
  • Chromosomes are present in the nucleus; they carry hereditary information.
  • DNA is the genetic material located in chromosomes.
  • Prokaryotic genetic material is not enclosed by a nuclear membrane (often called nucleoid).

⚙️CYTOPLASM + ORGANELLES: WHAT GOES WHERE?

  • Cytoplasm is present in both plant and animal cells; reactions here maintain the living state.
  • Eukaryotes contain membrane-bound organelles: ER, Golgi, lysosomes, mitochondria, vacuoles etc.
  • Prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles but do possess ribosomes for protein synthesis.
  • Ribosomes occur in cytoplasm; also inside mitochondria and chloroplasts; and on RER.

TOPIC3
Cells show wide size–shape variation; geometry often matches function

CELL SIZE AND SHAPE

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Cell size and shape vary greatly among organisms and even within one organism; often linked to function.
  • Size ranges from very small prokaryotes to very large single cells like some eggs.
  • Shape is influenced by the cell’s role, mechanical constraints, and surrounding environment.
  • Cells performing different activities show different geometries to optimise exchange, support, movement, or conduction.
  • In multicellular organisms, specialised cells display characteristic shapes (e.g., RBC, neuron, epithelium).

🎯Key Concepts

  • Typical sizes (NCERT): Mycoplasma ≈ 0.3 μm; bacteria typically in the micrometre range (often ≈ 1–5 μm); human RBC ≈ 7.0 μm diameter.
  • Largest isolated single cell: ostrich egg (a single cell visible to naked eye).
  • Longest cells: nerve cells (elongated with processes) for long-distance impulse conduction.
  • RBC is biconcave: higher surface area and flexibility for gas exchange and capillary flow.
  • Common shapes: disc-like, polygonal, columnar, cuboid, thread-like, irregular; not random—function-driven.

🔎WHY DO CELLS DIFFER IN SIZE?

  • Different cells perform different activities; size balances capacity with transport efficiency.
  • Smaller cells show faster exchange with surroundings due to short diffusion distances.
  • Very large cells face transport limits; functional large cells usually show adaptations that increase exchange area or internal transport efficiency.
  • Within one organism, different cell types (RBC, epithelial cells, neurons) show different characteristic sizes.

⚙️SHAPE–FUNCTION CORRELATION (HIGH-YIELD LINKS)

  • Biconcave RBC: high surface area, quick O2/CO2 exchange; flexible in capillaries.
  • Long neuron: elongated processes enable impulse conduction over long distances.
  • Columnar epithelium: tall cells suitable for absorption/secretion surfaces.
  • Polygonal/cuboid cells: efficient packing for lining and structural support in tissues.
  • Irregular/amoeboid cells (e.g., WBC): shape change helps movement and phagocytosis.

TOPIC4
Bacteria/cyanobacteria/PPLO: no true nucleus, simple internal plan, plasmids & inclusions

PROKARYOTIC CELL ORGANIZATION

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Prokaryotes are cells with no membrane-bound nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (ribosomes present).
  • Represented by bacteria, cyanobacteria (blue-green algae), and mycoplasma/PPLO.
  • Genetic material is naked DNA in cytoplasm; usually a single circular chromosome (nucleoid region).
  • Cell wall surrounds plasma membrane in most; Mycoplasma lacks cell wall (very small free-living cells).
  • Basic organisation is similar across shapes: envelope + cytoplasm + nucleoid + ribosomes + inclusions; plasmids often present.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧬Nucleoid: naked, usually single circular DNA; no nuclear envelope.
  • ⭕Plasmids: extra-chromosomal circular DNA; may carry antibiotic resistance.
  • 🧫Bacterial shapes: bacillus (rod), coccus (sphere), vibrio (comma), spirillum (spiral).
  • 🧱Cell wall present in most bacteria; absent in Mycoplasma (PPLO).
  • 🌀Mesosome: plasma membrane infoldings (vesicles/tubules/lamellae); roles in wall formation, DNA replication/segregation, respiration, secretion; increase surface area and enzymes.
  • 📦Inclusion bodies: non-membranous storage granules; gas vacuoles in some photosynthetic bacteria.

🔎BACTERIAL TYPES & SHAPES (HIGH-YIELD LIST)

  • Bacteria: typical prokaryotes; size commonly a few μm; rapid multiplication.
  • Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae): prokaryotes; may have pigment-containing membranous extensions called chromatophores.
  • Mycoplasma / PPLO: very small free-living cells; no cell wall.
  • Shapes: bacillus (rod), coccus (spherical), vibrio (comma), spirillum (spiral).

⚙️INTERNAL ORGANISATION: MESOSOME, INCLUSIONS, PLASMIDS

  • Mesosome: plasma membrane infoldings; vesicles/tubules/lamellae.
  • Functions (asked in exams): cell wall formation, DNA replication & segregation, respiration, secretion; ↑surface area & enzymes.
  • Inclusion bodies: non-membrane-bound reserves; examples: phosphate, glycogen, cyanophycean granules.
  • Gas vacuoles: in blue-green and purple/green photosynthetic bacteria; aid buoyancy.
  • Plasmids: small circular DNA; confer special traits (e.g., antibiotic resistance); extra-chromosomal DNA.

⚖️PROKARYOTES vs EUKARYOTES (CORE DIFFERENCES)

TOPIC5
Three-layered protective unit: glycocalyx + cell wall + plasma membrane; basis of Gram staining; membrane infoldings/extensions (mesosomes, chromatophores)

PROKARYOTIC CELL ENVELOPE

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Prokaryotic cell envelope is a tightly bound, chemically complex three-layered protective covering of bacteria.
  • Layers (outer → inner): glycocalyx, cell wall, plasma membrane.
  • All layers act together as a single protective unit, though each has a distinct role.
  • Cell wall lies outside plasma membrane in most prokaryotes; Mycoplasma lacks cell wall.
  • Differences in cell envelope underlie Gram-positive vs Gram-negative staining response.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧴Glycocalyx: outermost layer; varies in thickness/composition; forms slime layer or capsule.
  • 🧱Cell wall: gives shape and structural support; helps prevent osmotic lysis (bursting).
  • 🚪Plasma membrane: selectively permeable; interacts with outside; similar to eukaryotic membrane.
  • 🧪Gram stain basis: envelope differences; Gram-positive retains crystal violet (purple), Gram-negative does not retain crystal violet and appears pink/red after counterstain.
  • 🌀Mesosome: plasma-membrane infoldings as vesicles/tubules/lamellae; multiple functions.
  • 🎨Chromatophores (cyanobacteria): membranous extensions with pigments.

🔎GLYCOCALYX: SLIME LAYER vs CAPSULE

  • Glycocalyx is the outermost envelope layer; varies in thickness and composition.
  • Slime layer: loose sheath; less rigid outer covering.
  • Capsule: thick and tough; more distinct and firm protection.
  • Exam cue: a thick, well-defined outer coat is described as a capsule (capsulated bacteria).

⚙️MEMBRANE MODIFICATIONS: MESOSOMES & CHROMATOPHORES

  • Mesosome = infolding of plasma membrane into cytoplasm; forms vesicles, tubules, lamellae.
  • Functions: cell wall formation, DNA replication and distribution, respiration, secretion.
  • Also increases surface area and enzymatic content of plasma membrane.
  • Chromatophores (cyanobacteria): membranous extensions containing pigments.
  • Do not confuse chromatophores with chloroplasts (chloroplasts are eukaryotic organelles).

⚖️Gram-positive vs Gram-negative (as per Gram stain response)

TOPIC6
Flagella for motility; pili & fimbriae for attachment/other surface roles (cell-envelope extensions)

PROKARYOTIC SURFACE STRUCTURES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Prokaryotic surface structures are external appendages (often from the cell envelope) used for movement and/or attachment and other surface functions.
  • Flagella are thin, filamentous extensions in motile bacteria; they act as propellers.
  • A bacterial flagellum has three parts: filament, hook, basal body.
  • Pili and fimbriae are non-motile surface structures (not organs of motility).
  • Pili are elongated, tubular protein structures; typically fewer and longer than fimbriae; some pili (sex pili) help in conjugation.
  • Fimbriae are small, bristle-like fibres that help adhesion to surfaces (rocks, host tissues).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧭Motility in bacteria is mainly due to flagella, not pili/fimbriae.
  • 🧩Flagellum: filament (outside) + hook (joint) + basal body (anchor in envelope).
  • 🧷Fimbriae increase grip: attachment to rocks in streams and host tissues.
  • 🧵Pili: elongated tubular protein appendages; some are involved in conjugation and some in surface attachment.
  • 🔢Flagella show variation in number and arrangement on the bacterial surface.

🔎FLAGELLA: PARTS & THEIR ROLES

  • Filament: longest, external; extends from cell surface to outside.
  • Hook: short curved connector; links filament to basal body (acts like a universal joint).
  • Basal body: anchoring region embedded in the cell envelope; fixes the flagellum.
  • Flagella are thin, filamentous extensions from the cell wall/envelope in motile bacteria.

⚙️NUMBER & ARRANGEMENT OF FLAGELLA (EXAM-USEFUL TERMS)

  • Monotrichous: single flagellum at one pole.
  • Lophotrichous: tuft of flagella at one pole.
  • Amphitrichous: flagella at both poles.
  • Peritrichous: flagella distributed all around the cell surface.
  • Questions often ask to name arrangement from a diagram or description.

TOPIC7
70S (50S+30S) ribosomes make proteins; inclusions store reserves without membranes

PROKARYOTIC RIBOSOMES & INCLUSION BODIES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Prokaryotic ribosomes are non-membrane-bound 70S particles, often associated with the plasma membrane, that carry out protein synthesis.
  • A 70S ribosome has two subunits: 50S (large) + 30S (small).
  • Typical size of a prokaryotic ribosome is about 15 nm × 20 nm.
  • Polysomes (polyribosomes) are many ribosomes attached to a single mRNA, forming a translation chain.
  • Inclusion bodies are non-membrane-bound cytoplasmic storage structures for reserve materials in prokaryotes, lying free in cytoplasm.
  • Gas vacuoles occur in blue-green and purple/green photosynthetic bacteria and help in buoyancy regulation.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 170S is the whole ribosome; subunits are 50S + 30S (S = Svedberg sedimentation unit).
  • 2Ribosomes bind mRNA and translate codons into a polypeptide chain (translation = protein synthesis).
  • 3In a polysome, one mRNA is read by many ribosomes simultaneously → rapid protein output.
  • 4In prokaryotes, ribosomes are commonly associated with the plasma membrane (no RER present).
  • 5Inclusions lie free in cytoplasm and store reserves: phosphate, glycogen, cyanophycean granules.
  • 6Gas vacuoles are non-membrane-bound structures (made of gas vesicles) typical of photosynthetic bacteria for floating.

🔎70S RIBOSOME: STRUCTURE & FUNCTION

  • Prokaryotic ribosomes are associated with the plasma membrane; no rough ER in prokaryotes.
  • Complete ribosome is 70S, built from 50S (large) and 30S (small) subunits.
  • Size is about 15 nm × 20 nm; visible as dense particles under electron microscope.
  • Core role: translation—decoding mRNA to synthesize polypeptides (proteins).
  • Exam note: S is a sedimentation coefficient; 70S ≠ 50S + 30S by arithmetic.

⚙️POLYSOMES & INCLUSION BODIES (NON-MEMBRANE-BOUND)

  • Polysome/polyribosome: several ribosomes attached to single mRNA translating simultaneously.
  • Advantage: fast, efficient protein synthesis; many copies of protein from one mRNA.
  • Inclusion bodies: reserve material stored as granules, lying free in cytoplasm (no membrane).
  • Examples: phosphate granules, cyanophycean granules, glycogen granules.
  • Gas vacuoles: present in blue-green and purple/green photosynthetic bacteria; aid buoyancy.

TOPIC8
Organised nucleus + membrane-bound organelles; key plant–animal distinctions

EUKARYOTIC CELLS: PLANT VS ANIMAL

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Eukaryotic cells have an organised nucleus enclosed by a nuclear envelope.
  • Found in protists, plants, animals, fungi.
  • Show compartmentalisation due to many membrane-bound organelles.
  • Genetic material is organised into chromosomes (DNA + associated proteins) inside the nucleus.
  • Have complex cytoskeletal and (often) locomotory structures supporting shape, division, movement.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧫Cytoplasm is divided into specialised compartments by membrane-bound organelles.
  • 🧠Nucleus: nuclear envelope separates transcriptional control from cytoplasmic reactions.
  • 🧬Chromosomes package DNA; ensure accurate inheritance during cell division.
  • 🧱Plant cells: cell wall + plastids + large central vacuole (typical).
  • 🎯Animal cells: centrioles (in centrosome) commonly present; no wall or plastids.
  • 🧵Cytoskeleton (microtubules, microfilaments, etc.) supports shape, transport, division.

🔎EUKARYOTIC CELL HALLMARKS

  • Nuclear envelope present; separates nucleoplasm from cytoplasm.
  • Chromosomes in nucleus carry hereditary information (DNA).
  • Membrane-bound organelles enable specialised micro-environments for reactions.
  • Compartmentalisation increases efficiency and regulation of metabolism.
  • Cytoskeleton aids cell shape, intracellular transport, and cell division.

⚙️PLANT–ANIMAL DIFFERENCES

  • Plant cell: cell wall outside plasma membrane; gives rigidity and protection.
  • Plant cell: plastids (e.g., chloroplasts) present; absent in animals.
  • Plant cell: large central vacuole with tonoplast; typical mature plant cell feature.
  • Animal cell: centrosome with centrioles helps in cell division; absent in almost all plants.
  • Both: nucleus, mitochondria, ER, Golgi, ribosomes present (eukaryotic basics).

TOPIC9
How substances cross the selectively permeable cell membrane

PLASMA MEMBRANE TRANSPORT

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Plasma membrane transport is the movement of substances across the selectively permeable plasma membrane to maintain cellular composition and homeostasis.
  • Transport depends mainly on concentration gradient, membrane permeability, and presence of specific membrane proteins (carriers/channels).
  • Passive transport moves substances down their gradient and does not require ATP.
  • Active transport moves substances against their gradient and is ATP-dependent (primary active transport).
  • Polar/charged solutes generally require membrane proteins because the lipid bilayer core is nonpolar (hydrophobic).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧱Lipid bilayer: hydrophilic heads face water; hydrophobic tails face inward.
  • 🔎Selectively permeable: allows some molecules, restricts others.
  • 🫧Simple diffusion: small molecules move high → low concentration directly through membrane (no protein).
  • 💧Osmosis: movement of water across a semipermeable membrane from higher water potential (lower solute) to lower water potential (higher solute).
  • 🚚Facilitated diffusion: substances move down gradient via membrane proteins (channels or carriers); no ATP.
  • 🔋Active transport: movement against gradient using energy; e.g., Na+/K+ pump.

🔎PASSIVE TRANSPORT (NO ATP)

  • Simple diffusion: small molecules move down concentration gradient (high → low) without proteins.
  • Osmosis: water moves from higher water potential (lower solute) to lower water potential (higher solute) across a semipermeable membrane.
  • Facilitated diffusion: substances move down gradient using channels or carriers; no ATP.
  • Reason for proteins: ions/polar molecules cannot cross the hydrophobic lipid core efficiently.

⚙️ACTIVE TRANSPORT (USES ENERGY)

  • Moves substances against their electrochemical/concentration gradient (low → high).
  • Primary active transport uses ATP; transport proteins function as pumps (ATPases).
  • Example: Na+/K+ pump transports ions against their gradients.
  • Do not call carrier-mediated movement "active" unless it is against gradient and energy-dependent.

⚖️PASSIVE TRANSPORT vs ACTIVE TRANSPORT

TOPIC10
Non-living rigid wall: composition, layers (primary/secondary), middle lamella, and cytoplasmic links

CELL WALL AND PLASMODESMATA

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Cell wall is a non-living, rigid outer covering outside the plasma membrane in plants, fungi, and many protists (e.g., algae).
  • It gives shape and mechanical protection and helps resist injury and infection.
  • It enables cell-to-cell interaction and acts as a barrier to undesirable macromolecules.
  • Plant walls have primary wall, may develop secondary wall, and cells are cemented by middle lamella.
  • Plasmodesmata are channels through wall + middle lamella that connect cytoplasm of adjacent plant cells.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩Wall matrix: cellulose microfibrils embedded in hemicellulose + pectins + proteins (typical land plants).
  • 🌊Primary wall is thin and flexible; it is capable of growth in young cells.
  • 🪵Secondary wall is laid on inner side (towards plasma membrane) as cell matures; growth reduces.
  • 🧷Middle lamella is mainly calcium pectate; it cements neighbouring plant cells.
  • 🔗Plasmodesmata form a symplastic pathway for intercellular transport/signalling.
  • 🛡️Cell wall limits entry of large molecules/pathogens; selective plasma membrane still controls uptake.

🔎COMPOSITION: WHO HAS WHAT?

  • Plants (most): cellulose + hemicellulose + pectins + proteins.
  • Algae: cellulose + galactans + mannans; may have mineral deposits like CaCO3.
  • Fungi: cell wall present; provides rigidity and protection (composition differs from plants).
  • Cell wall is non-living; plasma membrane below it remains the selectively permeable boundary.

⚙️LAYERS AND CONNECTIONS:

  • Primary wall: in young cells; thin, flexible; permits cell enlargement during growth.
  • Secondary wall: deposited on inner side after growth; thicker and stronger; growth ability declines.
  • Middle lamella: mainly calcium pectate; acts as cement between adjacent cells.
  • Plasmodesmata: channels crossing wall + middle lamella; allow intercellular transport/signalling.
  • Wall is a barrier to undesirable macromolecules; plasmodesmata provide controlled cell-to-cell continuity.

TOPIC11
ER → Golgi → lysosome/vacuole/secretion: coordinated trafficking; mitochondria/chloroplast/peroxisomes are generally not included

ENDOMEMBRANE SYSTEM & KEY ORGANELLES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Endomembrane system is a functionally connected group of membranes: ER, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes and vacuoles, coordinating synthesis, modification, packaging and intracellular digestion/storage.
  • Its members are structurally distinct but linked by vesicle transport and membrane flow.
  • It is defined mainly by functional coordination (especially ER↔Golgi trafficking), not just by being membrane-bound.
  • Generally not included (NCERT): mitochondria, chloroplasts and microbodies/peroxisomes, because they are functionally not part of the ER–Golgi trafficking system.
  • Exam approach: connect structure → function (e.g., RER ribosomes → secretory/membrane proteins).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧵RER: ribosomes on cytosolic surface; synthesises secretory, lysosomal and membrane proteins; continuous with outer nuclear membrane.
  • 🧴SER: no ribosomes; lipid synthesis; in animals, steroid hormones synthesised in SER; also important in detoxification.
  • 📦Golgi: stacked cisternae; cis (forming) receives, trans (maturing) releases; major sorting station.
  • 🧬Golgi modifies ER-made proteins/lipids; forms glycoproteins and glycolipids.
  • 🧪Lysosomes: Golgi-derived vesicles with acidic hydrolases; digest biomolecules during intracellular digestion/autophagy.
  • 💧Vacuole: membrane-bound space; plant central vacuole may occupy up to 90% of cell volume; membrane is tonoplast.

🔎ER (ENDOPLASMIC RETICULUM): STRUCTURE → FUNCTION

  • Membranous network of tubules/cisternae in cytoplasm, enclosing an internal lumen; cytosolic side faces cytoplasm.
  • RER: ribosome-studded; abundant in cells secreting proteins (e.g., pancreatic acinar cells).
  • RER is continuous with outer nuclear membrane; helps route newly synthesised proteins into the endomembrane pathway.
  • SER: synthesises lipids; in animals, major site for steroid hormone synthesis; also aids detoxification.

⚙️GOLGI–LYSOSOME–VACUOLE: SORTING, DIGESTION, STORAGE

  • Golgi has stacked cisternae; receives ER vesicles at cis face and dispatches from trans face.
  • Golgi modifies proteins and lipids; forms glycoproteins and glycolipids for membranes and secretion.
  • Lysosomes are Golgi-packaged vesicles containing hydrolases active at acidic pH; digest proteins, lipids, carbohydrates and nucleic acids.
  • Vacuole membrane is tonoplast; central vacuole maintains solute balance, storage and turgor in plants.
  • Protists: contractile vacuole for osmoregulation/excretion; food vacuole forms after engulfment.

Mitochondria, PLASTIDS AND Ribosomes

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts are double-membrane energy-related organelles: mitochondria form ATP during aerobic respiration; chloroplasts convert light energy into chemical energy during photosynthesis.
  • Mitochondria: double membrane; inner membrane forms cristae; inner compartment is matrix.
  • Plastids (plants and some protists such as euglenoids): chloroplast (photosynthesis), chromoplast (colour), leucoplast (storage).
  • Ribosomes: non-membranous rRNA-protein particles; site of protein synthesis; 70S in prokaryotes and inside mitochondria/chloroplasts; 80S in eukaryotic cytosol.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔋Mitochondria: outer membrane + inner membrane with cristae; matrix has circular DNA + 70S ribosomes (semi-autonomous).
  • 🌿Chloroplast: double envelope; stroma + thylakoids; grana stacks; lumen inside thylakoid.
  • 🎨Plastid types: chloroplast (chlorophyll), chromoplast (carotenoids), leucoplast (storage).
  • 🧫Leucoplasts: amyloplast (starch), elaioplast (oils/fats), aleuroplast (proteins).
  • ⚙️Ribosomes: 80S = 60S+40S (euk cytosol); 70S = 50S+30S (prok + mitochondria + chloroplast).
  • 🧬Nucleus: double nuclear envelope with pores; nucleolus makes rRNA and assembles ribosomal subunits; chromatin forms chromosomes.

🔎MITOCHONDRIA: STRUCTURE

  • Double membrane: outer boundary; inner membrane forms cristae projecting into matrix.
  • Two aqueous spaces: intermembrane space and matrix (inner compartment).
  • Matrix contains circular DNA, RNAs and 70S ribosomes → semi-autonomous organelle.
  • Function: major site of aerobic respiration and ATP synthesis; number increases with cell activity.
  • Mitochondria divide by fission.

⚙️PLASTIDS and RIBOSOMES

  • Plastids: chloroplast (photosynthesis), chromoplast (carotenoid colours), leucoplast (storage).
  • Leucoplast types: amyloplast starch; elaioplast oils/fats; aleuroplast proteins.
  • Chloroplast: stroma + thylakoids; grana are thylakoid stacks; stroma lamellae connect grana; lumen inside thylakoid.
  • Ribosomes: non-membranous; 80S in eukaryotic cytosol; 70S in prokaryotes, mitochondria, chloroplasts.

TOPIC13

CYTOSKELETON, CILIA AND FLAGELLA

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Cytoskeleton: an elaborate network of filamentous proteinaceous structures in the cytoplasm — microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments.
  • Functions of the cytoskeleton: mechanical support, motility, and maintenance of cell shape.
  • Cilia and flagella are hair-like outgrowths of the cell membrane involved in movement.
  • Cilia: small structures that work like oars, causing movement of the cell or the surrounding fluid.
  • Flagella: comparatively longer, responsible for cell movement; prokaryotic flagella are structurally different from eukaryotic flagella.
  • Both are covered by the plasma membrane and emerge from a centriole-like basal body.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧱Microtubules: hollow tubulin-protein filaments; support, spindle fibres, axonemes.
  • 🧬Microfilaments: actin-based filaments; support cell shape and aid motility.
  • 🪢Intermediate filaments: rope-like proteinaceous fibres; give mechanical strength.
  • 🔬Axoneme: core of cilium/flagellum; microtubules run parallel to the long axis.
  • ⭕9+2 array: nine peripheral doublets of microtubules + one central pair.
  • ⚓Both cilium and flagellum arise from a basal body (centriole-like structure).

🔎CYTOSKELETON: COMPONENTS & ROLE

  • An elaborate network of filamentous proteinaceous structures present in the cytoplasm.
  • Composed of three fibre types: microtubules, microfilaments, intermediate filaments.
  • Function: mechanical support to the cell.
  • Function: motility — cell movement and internal transport.
  • Function: maintenance of the shape of the cell.

⚙️AXONEME: STRUCTURE IN DETAIL

  • Cilium/flagellum core = axoneme; covered externally by the plasma membrane.
  • Nine doublets of radially arranged peripheral microtubules + a central pair = 9+2 array.
  • Central tubules are joined by bridges and enclosed by a central sheath.
  • Central sheath connects to each peripheral doublet by a radial spoke — nine radial spokes in all.
  • Peripheral doublets are further interconnected by linkers; both structures emerge from a basal body.

TOPIC14
Two perpendicular centrioles with a cartwheel (triplet) plan; give rise to basal bodies and the mitotic spindle in animal cells

CENTROSOME AND CENTRIOLES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Centrosome: an organelle usually containing two cylindrical structures called centrioles.
  • Centrioles are surrounded by amorphous pericentriolar material.
  • The two centrioles of a centrosome lie perpendicular to each other.
  • Each centriole shows a cartwheel-like organisation in cross-section.
  • Centrioles are common in animal cells; absent in almost all plant cells.
  • Function: form the basal body of cilia/flagella and give rise to the spindle fibres/spindle apparatus during animal-cell division.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔗Centrosome usually contains two centrioles, positioned perpendicular to each other.
  • ☁️Pericentriolar material: amorphous material surrounding the centrioles.
  • 🎡Cartwheel organisation: nine evenly spaced peripheral fibrils of tubulin protein.
  • 🧩Each peripheral fibril is a triplet; adjacent triplets are linked to one another.
  • 🎯Hub: proteinaceous centre of the proximal region, joined to triplets by radial spokes (protein).
  • 🧬Centrioles form the basal body of cilia/flagella and the spindle apparatus in animal-cell division.

🔎CENTRIOLE STRUCTURE: THE CARTWHEEL PLAN

  • Nine evenly spaced peripheral fibrils of tubulin protein arranged like a cartwheel.
  • Each peripheral fibril is a triplet (three linked microtubule units).
  • Adjacent triplets are linked to each other around the rim.
  • Central part of the proximal region is proteinaceous, called the hub.
  • Hub is connected to the peripheral triplets by radial spokes made of protein.

⚙️FUNCTIONS OF THE CENTRIOLE

  • Gives rise to the basal body of cilia and flagella.
  • In animal cells, forms the spindle fibres that build the spindle apparatus during cell division.
  • Centrosome usually lies near the nucleus, organising the microtubule network of the cell.
  • Absent in almost all plant cells, which typically lack centrioles.

TOPIC15
Robert Brown (1831) described it; a double, pore-studded envelope gates all traffic between nucleus and cytoplasm

NUCLEUS: ENVELOPE, NUCLEOPLASM & NUCLEOLUS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Nucleus was first described as a cell organelle by Robert Brown (1831).
  • The stainable nuclear material was later named chromatin by Flemming.
  • Interphase nucleus (non-dividing) shows: chromatin, nuclear matrix, and one or more nucleoli.
  • Nuclear envelope: two parallel membranes with a perinuclear space (10–50 nm) between them.
  • Outer membrane is usually continuous with the ER and bears ribosomes on its surface.
  • Nuclear pores, formed by fusion of the two membranes, allow bidirectional RNA/protein movement.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔬Robert Brown (1831): first described the nucleus.
  • 🎨Flemming: named the basic-dye-stained nuclear material chromatin.
  • 🧫Nuclear envelope = 2 parallel membranes + perinuclear space (10–50 nm) between them.
  • 🔌Outer membrane continuous with ER; carries ribosomes on its outer face.
  • 🚪Nuclear pores form where the two membranes fuse; gateway for RNA & protein — both ways.
  • ⚪Nucleolus: not membrane-bound; site of active rRNA synthesis; larger/more numerous in protein-active cells.

🔎NUCLEAR ENVELOPE & NUCLEAR PORES

  • Nuclear envelope = two parallel membranes with the perinuclear space (10–50 nm) between.
  • Forms a barrier between the material inside the nucleus and the cytoplasm.
  • Outer membrane usually continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum; bears ribosomes.
  • Envelope is interrupted at intervals by nuclear pores — formed by fusion of its two membranes.
  • Nuclear pores are passages for the bidirectional movement of RNA and protein molecules between nucleus and cytoplasm.

⚙️NUCLEOPLASM, NUCLEOLUS & VARIATIONS

  • Nuclear matrix (nucleoplasm) contains the nucleolus and chromatin.
  • Nucleolus is spherical, not membrane-bound, and continuous with the rest of the nucleoplasm.
  • It is the site of active ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis; larger/more numerous nucleoli occur in cells active in protein synthesis.
  • Normally one nucleus per cell, but variation in nuclear number is frequently observed.
  • Some mature cells lack a nucleus altogether — e.g., mammalian erythrocytes (RBCs) and sieve tube cells of vascular plants.
  • This raises a reflective NCERT question: would you still consider such enucleate mature cells to be fully "living"?

TOPIC16

CHROMATIN AND CHROMOSOME STRUCTURE

📖Definition & Introduction

  • The interphase nucleus has a loose, indistinct network of nucleoprotein fibres called chromatin.
  • During cell division, this chromatin condenses into structured chromosomes.
  • Chromatin = DNA + basic proteins called histones + some non-histone proteins + RNA.
  • A single human cell packages ~2 metres of DNA into 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
  • Every chromosome has a primary constriction, the centromere, flanked by disc-shaped kinetochores.
  • Based on centromere position, chromosomes are classed as metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric or telocentric.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧵Chromatin = DNA + histones + non-histone proteins + RNA (nucleoprotein fibres).
  • 📏~2 m of DNA is packaged into 46 (23 pairs) chromosomes in a human cell.
  • ⚓Centromere: primary constriction; holds the two sister chromatids of a chromosome together.
  • 💿Kinetochore: disc-shaped structure present on either side of the centromere.
  • 🔀Four types by centromere position: metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric, telocentric.
  • 🏷️Satellite: a non-staining secondary constriction giving the appearance of a small chromosome fragment.

🔎CHROMATIN: COMPOSITION

  • Loosely packed nucleoprotein fibres seen in the interphase nucleus.
  • Composition: DNA, histones (basic proteins), non-histone proteins, and RNA.
  • Condenses into visible, structured chromosomes only during cell division.
  • A human cell: ~2 metre long DNA thread distributed among 46 (23 pairs) chromosomes.
  • The detailed mechanism of DNA packaging into a chromosome is studied further in Class XII.

⚙️CHROMOSOME: CENTROMERE, KINETOCHORE & SATELLITE

  • Every chromosome (visible only in dividing cells) has a primary constriction — the centromere.
  • Kinetochores are disc-shaped structures present on the sides of the centromere.
  • The centromere holds the two chromatids of a chromosome together.
  • Some chromosomes show a non-staining secondary constriction at a constant location.
  • This secondary constriction gives the appearance of a small fragment called the satellite.

MICROBODIES & CHAPTER SUMMARY

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Microbodies: many membrane-bound, minute vesicles that contain various enzymes.
  • Present in both plant and animal cells (e.g., peroxisomes shown in a typical cell diagram).
  • Like mitochondria and plastids, microbodies are generally not grouped with the ER–Golgi endomembrane system.
  • This page closes the organelle survey of Chapter 8 — from cell theory to nucleus and chromosomes.
  • Big picture: the cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of every living organism.
  • All organisms are composed of cells and products of cells; all cells arise from pre-existing cells.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧪Microbodies are membrane-bound and carry a variety of enzymes.
  • 🌿Found in both plant and animal cells, e.g., as peroxisomes in cell diagrams.
  • 🚫Excluded from the endomembrane system (ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles), like mitochondria/plastids.
  • 🏗️Chapter arc: cell theory → prokaryotic plan → eukaryotic organelles → nucleus/chromosomes.
  • ⚙️Ribosomes are the one non-membrane-bound organelle common to prokaryotes and eukaryotes.
  • 🧠Nucleus controls heredity and cellular activity via chromatin/chromosomes.

🔎MICROBODIES

  • Many membrane-bound, minute vesicular structures present in the cytoplasm.
  • Contain a variety of enzymes for localised biochemical reactions.
  • Present in both plant and animal cells.
  • Distinct from the endomembrane system, similar to how mitochondria/plastids are generally excluded from it.
  • Seen alongside other organelles in a typical plant/animal cell diagram (e.g., labelled peroxisome).

⚙️CHAPTER 8 AT A GLANCE — ORGANELLE ROLES

  • Nucleus: heredity + control centre — chromatin/chromosomes carry genetic material.
  • Mitochondria: aerobic respiration, ATP production — the cell's "power house".
  • Plastids (plants): photosynthesis, pigmentation, storage — chloroplast/chromoplast/leucoplast.
  • ER + Golgi + lysosomes + vacuoles: the endomembrane system — synthesis → packaging → digestion/storage.
  • Ribosomes: the one non-membrane-bound organelle carrying out protein synthesis in every cell type.
  • Cytoskeleton + centrioles + cilia/flagella: shape, support, motility, and division machinery.
