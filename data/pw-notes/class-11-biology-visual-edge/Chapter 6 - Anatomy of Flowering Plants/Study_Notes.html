Anatomy of Flowering Plants: Introduction

TOPIC1
Internal structure + functional organisation; organisation levels; diagnostic monocot–dicot differences and functional links

PLANT ANATOMY BASICS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Plant anatomy is the study of internal structure and functional organisation of plants.
  • Level of organisation: cells → tissues → organs (e.g., root, stem, leaf, flower).
  • Different organs show different internal structures because functions differ.
  • In angiosperms, monocots and dicots show diagnostic anatomical differences.
  • Internal structures also show functional specialisations for different environments (e.g., water-loss control, support).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧫Cells are the basic units; tissues are groups of similar cells with a shared function.
  • 🧩Three tissue systems: epidermal (covering), ground (bulk), vascular (transport).
  • 🌬️Stomata regulate transpiration and gas exchange; distribution differs in dicot vs monocot leaves.
  • 🚰Cuticle reduces water loss; generally absent in roots (facilitates absorption).
  • 🪵Vascular bundles: open (cambium present → secondary growth) vs closed (no cambium).
  • 🌾Monocot leaf bulliform cells help leaf rolling during water stress (anti-transpiration adaptation).

🔎LEVELS OF ORGANISATION

  • Cell: basic living unit; specialised for roles (absorption, conduction, support).
  • Tissue: group of similar cells performing a common function.
  • Organ: tissues arranged to perform major functions (e.g., root absorption, stem conduction, leaf photosynthesis).
  • Same tissue system shows different specialisations in different organs (e.g., epidermis with cuticle in shoot).

⚙️MONOCOT–DICOT DIFFERENCES

  • Cuticle + trichomes (often on shoots) reduce transpiration; helpful in dry/hot conditions.
  • Stomata distribution: dicot (dorsiventral) usually more on abaxial; monocot (isobilateral) on both sides.
  • Mesophyll: dicot leaf has palisade (upper) + spongy (lower); monocot leaf usually undifferentiated.
  • Vascular bundles: monocot stem typically scattered + closed (no cambium) → little secondary growth.
  • Monocot root: many xylem bundles (polyarch) + large pith; no typical secondary growth.

TOPIC2
Plant body tissues grouped by structure + location: epidermal, ground, vascular

THREE TISSUE SYSTEMS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • A tissue system is a group of tissues organised by location and function in the plant body.
  • Plant tissues vary with location; hence structure and function are location dependent.
  • Based on structure and location, flowering plants show three tissue systems: epidermal, ground (fundamental), vascular (conducting).
  • Epidermal system forms the outer covering for protection and exchange adaptations.
  • Ground system forms the bulk of the plant body (all tissues except epidermis and vascular bundles).
  • Vascular system consists of xylem and phloem arranged as vascular bundles for transport.

🖼️Diagram

Three tissue systems based on location: outer covering, bulk, and conducting bundles.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩Classification basis: structure + location (not just function).
  • 🛡️Epidermal system: epidermis + stomata + epidermal appendages (root hairs, trichomes).
  • 🧱Ground system: mainly simple tissues—parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma.
  • 🚰Vascular system: complex tissues—xylem (water/minerals) and phloem (food).
  • 🧵Vascular bundle = xylem + phloem together; arrangement varies in root vs stem/leaf.
  • 🌱Bundles may be open (cambium present; dicot stem) or closed (no cambium; monocot stem).

🔎EPIDERMAL TISSUE SYSTEM (OUTERMOST)

  • Forms the outermost covering of the primary plant body.
  • Epidermis: usually single-layered, compact cells forming a continuous protective layer.
  • Cuticle (waxy) on aerial parts reduces water loss; absent in roots.
  • Stomatal apparatus = pore + guard cells + subsidiary cells; controls gaseous exchange and transpiration.
  • Epidermal appendages: root hairs (unicellular; absorption) and trichomes (usually multicellular; protection/secretory).

⚙️GROUND (FUNDAMENTAL) TISSUE SYSTEM

  • Ground tissue: all tissues except epidermis and vascular bundles; mainly simple tissues.
  • Main components: parenchyma (common), collenchyma (support in young parts), sclerenchyma (strength).
  • Parenchyma occurs in cortex, pericycle, pith, medullary rays of primary stems and roots.
  • In leaves, ground tissue = mesophyll (thin-walled, chloroplast-containing cells).

TOPIC3
Outermost protective tissue system with stomata for gaseous exchange and epidermal appendages

EPIDERMAL TISSUE SYSTEM

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Epidermal tissue system forms the outermost covering of the primary plant body; chiefly protection plus regulated exchange.
  • Usually a single layer of compactly arranged, elongated epidermal cells forming a continuous layer.
  • Epidermal cells are living, parenchymatous; cytoplasm forms a thin lining along the cell wall with a large vacuole.
  • Cuticle is a waxy layer on epidermis (mainly aerial parts) that reduces water loss; absent in roots.
  • Stomata in leaf epidermis regulate transpiration and gaseous exchange via controlled pore opening.
  • Epidermal appendages include root hairs (absorption) and trichomes (protection, reduced transpiration, sometimes secretion).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • DEFEEpidermis: compact, usually single-layered barrier against injury, pathogens and desiccation.
  • CUTICuticle (waxy): lowers cuticular water loss; thicker in xeric conditions; absent in roots.
  • STOMStomatal pore is the adjustable gateway for CO2 entry and water vapour exit.
  • GUARGuard cells: bean-shaped in most plants; dumb-bell shaped in grasses.
  • APPAStomatal apparatus = pore + guard cells + subsidiary cells (specialised neighbouring epidermal cells).
  • TURGGuard cells contain chloroplasts and regulate opening/closing by turgor changes.

🔎STOMATA & STOMATAL APPARATUS (HIGH-YIELD)

  • Stoma = aperture in epidermis bordered by two guard cells.
  • Guard cells: bean-shaped in most plants; dumb-bell shaped in grasses.
  • Guard cell wall: inner wall thick (towards pore), outer wall thin (away from pore).
  • Subsidiary cells: specialised neighbouring epidermal cells; support guard cell function.
  • Stomatal apparatus = pore + guard cells + subsidiary cells.

⚙️EPIDERMAL APPENDAGES: ROOT HAIRS vs TRICHOMES

  • Root hairs: unicellular epidermal outgrowths (epiblema); greatly increase absorptive surface.
  • Main role: absorption of water and minerals from soil solution.
  • Trichomes (shoot/stem): epidermal hairs, usually multicellular; may be branched/unbranched.
  • Roles of trichomes: reduce transpiration, reflect radiation, provide mechanical/chemical defence.
  • Some trichomes are secretory (e.g., oils/resins) depending on plant type.

TOPIC4
All tissues except epidermis and vascular bundles (xylem + phloem); mainly simple tissues and leaf mesophyll

GROUND TISSUE SYSTEM

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Ground tissue (fundamental tissue) = all tissues except epidermis and vascular bundles (xylem + phloem).
  • It forms the bulk of plant body and is mainly made of simple tissues: parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma.
  • In primary stems/roots, parenchyma of ground tissue is usually present in cortex (including endodermis), pericycle, pith and medullary rays.
  • In leaves, the ground tissue is mesophyll (between upper and lower epidermis).
  • Mesophyll cells are thin-walled and chloroplast-containing, hence chiefly photosynthetic.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩Identify by exclusion: ground tissue = everything inside epidermis except xylem+phloem bundles.
  • 🧫Core components: parenchyma (most common), collenchyma (support in young parts), sclerenchyma (strength).
  • 🍃Leaf ground tissue = mesophyll with chloroplasts; in dicot leaf: palisade + spongy parenchyma.
  • 🧱Cortex + pith are typical ground-tissue regions; monocot stem may show undifferentiated ground tissue.

🔎COMPONENTS & WHERE THEY OCCUR

  • Parenchyma: most widespread ground tissue; living, thin-walled; storage, photosynthesis, packing.
  • Collenchyma: living, unevenly thickened corners; support in young growing parts (e.g., hypodermis).
  • Sclerenchyma: dead at maturity, thick lignified walls; mechanical strength (fibres/sclereids).
  • Parenchyma is usually present in cortex (incl. endodermis), pericycle, pith, medullary rays (primary stems/roots).

⚙️LEAF MESOPHYLL AS GROUND TISSUE (HIGH-YIELD)

  • Mesophyll lies between upper and lower epidermis and contains chloroplasts → photosynthesis.
  • In dorsiventral (dicot) leaf: palisade (upper/adaxial, elongated) + spongy (lower/abaxial, air spaces).
  • In isobilateral (monocot) leaf: mesophyll often not differentiated into palisade and spongy.
  • Exam phrase: mesophyll = thin-walled chloroplast-containing cells.

TOPIC5
Xylem + phloem form vascular bundles; classified by cambium (open/closed) and arrangement (radial/conjoint)

VASCULAR TISSUE SYSTEM

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Vascular tissue system is the conducting system of plants made of xylem and phloem arranged as vascular bundles.
  • Xylem conducts water and minerals mainly from roots to aerial parts; it is a complex tissue.
  • Phloem conducts organic food (photosynthates) from source to sink; it is a complex tissue.
  • A vascular bundle is the structural unit where xylem and phloem occur together in a definite arrangement.
  • Presence/absence of cambium decides whether a bundle can form secondary xylem and secondary phloem (secondary growth).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧬Complex tissues: xylem and phloem each contain multiple cell types working together.
  • 🌿Open vascular bundle: cambium present between xylem and phloem → secondary growth possible.
  • 🌾Closed vascular bundle: cambium absent → no secondary xylem/phloem via cambium (typical monocot stem).
  • 🫚Radial bundle: xylem and phloem on different radii, alternating (typical roots).
  • 🍃Conjoint bundle: xylem and phloem on same radius (common stems/leaves); phloem external to xylem.

🔎OPEN vs CLOSED VASCULAR BUNDLES (CAMBIUM-BASED)

  • Cambium is a meristematic strip between xylem and phloem in a bundle.
  • Open bundle: cambium present → forms secondary xylem (inside) and secondary phloem (outside).
  • Typical location: dicot stem bundles are usually open; hence secondary growth is common.
  • Closed bundle: cambium absent → no secondary tissues via cambium; typical of monocot stems.

⚙️RADIAL vs CONJOINT (ARRANGEMENT-BASED)

  • Radial: xylem and phloem occur on different radii and alternate with each other; typical roots.
  • Conjoint: xylem and phloem lie on the same radius; common in stems and leaves.
  • In conjoint bundles, phloem is usually external (towards periphery) and xylem internal (towards pith).

⚖️Vascular Bundle Types: Quick Comparison

TOPIC6
Sunflower root T.S.: epiblema → cortex → endodermis (Casparian strips) → pericycle → stele (radial vascular bundles)

DICOT ROOT ANATOMY (T.S.)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Dicot root T.S. shows concentric tissue zones with a central stele and radial vascular arrangement.
  • Epiblema (root epidermis) is single-layered, lacks cuticle, and bears unicellular root hairs.
  • Cortex has several layers of thin-walled parenchyma with intercellular spaces; mainly storage and lateral transport.
  • Endodermis is the innermost cortex; barrel-shaped cells without intercellular spaces; has Casparian strips.
  • Pericycle lies just inside endodermis; initiates lateral roots and vascular cambium during secondary growth.
  • Pith is small/inconspicuous in dicot roots; tissues inside endodermis together form the stele.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧫Epiblema: absorption surface; root hairs increase surface area for water/mineral uptake.
  • 🧱Endodermis: selective barrier; suberin in Casparian strips blocks apoplastic flow.
  • 🧩Pericycle: lateral root origin is endogenous (from pericycle, not cortex/epiblema).
  • 🩸Radial bundles: xylem and phloem alternate on different radii; typical of roots.
  • 🌿Conjunctive tissue: parenchyma between xylem and phloem; later contributes to cambium ring formation.
  • 🎯Stele starts inside endodermis: pericycle + vascular tissues + pith.

🔎LAYER-WISE FEATURES (OUTSIDE → INSIDE)

  • Epiblema: single layer; no cuticle; many cells form unicellular root hairs.
  • Cortex: multilayered parenchyma; intercellular spaces; storage and water movement.
  • Endodermis: single layer, barrel-shaped; no intercellular spaces.
  • Casparian strips: suberin bands on radial + tangential walls; force symplastic entry to stele.
  • Pericycle: a few layers of thick-walled parenchyma; immediately inside endodermis.

⚙️STELE + VASCULAR ARRANGEMENT (HIGH-YIELD)

  • Stele = all tissues inside endodermis: pericycle + vascular tissues + pith.
  • Radial vascular bundles: xylem and phloem lie on different radii and alternate.
  • Usually 2–4 patches of xylem and phloem in dicot root (diarch to tetrarch common).
  • Conjunctive tissue (parenchyma) lies between xylem and phloem patches.
  • During secondary growth, cambium ring develops between xylem and phloem, aided by pericycle/conjunctive cells.

⚖️DICOT ROOT vs MONOCOT ROOT (T.S. QUICK CONTRAST)

TOPIC7
Root T.S. plan like dicot, but polyarch xylem, large pith, and no secondary growth

MONOCOT ROOT ANATOMY

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Monocotyledonous root anatomy is the internal structure of a monocot root in T.S. (mature zone).
  • Basic tissue sequence: epidermis (epiblema) → cortex → endodermis → pericycle → vascular tissues → pith.
  • Vascular bundles are radial: xylem and phloem lie on different radii, alternating.
  • Xylem is typically polyarch (usually > 6 xylem bundles/patches).
  • Pith is large and well developed in monocot roots.
  • Secondary growth is absent in monocot roots (no vascular cambium formation).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩Monocot root is similar to dicot root in having epidermis, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, and a stele containing vascular bundles + pith.
  • 🚰Polyarch condition: many xylem patches (commonly more than six) around the pith.
  • 🔁Radial arrangement: xylem and phloem alternate; typical diagnostic of roots.
  • 🧱Endodermis is single-layered; walls show Casparian strips (suberin) for selective entry.
  • 🌱Pericycle (part of the stele) gives rise to lateral roots; typically not to cambium for secondary growth in monocots.
  • 🛑No secondary growth: absence of cambium activity means no secondary xylem/phloem in monocot roots.

🔎T.S. FEATURES (OUTSIDE → INSIDE)

  • Epidermis/epiblema: single layer; bears root hairs; cuticle absent in roots.
  • Cortex: many layers of thin-walled parenchyma with intercellular spaces; storage and aeration.
  • Endodermis: compact barrel-shaped cells; Casparian strips on tangential and radial walls.
  • Pericycle: a few layers just inside endodermis (part of stele); initiates lateral roots.
  • Vascular region (stele): xylem and phloem in radial arrangement; xylem is polyarch.

⚙️WHY NO SECONDARY GROWTH IN MONOCOT ROOTS?

  • Secondary growth needs a vascular cambium ring between xylem and phloem.
  • Monocot roots typically do not form this cambium ring; hence no secondary xylem/phloem.
  • Therefore, root shows mainly primary tissues throughout life.

TOPIC8
Epidermis to endodermis: protective outer layers + supportive cortex ending in starch sheath

DICOT STEM ANATOMY (YOUNG STEM, T.S.)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • T.S. of a young dicot stem shows epidermis with cuticle/trichomes, followed by a 3-zoned cortex ending in endodermis (starch sheath).
  • Epidermis is usually single-layered, compact, protective; bears cuticle, trichomes and few stomata.
  • Cortex lies between epidermis and pericycle; it is multilayered and part of the ground tissue system.
  • Hypodermis (just below epidermis) is collenchymatous in young dicot stem, providing mechanical strength.
  • Middle cortex is parenchymatous with conspicuous intercellular spaces (aeration/storage).
  • Endodermis is the innermost cortex layer; rich in starch grains hence called starch sheath.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🛡️Epidermis: continuous protective layer; compact elongated parenchymatous cells.
  • 💧Cuticle: waxy layer on epidermis; reduces water loss from young stem surface.
  • 🧶Trichomes: usually multicellular epidermal hairs; reduce transpiration; may be secretory.
  • 🌬️Stomata on stem: few; enable gaseous exchange and transpiration regulation.
  • 🏗️Hypodermis in dicot stem: collenchyma; gives flexible strength to young parts.
  • 🫧Cortical parenchyma: thin-walled rounded cells; prominent intercellular spaces.

🔎EPIDERMIS: CUTICLE, TRICHOMES & STOMATA

  • Epidermis is usually single-layered, tightly packed, and protective.
  • Cuticle is a waxy outer covering on epidermis; limits water loss.
  • Trichomes on stem are epidermal hairs, usually multicellular; branched/unbranched; may be secretory.
  • Trichomes lower transpiration by trapping still air and reducing direct exposure.
  • Stomata may occur on young stem epidermis, but generally fewer than in leaves.

⚙️CORTEX ZONES (DICOT STEM) + STARCH SHEATH

  • Hypodermis: few layers of collenchyma just below epidermis; provides flexible support.
  • Middle cortex: rounded thin-walled parenchyma with conspicuous intercellular spaces.
  • Intercellular spaces help aeration and allow tissue expansion in young stem.
  • Endodermis (innermost cortex) is rich in starch grains → called starch sheath.
  • Exam pointer: stem endodermis is identified by starch (starch sheath).

TOPIC9
Diagnostic features: sclerenchymatous hypodermis, undifferentiated ground tissue, scattered closed bundles

MONOCOT STEM ANATOMY (T.S.)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Monocot stem T.S. shows numerous, scattered, conjoint and closed vascular bundles embedded in a large, undifferentiated ground tissue.
  • Hypodermis is typically sclerenchymatous and provides mechanical strength.
  • Ground tissue is mainly parenchymatous and not clearly divided into cortex, endodermis, pericycle and pith.
  • Each vascular bundle is surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath.
  • Vascular bundles lack cambium, so typical monocot stems do not show secondary growth via vascular cambium.
  • Phloem parenchyma is absent; water-containing cavities (protoxylem lacuna) may occur in vascular bundles.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 1Hypodermis: sclerenchyma below epidermis gives rigidity to monocot stem.
  • 2Ground tissue: large, parenchymatous, undifferentiated (no clear cortex/pith).
  • 3Vascular bundles: numerous and scattered (not in a ring).
  • 4Closed bundles: cambium absent → secondary tissues not formed from vascular cambium (typical).
  • 5Bundle sheath: sclerenchymatous ring around each bundle for support.
  • 6Water cavities: protoxylem lacuna may be present within vascular bundles.

🔎LAYER-WISE DIAGNOSTIC FEATURES (OUTSIDE → INSIDE)

  • Epidermis: outer protective single layer; cuticle may be present.
  • Hypodermis: few layers of sclerenchyma providing mechanical support.
  • Ground tissue: parenchymatous mass; no clear cortex/endodermis/pericycle/pith separation.
  • Vascular bundles: numerous and scattered; peripheral bundles smaller than central ones (general trend).

⚙️VASCULAR BUNDLE DETAILS (MONOCOT STEM)

  • Type: conjoint; phloem usually external to xylem.
  • Closed: cambium absent between phloem and xylem → typical absence of secondary growth via vascular cambium.
  • Bundle sheath: sclerenchymatous sheath surrounds each bundle.
  • Phloem parenchyma: absent (diagnostic).
  • Water cavity: protoxylem lacuna (water-containing cavity) may be present within the bundle.

TOPIC10
Leaf with distinct upper (adaxial) and lower (abaxial) surfaces: palisade + spongy mesophyll

DORSIVENTRAL (DICOT) LEAF ANATOMY

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Dorsiventral (dicot) leaf shows anatomically distinct adaxial (upper) and abaxial (lower) sides.
  • A V.S. through lamina shows three parts: epidermis, mesophyll, and vascular system (veins + midrib).
  • Both epidermises bear a conspicuous cuticle that reduces water loss.
  • Stomata are generally more numerous on abaxial epidermis; adaxial may have fewer or even none.
  • Mesophyll is chloroplast-rich and differentiated into palisade (upper) and spongy (lower) parenchyma.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⬆️Adaxial epidermis: upper surface; thick cuticle; stomata fewer or absent.
  • ⬇️Abaxial epidermis: lower surface; stomata generally more numerous.
  • 🧱Palisade parenchyma: adaxial; elongated cells, vertically arranged; major photosynthesis site.
  • 🫧Spongy parenchyma: below palisade; loosely packed with large intercellular spaces (air cavities).
  • 🧬Vascular bundles in veins/midrib; each surrounded by a bundle sheath (thick-walled sheath cells).

🔎 EPIDERMIS: ADAXIAL vs ABAXIAL

  • Both surfaces: single-layered epidermis with cuticle (water-loss prevention).
  • Adaxial (upper): stomata fewer; may be absent in many dicot leaves.
  • Abaxial (lower): stomata generally more numerous for efficient gas exchange.
  • Stoma includes guard cells (bean-shaped in most dicots) + pore + subsidiary cells (if present).

⚙️MESOPHYLL + VASCULAR SYSTEM (VEINS/MIDRIB)

  • Mesophyll lies between the two epidermises; chloroplast-rich; main photosynthetic tissue.
  • Palisade parenchyma: adaxial; elongated, closely packed; vertical arrangement.
  • Spongy parenchyma: towards abaxial; loosely arranged with large air cavities aiding gas diffusion.
  • Vascular bundles occur in veins and midrib; bigger vein usually has a bigger bundle.
  • Each vascular bundle is enclosed by a bundle sheath layer of thick-walled cells.

ISOBILATERAL LEAF ANATOMY (MONOCOT LEAF)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Isobilateral leaf is a monocot leaf in which adaxial and abaxial surfaces are anatomically similar.
  • Both epidermal surfaces usually bear stomata (amphistomatic condition).
  • Mesophyll is chlorenchymatous but not differentiated into palisade and spongy parenchyma.
  • Parallel venation produces many veins of similar thickness, so vascular bundle sizes are nearly uniform (except main veins).
  • In grasses, bulliform cells on adaxial epidermis help leaf rolling/unrolling to reduce water loss.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⬆️Adaxial epidermis = upper surface; in grasses it bears bulliform (motor) cells near veins.
  • ⬇️Abaxial epidermis = lower surface; similar to adaxial in isobilateral leaves.
  • 🫁Stomata on both surfaces help gas exchange; distribution is not strongly upper-vs-lower biased.
  • 🧩Mesophyll undifferentiated: no clear palisade layer or spongy layer as in dorsiventral leaves.
  • 🧵Parallel venation correlates with many similar veins → similar-sized vascular bundles.
  • 💧Bulliform cells become turgid/flaccid with water status, acting as a hydraulic hinge for rolling.

🔎BULLIFORM CELLS: LEAF ROLLING MECHANISM (GRASSES)

  • Location: large, thin-walled, colourless epidermal cells on adaxial surface, usually along veins.
  • Water available → bulliform cells absorb water → turgid → leaf opens/unrolls.
  • Water stress → bulliform cells lose water → flaccid → leaf rolls inward.
  • Inward rolling reduces exposed surface area → minimises transpiration and water loss.
