Biology

Cell Cycle

TOPIC1
How cells coordinate growth, DNA replication and division to form two genetically intact daughter cells

CELL CYCLE OVERVIEW

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Cell cycle = ordered sequence in which a cell duplicates genome, makes cell components, then divides into two daughter cells.
  • All organisms begin as a single cell; multicellular bodies arise by repeated growth + division.
  • Each division of one parental cell produces two daughter cells that can again grow and divide.
  • Key coordination: DNA replication + cell growth + chromosome/cytoplasm partitioning must be synchronized.
  • Goal of coordination: each progeny cell receives an intact genome (complete, correctly distributed DNA).
  • Chromosome replication and distribution are under genetic control (cell-cycle regulators and checkpoints).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🌱Need: growth, repair/replacement, and reproduction depend on increasing cell number.
  • 🧩Three coupled processes: genome duplication, cytoplasmic growth, and cell division.
  • ⏱️Growth is broadly continuous, but DNA synthesis occurs only in S phase.
  • 🛑Checkpoints prevent division if DNA is unreplicated or damaged, protecting genome integrity.
  • 🧫Time varies widely: many human cultured cells ~24 h; yeast can be ~90 min.

🔎WHY CELL DIVISION IS NEEDED

  • Single cell → multicellular body by repeated rounds of growth and division.
  • In unicellular organisms, division itself is reproduction (new individual).
  • In multicellular organisms, division increases cell number for growth and restores tissue via repair.
  • Maintains functional cell size: growth increases cytoplasm; division helps restore nucleus:cytoplasm balance.

⚙️COORDINATION UNDER GENETIC CONTROL

  • Replication must occur once per cycle before chromosome segregation.
  • If DNA is not fully replicated, entry into division is blocked by checkpoints.
  • Distribution of replicated chromosomes ensures each daughter cell receives a complete genome.
  • Key exam line: Interphase is not "rest"; it is a highly active preparation phase.

⭐Important Points

  • Cell division must be coordinated with DNA replication and cell growth to preserve genome integrity.
  • DNA synthesis occurs only in S phase; growth is broadly continuous across interphase.
  • Events of chromosome replication and distribution are under genetic control (regulators + checkpoints).
  • Core outcome: two daughter cells can re-enter growth and division, forming a cell population from one original cell.

TOPIC2
Longest, most active part of cell cycle: G1 → S → G2; plus G0 quiescence

INTERPHASE AND G0

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Interphase is the period between two successive M phases when cell grows and prepares for division.
  • Interphase occupies >95% of a typical 24 h human cell cycle; M phase is ~1 h.
  • Interphase is not a true resting stage; it is metabolically active with growth and biosynthesis.
  • Interphase has three subphases: G1 (growth, no DNA replication), S (DNA replication), G2 (mitotic preparation).
  • G0 is a quiescent state entered from G1: cells remain metabolically active but do not proliferate.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⏳Interphase = bulk of cycle time; major biosynthesis happens here, not during mitosis.
  • 🌱G1: growth + normal metabolism; no DNA replication.
  • 🧬S phase: only stage of DNA synthesis; DNA content doubles (2C → 4C) but chromosome number stays 2n.
  • 🧰G2: proteins for mitosis synthesized; cell growth continues.
  • 🛑G0: non-proliferative but metabolically active; may re-enter cycle if needed.

🔎INTERPHASE: WHAT HAPPENS IN EACH SUBPHASE

  • G1 (Gap 1): interval after mitosis till start of DNA replication; cell is metabolically active.
  • G1: major growth; organelle duplication mainly here; DNA replication absent.
  • S (Synthesis): DNA replication in nucleus; in animal cells, centriole duplicates in cytoplasm.
  • S phase: DNA amount doubles 2C → 4C; chromosome number stays same (2n remains 2n).
  • G2 (Gap 2): proteins and factors made for mitosis; growth continues; final preparation for M.

⚙️G0 (QUIESCENT) + EXAM TRAPS

  • G0 is entered from G1 when a cell stops dividing; it remains metabolically active.
  • Many cells can re-enter the cycle from G0 if called upon (e.g., to replace lost cells).
  • Example of largely non-dividing cells: many adult heart cells do not appear to divide.
  • Trap 1: calling interphase a resting phase (wrong); it is highly active and biosynthetic.
  • Trap 2: claiming DNA replication occurs in G1/G2 (wrong); replication is restricted to S phase.

  • Interphase = G1 + S + G2; growth is broadly continuous across interphase.
  • Only S phase has DNA replication; DNA content 2C → 4C; chromosome number unchanged (2n).

TOPIC3
Track chromosome number (N) vs DNA amount (C) through G1 → S → G2 → M, especially anaphase counting

DNA CONTENT: N Vs C (CELL CYCLE)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • N = chromosome number per cell (count centromeres); C = DNA content relative to haploid genome.
  • In a diploid somatic cell: G1 has 2N, 2C.
  • During S phase, DNA replicates: C doubles (2C → 4C) but N remains 2N.
  • G2 has 2N, 4C (each chromosome has two sister chromatids).
  • At anaphase, sister chromatids separate; each chromatid is counted as an independent chromosome in that cell.
  • After cytokinesis, each daughter cell returns to 2N, 2C.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔢N counts chromosomes (centromeres); sister chromatids together = 1 chromosome until anaphase.
  • 📈C measures DNA amount; only S phase makes 2C → 4C.
  • 🧷In G2/metaphase: 2N chromosomes, but each is duplicated (two chromatids) so DNA is 4C.
  • ✂️Anaphase trap: chromatids separate and are counted as chromosomes → chromosome count per cell doubles transiently.
  • ✅Mitosis is equational: final chromosome number in each daughter cell equals parent (2N → 2N).

🔎HOW TO COUNT N CORRECTLY (EXAM RULE)

  • Count chromosomes by centromeres; one centromere = one chromosome.
  • Before anaphase, sister chromatids share one centromere → counted as one chromosome.
  • At anaphase, centromere splits; each chromatid gets its own centromere → becomes a chromosome.
  • So, a mitotic cell at anaphase has double the chromosome count compared to G1/G2.

⚙️PHASE-WISE SNAPSHOT (DIPLOID MITOTIC CYCLE)

  • G1: unreplicated chromosomes; 2N, 2C.
  • S: replication forms sister chromatids; 2N, 2C → 4C.
  • G2: replicated but joined chromatids; 2N, 4C.
  • M (prophase→metaphase): still 2N, 4C.
  • Anaphase: chromatids separate; in one cell 4N, 4C.

  • In mitosis, N is conserved in daughter cells (equational division).
  • At anaphase, chromatids become daughter chromosomes; temporary rise to 4N in one cell.
  • Use centromere rule to decide whether chromatids count as one or two chromosomes.

TOPIC4
M phase = karyokinesis + cytokinesis; mitosis is equational (chromosome number maintained)

M PHASE BASICS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • M phase is the division phase: karyokinesis (nuclear division) followed by cytokinesis (cytoplasmic division).
  • Karyokinesis separates replicated chromosomes into two daughter nuclei (prophase → metaphase → anaphase → telophase).
  • Cytokinesis physically splits the cytoplasm to form two daughter cells after nuclear division.
  • Mitosis is called equational division because each daughter cell retains the same chromosome number as the parent cell.
  • Mitosis is described in stages for convenience, but it is a progressive process; stage boundaries are not perfectly sharp.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩M phase begins with karyokinesis and usually ends with cytokinesis.
  • ⚖️Equational division: chromosome number is maintained in progeny cells (e.g., 2n → 2n).
  • 🧠Do not confuse chromosome number with DNA content: DNA doubles in S, not chromosome number.
  • 🐝Animals: mitosis usually in diploid somatic cells; exception: haploid male honey bee shows mitosis.
  • 🌿Plants: mitosis occurs in both haploid (gametophyte) and diploid (sporophyte) stages (alternation of generations).
  • 🧫Sometimes karyokinesis is not followed by cytokinesis, producing a multinucleate syncytium (e.g., liquid endosperm in coconut).

🔎WHAT MAKES MITOSIS "EQUATIONAL"?

  • Sister chromatids separate and are distributed equally to opposite poles during anaphase.
  • Each daughter nucleus receives one chromatid of every replicated chromosome.
  • Therefore, parent and daughter cells show the same chromosome number (e.g., 2n → 2n).
  • Remember: chromosome number is maintained; DNA content per daughter cell becomes normal only after division.

⚙️WHERE MITOSIS OCCURS: ANIMALS vs PLANTS (PLOIDY CONTEXT)

  • Animals: mitosis is generally restricted to diploid somatic cells (growth, repair).
  • Exception: some haploid animal cells can divide by mitosis (classic example: male honey bee).
  • Plants: mitosis occurs in both haploid and diploid phases.
  • Haploid plant stage (gametophyte) grows by mitosis; diploid stage (sporophyte) also grows by mitosis.
  • This fits the plant life cycle pattern of alternation of generations.

TOPIC5
Chromatin condenses, duplicated centrosomes separate, and the mitotic spindle begins to assemble

MITOSIS: PROPHASE

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Prophase is the first stage of mitotic karyokinesis where chromatin condenses and the spindle apparatus begins forming.
  • It follows S and G2 phases, when DNA has replicated but remains intertwined and not distinctly visible as individual chromosomes.
  • Chromosomes become clearly visible as compact mitotic chromosomes by progressive condensation.
  • Each visible mitotic chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at a centromere.
  • Duplicated centrosomes start moving to opposite poles and radiate microtubules as asters (animal cells).
  • Asters + spindle fibres together constitute the developing mitotic apparatus.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧵Chromatin thread → progressively condensed, untangled mitotic chromosomes (visible under light microscope).
  • ✌️After S phase, each chromosome is duplicated: two sister chromatids, still counted as one chromosome.
  • 📍Centromere is the region holding sister chromatids together (kinetochore is emphasized later at metaphase).
  • 🧭Duplicated centrosomes separate and move toward opposite poles to establish spindle polarity.
  • ☀️Each centrosome forms an aster: a star-like array of microtubules radiating outward (typical in animal cells).
  • 🕸️Microtubules reorganize into spindle fibres; with asters they form the mitotic apparatus.

🔎WHAT CHANGES FROM S/G2 TO PROPHASE?

  • In S and G2, replicated DNA exists but is not distinct; newly formed DNA remains intertwined.
  • Prophase begins chromatin condensation: fibres shorten, thicken, and become untangled into chromosomes.
  • Visibility increases: compact chromosomes become microscope-visible as condensation proceeds.

⚙️END-OF-PROPHASE FEATURES TO LABEL IN AN EXAM DIAGRAM

  • Mitotic chromosomes visible, each with two sister chromatids joined at one centromere.
  • Centrosomes start moving toward opposite poles (establishing bipolar spindle).
  • Asters radiate from each centrosome (animal cells) as microtubules reorganize.
  • Spindle fibres appear; asters + spindle fibres constitute the mitotic apparatus.
  • By the end of prophase, nucleolus, Golgi complex, ER, and nuclear envelope are not visible (they reappear/reform in telophase).

TOPIC6
Metaphase alignment (equator) → Anaphase chromatid separation and poleward movement

MITOSIS: METAPHASE AND ANAPHASE

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Metaphase is the mitotic stage where chromosomes align at the cell equator after nuclear envelope disintegration (as a start marker in this text).
  • Spindle fibres attach to kinetochores on centromeres, creating bipolar attachment (one sister chromatid to each pole).
  • Metaphase plate is the imaginary equatorial plane on which chromosomes line up.
  • Anaphase begins when centromeres split simultaneously and sister chromatids separate.
  • Separated chromatids are now daughter chromosomes that migrate to opposite poles with centromere leading.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧱Metaphase start marker (as per source text): complete disintegration of nuclear envelope; chromosomes are in cytoplasm.
  • 🎯Centromere joins sister chromatids; kinetochore on centromere binds spindle microtubules.
  • 🧲Bipolar attachment: each sister kinetochore connects to opposite spindle poles.
  • 📍Best stage for chromosome morphology: metaphase (maximally condensed chromosomes).
  • ↔️Alignment at metaphase plate helps ensure equal distribution to daughter nuclei.
  • ✂️Anaphase hallmark: centromere splits → sister chromatids separate (not homologues).

🔎METAPHASE: WHAT TO WRITE IN AN EXAM DIAGRAM/ANSWER

  • Start marker (as per source): complete disintegration of nuclear envelope.
  • Chromosomes are maximally condensed → best stage to study morphology/karyotype.
  • Each chromosome has two sister chromatids joined at centromere.
  • Kinetochore is a disc-like protein structure on centromere; spindle fibres attach here.
  • All chromosomes align at equator forming the metaphase plate.

⚙️ANAPHASE: SEPARATION AND MOVEMENT

  • Onset: centromeres split simultaneously for all chromosomes on metaphase plate.
  • Sister chromatids separate and are now called daughter chromosomes.
  • Daughter chromosomes migrate to opposite poles via spindle fibres.
  • During movement, centromere faces the pole (leading), arms trail behind.
  • Mitotic anaphase separates sister chromatids, not homologous chromosomes.

TOPIC7
Chromosomes unwind, nuclei rebuild, then cytoplasm divides: furrow in animals, cell plate in plants; sometimes no cytokinesis → syncytium

MITOSIS: TELOPHASE AND CYTOKINESIS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Telophase is the final stage of mitotic karyokinesis in which daughter chromosomes reach opposite poles, decondense, and daughter nuclei reform.
  • Chromosomes lose individuality and appear as diffuse chromatin at each pole.
  • Nuclear envelope reforms around each chromatin mass, producing two daughter nuclei.
  • Nucleolus reappears; Golgi complex and ER reform and become distinctly visible again in each daughter-cell region.
  • Cytokinesis is the division of cytoplasm after karyokinesis, producing two separate daughter cells.

SIGNIFICANCE OF MITOSIS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Mitosis is an equational division producing two genetically identical daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent.
  • It increases cell number in multicellular organisms, forming the basis of body growth and development.
  • It enables repair and replacement of worn-out, damaged, or dead somatic cells.
  • In plants, mitosis in meristematic tissues ensures continuous growth throughout life.
  • It helps restore the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio disturbed by cell growth, maintaining efficient cellular control.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⚖️Equational nature: chromosome number is maintained; daughter cells are genetically similar to parent.
  • 📈Growth: repeated mitosis increases cell population; major contributor to size increase in multicellular organisms.
  • 🩹Repair/replacement: new cells replace upper epidermis and gut lining; many blood cells are continuously replaced through mitotic division of precursor/progenitor cells.
  • 🌱Plant lifelong growth: apical meristem adds length; lateral cambium adds girth (secondary growth).
  • 🎯Nucleus–cytoplasm balance: division restores optimal nucleus:cytoplasm for regulation and metabolism.

🔎HOW MITOSIS HELPS THE BODY (GROWTH + MAINTENANCE)

  • Growth in multicellular organisms: increase in cell number is primarily due to mitotic divisions.
  • Repair: replaces cells lost by injury, wear-and-tear, or programmed cell death.
  • Replacement examples: cells of upper epidermis and gut lining are regularly renewed; many blood cells are continuously replaced through mitotic division of precursor/progenitor cells.
  • Genetic stability: daughter cells inherit the same genetic complement, preserving tissue identity.

⚙️PLANT CONTINUOUS GROWTH + NUCLEO-CYTOPLASMIC RATIO

  • Meristematic tissues remain mitotically active throughout life, unlike many animal tissues.
  • Apical meristem: mitosis increases length of root and shoot (primary growth).
  • Lateral cambium: mitosis increases thickness/girth (secondary growth).
  • As cytoplasm increases during growth, mitosis restores a favorable nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio for efficient control.

TOPIC9
Specialised reduction division required for sexual reproduction: two divisions, one replication; recombination + assortment → four haploid cells

MEIOSIS: CONCEPT AND FEATURES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Meiosis is a specialised division that reduces chromosome number to half, forming haploid cells from diploid cells.
  • In animals, meiosis occurs during gametogenesis. In plants and other life cycles, meiosis forms haploid products—spores or gametes depending on the life cycle.
  • Has two sequential divisions (meiosis I and meiosis II) but only one DNA replication (before meiosis I).
  • Key unique events: synapsis of homologues and crossing over (recombination) in prophase I.
  • End result: four haploid daughter cells, genetically variable due to recombination and independent assortment.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⬇️Reduction division: chromosome number halves in meiosis I (2n → n).
  • 🔁One replication, two divisions: DNA replicates once in S phase; no replication before meiosis II.
  • 🤝Synapsis: pairing of homologous chromosomes in zygotene via synaptonemal complex.
  • 🔀Crossing over: exchange between non-sister chromatids in pachytene → recombination.
  • ✂️Anaphase I separates homologues (centromeres intact); Anaphase II separates sister chromatids.
  • 🧫Products: four haploid cells (equal-sized in spermatogenesis; in oogenesis, one ovum forms + polar bodies due to unequal cytokinesis).

🔎SIGNATURE EVENTS THAT MAKE MEIOSIS UNIQUE

  • Synapsis (zygotene): homologous chromosomes pair to form a bivalent (also called tetrad because it contains 4 chromatids).
  • Crossing over (pachytene): exchange between non-sister chromatids at recombination nodules.
  • Chiasmata (diplotene): visible X-like links where homologues stay connected after crossing over.
  • Terminalisation (diakinesis): chiasmata shift toward chromosome ends; spindle forms; nuclear envelope breaks.

⚙️OUTCOME AND BIOLOGICAL SIGNIFICANCE (EXAM CORE)

  • Maintains species chromosome number: meiosis forms haploid products (spores or gametes depending on the life cycle); fertilisation of gametes restores 2n.
  • Creates genetic variation via crossing over and random segregation (independent assortment) of homologous chromosomes in meiosis I.
  • Meiosis I is reductional; meiosis II is equational (mitosis-like).
  • Meiosis produces four haploid cells; cytokinesis may be equal (often male) or unequal (often female).

MEIOSIS I: PROPHASE I (LZPDD)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Prophase I is the first stage of meiosis I where homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis) and recombine.
  • It has five substages: Leptotene → Zygotene → Pachytene → Diplotene → Diakinesis (LZPDD).
  • Key outcomes: bivalents/tetrads formation and genetic recombination via crossing over.
  • Crossing over is completed by the end of pachytene; chiasmata become visible in diplotene and help hold homologues together.
  • Terminalisation moves chiasmata toward chromosome ends in diakinesis, preparing for metaphase I.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔗Synapsis (zygotene): pairing of homologous chromosomes using the synaptonemal complex.
  • 🧩Bivalent/tetrad: one paired homologous chromosome set with 4 chromatids total.
  • 🧪Recombination nodules (pachytene): protein sites where crossing over occurs.
  • 🔀Crossing over: exchange between non-sister chromatids of homologues; enzyme-mediated (recombinase).
  • ❌Chiasmata (diplotene): visible crossover holding points after synaptonemal complex dissolves.
  • 🎯Diakinesis: terminalisation + maximum condensation + spindle assembly + nuclear envelope breakdown.

🔎STAGE-WISE EVENTS (LZPDD) — WHAT TO WRITE IN EXAMS

  • Leptotene: chromosomes become visible; compaction/condensation begins and continues.
  • Zygotene: synapsis starts; synaptonemal complex forms along homologues.
  • Pachytene: tetrads clear; recombination nodules appear; crossing over occurs (recombination completed by end).
  • Diplotene: synaptonemal complex dissolves; homologues begin to separate except at chiasmata (now visible).
  • Diakinesis: terminalisation of chiasmata; max condensation; spindle forms; nuclear envelope breaks.

⚙️KEY TERMS (VERY TESTED) — 1-LINE DEFINITIONS

  • Synaptonemal complex: zipper-like protein scaffold that holds homologues closely during synapsis.
  • Bivalent: paired homologous chromosomes during meiosis I; also called tetrad due to 4 chromatids.
  • Recombination nodule: pachytene protein focus on synaptonemal complex where crossing over happens.
  • Crossing over: exchange between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
  • Chiasma: visible X-shaped connection at crossover site in diplotene; helps maintain pairing until anaphase I.

MEIOSIS I AND INTERKINESIS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes (not sister chromatids), reducing chromosome number by half.
  • Metaphase I: bivalents (tetrads) align on the equatorial plate; each homologue attaches to opposite spindle poles.
  • Anaphase I: homologous chromosomes disjoin and move to opposite poles; centromeres do not split.
  • Telophase I restores nuclei (often) and cytokinesis forms a dyad (two haploid cells with duplicated chromosomes).
  • Interkinesis: brief interval between meiosis I and II; no DNA replication occurs.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📌Bivalent = pair of homologues (4 chromatids total) aligned in metaphase I.
  • ↔️Metaphase I orientation enables independent assortment of maternal vs paternal homologues.
  • 🧲Spindle fibres attach to kinetochores; homologues are pulled as intact chromosomes in anaphase I.
  • ⛔Anaphase I hallmark: sister chromatids remain associated; centromeres stay intact.
  • 🧫After telophase I + cytokinesis: dyad of cells, each haploid (n) but chromosomes are still duplicated.
  • ⏸️Interkinesis is like a short interphase without S-phase: no replication, prepares for meiosis II.

🔎STAGE-WISE SNAPSHOT (WHAT TO WRITE IN ANSWERS)

  • Metaphase I: bivalents arranged on metaphase plate; each homologue faces opposite pole.
  • Spindle attachment is to kinetochores of homologous chromosomes (whole chromosomes behave as units).
  • Anaphase I: disjunction of homologues; reduction in chromosome number happens here.
  • Because centromeres do not split, each migrating chromosome still has two sister chromatids.
  • Telophase I: nuclei may re-form; cytokinesis produces two haploid cells (dyad).

⚙️INTERKINESIS (HIGH-YIELD DIFFERENTIATOR)

  • A short resting interval after meiosis I; chromosomes may partly decondense but not like true interphase.
  • No DNA synthesis/replication; cells enter meiosis II with chromosomes still duplicated.
  • Often resembles a brief G phase; prepares spindle formation for prophase II.

MEIOSIS II AND SIGNIFICANCE

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Meiosis II is the second meiotic division that is mitosis-like, separating sister chromatids.
  • It is an equational division: chromosome number per cell remains N → N (no further reduction).
  • Starts after interkinesis (short gap) with no DNA replication in between.
  • Key event: anaphase II begins with centromere splitting and chromatid segregation to opposite poles.
  • Ends with telophase II + cytokinesis producing a tetrad: four haploid daughter cells.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩Meiosis II resembles mitosis, but occurs in haploid cells formed after meiosis I.
  • 🧷Metaphase II: chromosomes align singly; kinetochores attach to opposite poles.
  • ✂️Anaphase II: centromere splits → sister chromatids become daughter chromosomes.
  • 👥Final product: 4 genetically different haploid cells (typically).
  • 🔁Chromosome number across generations: meiosis halves, fertilisation restores diploidy.
  • 🎲Genetic variability mainly from crossing over (prophase I) and independent assortment (metaphase I).

🔎STAGE-WISE EVENTS (MEIOSIS II)

  • Prophase II: chromosomes re-condense; spindle forms; nuclear envelope disappears (if present).
  • Metaphase II: chromosomes align at equator; sister kinetochores attach to opposite poles.
  • Anaphase II: centromeres split; sister chromatids move to opposite poles.
  • Telophase II: nuclei reform; chromosomes decondense; cytokinesis completes the division.

⚙️SIGNIFICANCE: CHROMOSOME NUMBER + VARIATION

  • Conservation: meiosis makes gametes haploid; fertilisation (N + N) restores diploidy (2N).
  • Meiosis II ensures equal distribution of chromatids so each haploid daughter cell/product has one copy of each chromosome.
  • Genetic variability arises due to crossing over (prophase I) and independent assortment (random bivalent orientation in metaphase I).
  • Variation provides raw material for adaptation and evolution across generations.
