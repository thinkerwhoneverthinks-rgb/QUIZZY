TOPIC1
Sunlight captured into food + oxygen: the base of ecosystems

PHOTOSYNTHESIS: IMPORTANCE

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Photosynthesis is a physico-chemical process where green plants use light energy to synthesise organic compounds.
  • It converts light energy → chemical energy stored in carbohydrates (food).
  • It uses CO2 and H2O as raw materials; green tissues with chlorophyll perform it.
  • Autotrophs (plants) are producers; heterotrophs (animals, fungi) depend on them directly or indirectly.
  • It is the primary entry point of solar energy into the biosphere and food chains.
  • It releases O2 into the atmosphere, supporting aerobic respiration and life.

🖼️Diagram

Photosynthesis links sunlight to food production and oxygen supply.

🎯Key Concepts

  • SunPhotosynthesis is the major biological process that captures solar energy for life on Earth.
  • FoodPrimary source of food: plant-made organic matter forms the base of food chains/webs.
  • OxygOxygen release: green plants maintain atmospheric O2 needed for aerobic respiration.
  • CarbRemoves CO2 and stores carbon in biomass, supporting the global carbon cycle and influencing climate.
  • EcosAutotroph–heterotroph dependence: consumers rely on producers for energy and biomass.

🔎WHY AUTOTROPHS SUPPORT HETEROTROPHS

  • Autotrophs synthesise organic matter; heterotrophs cannot make food from CO2 + H2O.
  • Energy flow: Sun → producers → consumers → decomposers (ecosystem energy largely starts with photosynthesis).
  • Even carnivores depend on plants indirectly via herbivores and detritus-based chains.
  • Plants supply raw materials: cellulose, oils, proteins, and carbon skeletons for metabolism.

⚙️WHY PHOTOSYNTHESIS IS VITAL FOR EARTH

  • Maintains atmospheric O2 enabling aerobic respiration (high energy yield).
  • Acts as a major CO2 sink by fixing carbon into biomass; important in global carbon cycling.
  • Supports agriculture and human nutrition: most dietary calories trace back to plant photosynthate.
  • If photosynthesis stops, food chains collapse and atmospheric O2 would gradually decline due to continued respiration and oxidation.

TOPIC2
Classic leaf tests proving: chlorophyll, light and CO₂ are essential for starch formation

BASIC REQUIREMENTS EXPERIMENTS (PHOTOSYNTHESIS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Simple school experiments show that photosynthesis needs chlorophyll, light and CO₂, by testing leaves for starch.
  • Starch in leaf is used as evidence of photosynthesis (carbohydrate formation).
  • Iodine test is done after decolourising the leaf to clearly see blue-black starch colour.
  • In variegated/covered leaf, starch appears only where chlorophyll is present and light falls.
  • In KOH experiment, starch forms only in the region that has access to CO₂.

🖼️Diagram

Half-leaf with KOH lacks CO₂ → no starch; exposed half gets CO₂ → starch forms.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧪Starch test: boil leaf in water, then alcohol (remove chlorophyll), rinse, add iodine → blue-black if starch present
  • 🌞Light requirement: covered region (black paper) shows no starch though rest of leaf may
  • 🟩Chlorophyll requirement: only green parts of variegated leaf test positive for starch
  • 🌫️CO₂ requirement: KOH absorbs CO₂; area enclosed with KOH shows no starch
  • ⚠️KOH does not provide CO₂; it removes CO₂ from air around the leaf part

🔎EXPERIMENT 1: LIGHT + CHLOROPHYLL (VARIEGATED / COVERED LEAF)

  • Destarch plant (keep in dark) before experiment for reliable starch test.
  • Variegated leaf: expose to light → iodine test → only green regions turn blue-black.
  • Covered leaf: cover part with black paper → expose to light → only uncovered region turns blue-black.
  • Inference: chlorophyll-containing tissue and light are necessary for starch formation.

⚙️EXPERIMENT 2: CO₂ REQUIREMENT (KOH ABSORPTION TEST)

  • Enclose part of a leaf in a test tube containing KOH-soaked cotton; other part remains in normal air.
  • Keep setup in light for some time, then perform iodine starch test on both regions.
  • Observation: region in air shows starch; region with KOH shows no starch.
  • Inference: CO₂ is essential; KOH removes CO₂ creating a CO₂-deficient environment.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
A variegated leaf is exposed to sunlight and then tested with iodine after removing chlorophyll. Only green patches turn blue-black. What does this prove?
Answer:
Blue-black with iodine indicates starch presence.
Starch forms only in green (chlorophyll-containing) parts though all parts were exposed to the same external conditions.
✓  Chlorophyll is necessary for photosynthesis (starch formation).
2
In the KOH half-leaf experiment, why does the portion inside the tube not show a blue-black colour with iodine even in bright light?
Answer:
KOH absorbs CO₂ from the air inside the tube.
Without CO₂, photosynthesis cannot fix carbon to form carbohydrates, so starch is not produced.
✓  Because CO₂ is removed by KOH; CO₂ is essential for photosynthesis and starch formation.

⭐Important Points

  • Use starch formation in leaf as evidence for photosynthesis in these basic experiments.
  • In variegated leaf: only green regions form starch → chlorophyll requirement.
  • In covered leaf: only light-exposed region forms starch → light requirement.
  • In KOH experiment: CO₂-available region forms starch; CO₂-absorbed region does not → CO₂ requirement.
  • Write conclusions precisely: not “leaf”, but “chlorophyll-containing, light-exposed part” shows starch.

💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: “LiC” for Photosynthesis Proofs”
Li → Light needed (covered part: no starch).C → Chlorophyll needed (non-green part: no starch).C → CO₂ needed (KOH part: no starch).

📌Quick Recap

TOPIC3
Priestley: plants restore air in a closed jar; Ingenhousz: light + green parts lead to O2 evolution

EARLY PHOTOSYNTHESIS EXPERIMENTS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Early photosynthesis experiments are classic bell-jar and aquatic-plant setups that linked plants with air restoration and oxygen evolution.
  • Joseph Priestley showed burning and respiration make air unsuitable, but a mint plant can restore it in a closed jar.
  • Jan Ingenhousz showed the restoring effect needs sunlight and occurs in green parts of plants.
  • Ingenhousz observed gas bubbles from aquatic plants in light, later identified as oxygen.
  • These experiments established that green tissues in light evolve O2 (seen as bubbles in aquatic plant setups).

🖼️Diagram

Priestley: plants restore air in a closed jar; Ingenhousz: sunlight + green parts → O2 bubbles.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🕯️In a sealed bell jar, a candle soon extinguishes: burning makes air unsuitable.
  • 🐭A mouse in a sealed bell jar suffocates: respiration also makes air unsuitable.
  • 🌿Mint plant in the jar restores air so candle burns again and mouse survives longer.
  • ☀️Ingenhousz: only in sunlight does the plant process "purify" air.
  • 🫧Aquatic plants form bubbles in light; bubbles were later identified as O2.
  • 🟩Only green parts release O2; non-green parts do not.

🔎PRIESTLEY: BELL JAR "CLOSED WORLD"

  • Setup: sealed bell jar with candle or mouse; later add a mint plant.
  • Observation 1: candle burns briefly, then extinguishes.
  • Observation 2: mouse survives briefly, then suffocates.
  • Key observation: with mint plant present for some days, candle can burn again; mouse lives longer.
  • Inference: burning and respiration "damage" air; plants "restore" it.

⚙️INGENHOUSZ: SUNLIGHT + GREEN PARTS ARE ESSENTIAL

  • Idea: compare plant effect in sunlight versus dark using similar setups.
  • Aquatic plant test: in bright light, bubbles appear around green parts; in dark, bubbles stop.
  • Identification: bubbles were later identified as oxygen.
  • Conclusion: O2 evolution occurs only in light and only from green tissues.

1
In Priestley’s bell jar, why does a candle extinguish and a mouse suffocate, but both can burn/survive again when a mint plant is kept in the jar for some time?
Answer:
In a sealed jar, burning and animal respiration make the air unsuitable for flame and breathing.
With a mint plant kept inside for some days, the plant restores the air quality in the closed system.
This is inferred because the candle can be lit again and the mouse survives longer compared to the jar without a plant.
✓  Because burning/respiration spoil the air in a closed jar, while the mint plant restores the air over time.
2
An aquatic plant gives bubbles only in sunlight and only from green regions. What conclusions did Ingenhousz draw from this?
Answer:
Bubbles appearing only in light show the process is light-dependent.
Bubbles coming only from green regions show chlorophyll-containing parts perform it.
With later identification of the bubbles as oxygen, the experiment links light + green parts to O2 evolution.
✓  Sunlight is essential, and only green parts evolve O2 in light.

⭐Important Points

  • Priestley’s contribution: plants restore/purify air made unsuitable by burning or respiration.
  • Ingenhousz’s contribution: sunlight is necessary for the restoring effect.
  • Ingenhousz: bubbles in aquatic plant setup were later identified as oxygen.
  • Only green parts (chlorophyll-containing) evolve O2 in light; non-green parts do not.
  • A bell jar acts as a closed system, making air-composition changes detectable.

TOPIC4
How we know carbohydrate accumulates, O2 evolves, and what the overall reaction really means

EVIDENCE AND OVERALL EQUATIONS (PHOTOSYNTHESIS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Evidence-based summary: Photosynthesis uses light to reduce CO2 to carbohydrate in green tissues and evolves O2.
  • Julius von Sachs showed that carbohydrate produced in green parts is commonly stored as starch (iodine test).
  • Engelmann linked wavelength to photosynthetic rate using O2-seeking aerobic bacteria as indicators.
  • Overall equations are net summaries of many steps, not single one-step reactions.
  • Key inference (van Niel, later confirmed by isotopes): evolved O2 in green plants comes from H2O, not CO2.

🖼️Diagram

Engelmann’s setup: aerobic bacteria show where O2 evolution (photosynthesis) is highest.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧪Sachs: photosynthesis occurs in green parts; starch accumulates there; chlorophyll is in chloroplasts.
  • 🔦Engelmann: action spectrum of photosynthesis shows peaks in blue and red regions.
  • 🦠Aerobic bacteria cluster where O2 is highest → indirect detector of O2 evolution.
  • 🧩[CH2O] denotes a generic carbohydrate unit (C:H:O ≈ 1:2:1), not a real molecule.
  • 💧van Niel generalisation: hydrogen donor H2A decides the oxidised product; O2 is released only when donor is H2O (oxygenic photosynthesis).

🔎EVIDENCE: CARBOHYDRATE FORMATION (SACHS)

  • Sachs: carbohydrate accumulates in green parts during photosynthesis.
  • The accumulated carbohydrate is commonly stored as starch (iodine test).
  • Chlorophyll is located in special bodies later called chloroplasts.
  • Inference: carbohydrate formation is associated with chlorophyll-containing tissues.

⚙️EVIDENCE: O2 EVOLUTION & ACTION SPECTRUM (ENGELMANN)

  • White light split by a prism illuminates different wavelengths on a Cladophora filament.
  • Aerobic bacteria act as O2 detectors; they accumulate where O2 is released most.
  • Bacteria cluster mainly in blue and red regions → photosynthetic activity is highest there.
  • Action spectrum roughly resembles chlorophyll absorption spectra; accessory pigments can shift/extend activity.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Why did Engelmann use aerobic bacteria in his experiment, and what conclusion did he draw from their distribution?
Answer:
Aerobic bacteria require O2 for respiration, so they move toward regions with higher O2 concentration.
When different wavelengths illuminated different parts of the alga, bacteria clustered near blue and red illuminated zones.
Thus, O2 evolution (and hence photosynthesis) was maximum in blue and red light: the action spectrum peaks there.
✓  Aerobic bacteria acted as O2 indicators; their blue/red clustering showed photosynthesis is most active in blue and red wavelengths.
2
A student claims: "The O2 released in photosynthesis comes from CO2 because CO2 contains oxygen." Correct this using van Niel’s general equation.
Answer:
van Niel generalised photosynthesis as: CO2 + 2H2A → [CH2O] + H2O + 2A (in light).
Here H2A is the hydrogen donor; A is the oxidised product after donation.
In green plants, H2A = H2O, so the hydrogen donor is oxidised and O2 is released.
Therefore, evolved O2 originates from water, not from CO2 (confirmed by isotope tracing).
✓  O2 is released from oxidation of H2O (the hydrogen donor), not from CO2.

⭐Important Points

  • Sachs connected green parts with carbohydrate accumulation and starch storage.
  • Engelmann inferred photosynthesis from O2 distribution using aerobic bacteria.
  • Action spectrum maxima: blue and red; do not say only these wavelengths work.
  • Empirical equation uses [CH2O] as a generic carbohydrate unit, not a single-step product.
  • van Niel’s equation explains oxygenic vs anoxygenic photosynthesis via the hydrogen donor.

TOPIC5
Where photosynthesis happens: plant organs → mesophyll cells → thylakoid membranes and stroma

CHLOROPLAST STRUCTURE AND SITES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Photosynthesis occurs in chlorophyll-containing (green) parts, mainly leaves, inside chloroplasts.
  • In leaves, photosynthesis is chiefly in mesophyll cells rich in chloroplasts.
  • Chloroplasts often align along mesophyll cell walls to optimise incident light capture.
  • Inside chloroplasts, thylakoid membranes (grana + stroma lamellae) perform light reactions.
  • Stroma (matrix) contains enzymes for CO2 fixation and sugar synthesis; sugars may be stored as starch.
  • “Dark reaction” means not directly light-driven, but it depends on ATP and NADPH from light reactions.

🖼️Diagram

From leaf mesophyll to chloroplast: membranes run light reactions; stroma runs carbon fixation and sugar formation.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🍃Site in plant: leaves primarily; any green tissue with chloroplasts can photosynthesise.
  • 🧫Leaf site: mesophyll cells have many chloroplasts; positioning along walls improves light interception.
  • 🧩Division of labour: thylakoid membranes make ATP + NADPH; stroma uses them to make sugars.
  • ⚡Light reactions are photochemical and membrane-bound (grana/lamellae).
  • 🧪Carbon reactions (Calvin cycle) occur in stroma; they are indirectly light-dependent.

🔎DIVISION OF LABOUR INSIDE CHLOROPLAST

  • Grana = stacks of thylakoids; large membrane area for photochemical steps.
  • Stroma lamellae connect grana; part of the membrane system for electron transport.
  • Membrane system traps light energy and forms ATP + NADPH (energy and reducing power).
  • Stroma is enzyme-rich; synthesises sugars from CO2 using ATP and NADPH.
  • Synthesised sugars may be converted to starch within the chloroplast.

⚙️LIGHT vs DARK (CARBON) REACTIONS: WHAT THE NAMES REALLY MEAN

  • Light reactions: directly light-driven; occur on thylakoid membranes; produce ATP, NADPH, and O2.
  • Dark reactions (carbon reactions): not directly light-driven; occur in stroma; use ATP + NADPH for CO2 fixation.
  • Carbon reactions can continue briefly in darkness only until ATP/NADPH last.
  • Therefore, carbon reactions are indirectly light-dependent and not “night-only” processes.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Why is the term “dark reaction” considered misleading in photosynthesis?
Answer:
“Dark” means not directly light-driven, not that it happens only in darkness.
Carbon reactions require ATP and NADPH made by light reactions on thylakoid membranes.
In darkness, carbon reactions may continue briefly using existing ATP/NADPH, then stop until light restores them.
✓  Carbon reactions occur in stroma and are indirectly dependent on light via ATP and NADPH; they are not night-only.
2
A student claims: “Photosynthesis happens only in leaves.” Correct the statement using proper site terms.
Answer:
Photosynthesis occurs in chlorophyll-containing (green) parts of the plant, not only leaves.
Leaves are the primary site because mesophyll cells contain many chloroplasts, but other green tissues with chloroplasts can also photosynthesise.
✓  Photosynthesis occurs mainly in leaves but also in other green chloroplast-containing tissues.

⭐Important Points

  • Write green parts (chloroplast-containing), not simply “leaf”, as the site of photosynthesis.
  • In leaves, the key cellular site is mesophyll; chloroplasts are numerous and often wall-aligned for light.
  • Thylakoid membranes (grana + stroma lamellae) are the site of ATP and NADPH formation.
  • Stroma is the site of enzyme-mediated CO2 fixation and sugar synthesis.
  • “Dark reaction” = carbon reactions in stroma; they require products of light reactions.

TOPIC6
Types, chromatography, spectra & why accessory pigments matter

PHOTOSYNTHETIC PIGMENTS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Photosynthetic pigments are light-absorbing molecules in thylakoid membranes that capture light energy for photosynthesis.
  • Chlorophyll a is the chief pigment that performs primary photochemistry in reaction centres.
  • Accessory pigments (chlorophyll b, xanthophylls, carotenes) broaden usable wavelengths by transferring energy to chlorophyll a.
  • Pigments show characteristic absorption spectra (absorption vs wavelength).
  • Action spectrum shows effectiveness of different wavelengths in driving photosynthesis (often inferred from O2 evolution).
  • Accessory pigments also provide photoprotection by reducing photo-oxidation damage under excess light.

🖼️Diagram

Chromatographic bands identify major pigment types; accessory pigments funnel excitation energy to chlorophyll a for photochemistry.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🟩Chlorophyll a: chief/primary pigment; absorbs mainly in blue and red; directly drives reaction-centre photochemistry.
  • 🟨Chlorophyll b: accessory; extends light harvesting; funnels excitation energy to chlorophyll a.
  • 🟧Carotenoids (carotenes + xanthophylls): absorb mainly in blue/blue-green; aid energy transfer + photoprotection.
  • 🧪Paper chromatography separates pigments by differential solubility/partitioning and adsorption to paper.
  • 📈Absorption spectrum and action spectrum need not match exactly because multiple pigments contribute in vivo.
  • 🛡️Photoprotection: carotenoids help dissipate excess excitation energy and reduce chlorophyll photo-oxidation.

🔎TYPES OF PHOTOSYNTHETIC PIGMENTS (HIGHER PLANTS)

  • Chlorophyll a: blue-green; chief pigment in reaction centres; also present in antennae.
  • Chlorophyll b: yellow-green; accessory; expands light harvesting compared to chlorophyll a alone.
  • Xanthophylls: yellow oxygenated carotenoids; accessory + photoprotective role.
  • Carotenes: yellow to yellow-orange carotenoids; accessory + protect against photo-oxidation.

⚙️PAPER CHROMATOGRAPHY: SEPARATION OF LEAF PIGMENTS

  • Principle: pigments partition between stationary phase (paper) and mobile solvent.
  • Different pigments move different distances due to differences in solubility and adsorption/partitioning.
  • Typical band order (top → bottom): carotene → xanthophyll → chlorophyll b → chlorophyll a (near origin).
  • Use: demonstrates multiple pigments in leaves, explaining shades of green and broader light absorption.

TOPIC7
How pigments are organised into LHC/antenna + reaction centres P680 & P700 to start electron flow

LIGHT REACTIONS: PHOTOSYSTEMS (PS I & PS II)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Light reactions are photochemical events on thylakoid membranes producing ATP, NADPH and releasing O2.
  • Pigments are arranged into two pigment–protein units: Photosystem II (PS II) and Photosystem I (PS I).
  • LHC (Light Harvesting Complex) contains hundreds of pigments bound to proteins; it captures light efficiently.
  • Antenna complex = all pigments of a photosystem except one special chlorophyll a; it funnels excitation energy.
  • Reaction centre = the special chlorophyll a that performs primary photochemistry by donating an excited electron.
  • Reaction centres: P680 in PS II (680 nm) and P700 in PS I (700 nm); photosystems are named by discovery order.

🖼️Diagram

Pigment organisation: LHC/antenna funnel energy to P680 (PS II) and P700 (PS I); PS II links to water splitting.

🎯Key Concepts

  • ☀️Energy flow: antenna pigments absorb many wavelengths → transfer excitation to reaction centre.
  • 🎯Electron flow: PS II (P680) excites first; electrons move via ETS → PS I (P700) → NADP+.
  • 💧Photolysis is linked to PS II: 2H2O → O2 + 4H+ + 4e− (replaces PS II electrons).
  • 🫧Oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from H2O, not from CO2.
  • 🔋Outputs of light reactions: ATP (photophosphorylation) + NADPH (NADP+ reduction).
  • 🧭Name trap: PS I discovered before PS II, but non-cyclic electron flow starts at PS II.

🔎ORGANISATION INSIDE A PHOTOSYSTEM

  • LHC: pigment–protein complex; includes chlorophylls + carotenoids; broadens usable wavelengths.
  • Antenna pigments: absorb light and transfer excitation energy (not electrons) to reaction centre.
  • Reaction centre chlorophyll a: performs primary charge separation; sends electron to primary acceptor.
  • Accessory pigments also provide photoprotection (reduce photo-oxidation of chlorophyll a).

⚙️PHOTOCHEMICAL EVENTS: WHO DOES WHAT?

  • PS II (P680) is the first to lose excited electrons in non-cyclic flow; electrons enter ETS.
  • Photolysis replaces PS II electrons; generates O2 and adds H+ (lumen-side concept).
  • PS I (P700) re-excites electrons; final acceptor is NADP+ → NADPH.
  • Overall outputs: ATP (photophosphorylation) + NADPH; O2 diffuses out.

ELECTRON TRANSPORT: Z SCHEME (NON-CYCLIC FLOW)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Z scheme is the non-cyclic electron transport pathway: PSII → electron carriers (PQ, cytochrome b6f, PC) → PSI → NADP+.
  • Electrons are excited twice: at P680 (PSII) and again at P700 (PSI), giving a Z-shaped energy profile.
  • Excited electrons are first trapped by a primary electron acceptor of each photosystem.
  • Between the two photo-excitation steps, electrons pass through carriers in a net downhill redox/energy sequence; the two light-driven boosts create the overall Z-shaped pattern.
  • PSII replaces lost electrons by photolysis of water at the oxygen-evolving complex (OEC): 2H2O → 4H+ + O2 + 4e−.

🖼️Diagram

Electrons originate from water at PSII (OEC), pass via PQ/cytochrome b6f/PC to PSI, then reduce NADP+ to NADPH via FNR on the stroma side.

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⚡PSII (P680) acts first; PSI (P700) acts later despite names.
  • 🎯Primary electron acceptor captures excited electrons rapidly to reduce back-transfer/energy loss.
  • 🧬Major carriers: plastoquinone (PQ), cytochrome b6f complex, plastocyanin (PC); electrons are transferred carrier-to-carrier.
  • ⬇️Energy released during downhill transfers supports proton pumping (via cytochrome b6f), building a H+ gradient.
  • 💧Water-splitting (OEC) is part of PSII and faces the thylakoid lumen (inner side).
  • 🧪Terminal acceptor: NADP+; reduced to NADPH on the stroma side by –NADP+ reductase .

🔎STEPWISE WORKING (WHAT HAPPENS WHERE)

  • 1. PSII excitation: P680 absorbs ~680 nm; electron is excited and trapped by the PSII primary acceptor.
  • 2. Carrier transfer: electron moves via PQ → cytochrome b6f → PC in a net downhill sequence.
  • 3. PSI excitation: electron reaches PSI; P700 absorbs ~700 nm and re-excites an electron.
  • 4. Terminal reduction: electrons pass to ferredoxin and then to NADP+ via FNR; NADPH forms on the stroma side.
  • 5. Replacement electrons: PSII regains electrons from photolysis of water at the OEC to keep flow continuous.

⚙️PHOTOLYSIS OF WATER (ELECTRON SOURCE FOR PSII)

  • Water-splitting complex (OEC) is associated with PSII on the inner (lumen) side of thylakoid membrane.
  • Reaction: 2H2O → 4H+ + O2 + 4e−.
  • Electrons replenish P680; O2 is released as a net product of oxygenic photosynthesis.
  • H+ released into lumen contributes to proton gradient used for ATP synthesis (chemiosmosis).

PHOTOPHOSPHORYLATION & CHEMIOSMOSIS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Photophosphorylation is light-driven synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate in chloroplasts.
  • Occurs on thylakoid membranes where PSII/PSI, ETS carriers and ATP synthase are embedded.
  • Two modes: non-cyclic (PSII → PSI → NADP+) and cyclic (PSI only; e− returns to PSI).
  • Chemiosmosis explains ATP synthesis by a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane.
  • In chloroplasts, H+ accumulate in the lumen; ATP is made on stroma side by CF0–CF1 ATP synthase.

🖼️Diagram

Non-cyclic (PSII→PSI→NADP+) builds H+ in lumen; return via CF0–CF1 makes ATP; cyclic loops e− back to PSI.

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⚡Non-cyclic flow forms ATP + NADPH; cyclic flow forms ATP only.
  • 🔁Cyclic photophosphorylation: PSI e− cycles via ETS back to PSI; NADP+ not reduced.
  • 🧩Stroma lamellae: lack PSII and lack NADP reductase → favors cyclic pathway.
  • 💧Water splitting at PSII adds H+ to lumen and supplies replacement e−.
  • ⛽NADP+ reduction on stroma side consumes H+ from stroma, strengthening gradient.
  • 🚪H+ returns lumen → stroma through CF0; CF1 synthesises ATP.

🔎CYCLIC vs NON-CYCLIC PHOTOPHOSPHORYLATION (WHAT HAPPENS TO ELECTRONS?)

  • Non-cyclic: e− originate from H2O (PSII) and end on NADP+ (NADPH formed).
  • Non-cyclic: both PSII (P680) and PSI (P700) participate in series (Z-scheme).
  • Cyclic: only PSI functions; e− returns to PSI via ETS; no net e− gain/loss by PSI.
  • Cyclic favored in stroma lamellae because PSII absent and NADP reductase absent.
  • Result check: cyclic → ATP only; non-cyclic → ATP + NADPH; O2 released only with PSII.

⚙️CHEMIOSMOTIC ATP SYNTHESIS (HOW H+ GRADIENT IS MADE & USED)

  • Core idea: ATP synthesis is coupled to proton motive force across the thylakoid membrane.
  • H+ accumulation in lumen occurs by three linked events during light reaction:
  • (1) Photolysis at PSII on inner side releases H+ into lumen; O2 is by-product.
  • (2) Electron transport drives H+ translocation from stroma to lumen via carriers.
  • (3) NADP+ reduction on stroma side consumes H+ from stroma (stroma becomes more alkaline).

CALVIN CYCLE (C3 PATHWAY)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Calvin cycle is the cyclic enzymatic pathway in chloroplast stroma that reduces CO2 to carbohydrates using ATP and NADPH.
  • Also called C3 pathway because the first stable product of CO2 fixation is 3-PGA (3-carbon).
  • Primary CO2 acceptor is RuBP (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate), a 5-carbon sugar.
  • Key enzyme is RuBisCO (ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase) that catalyses carboxylation.
  • The cycle has three stages: carboxylation → reduction → regeneration of RuBP.
  • Energy cost (standard): 3 ATP + 2 NADPH per CO2; for one glucose: 18 ATP + 12 NADPH.

🖼️Diagram

Three stages in stroma: RuBP carboxylation (RuBisCO) → PGA reduction (ATP, NADPH) → RuBP regeneration (ATP).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📍ATP and NADPH made in thylakoid membranes are used in stroma (biosynthetic phase).
  • 🧪Calvin used radioactive C14O2 to trace carbon; first stable labelled product: PGA.
  • 🧩Primary acceptor ≠ first stable product: acceptor RuBP (5C), first stable product PGA (3C).
  • ⚙️RuBisCO is carboxylase-oxygenase; in C3 plants it fixes CO2 to RuBP.
  • 🔁Cycle is cyclic because RuBP is regenerated using ATP.
  • 🔋Accounting per CO2: reduction uses 2 ATP + 2 NADPH; regeneration uses 1 ATP.

🔎CALVIN’S C14 TRACING: WHAT IT PROVED

  • Used C14O2 in algae to track labelled carbon through intermediates.
  • Earliest stable labelled compound detected: 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA).
  • Hence pathway named C3: first stable product has 3 carbons.
  • Helped map a cyclic sequence where RuBP is regenerated.

⚙️STAGES + ENERGY ACCOUNTING (EXAM CORE)

  • 1. Carboxylation: RuBP (5C) + CO2 —RuBisCO→ unstable 6C → 2 × PGA (3C).
  • 2. Reduction: 2 PGA → sugars (via triose phosphate) using 2 ATP + 2 NADPH per CO2.
  • 3. Regeneration: rearrangements regenerate RuBP using 1 ATP per CO2 fixed.
  • Total per CO2: 3 ATP + 2 NADPH; per glucose: 18 ATP + 12 NADPH.

C4 PATHWAY (HATCH–SLACK) & KRANZ ANATOMY

📖Definition & Introduction

  • C4 pathway (Hatch–Slack) is a CO2 fixation route where the first stable product is OAA (4C).
  • Occurs with Kranz anatomy: mesophyll surrounds bundle sheath in a wreath-like arrangement.
  • Initial CO2 fixation happens in mesophyll using PEP as primary acceptor via PEP carboxylase.
  • C4 acids (malate/aspartate) move to bundle sheath, get decarboxylated, releasing CO2.
  • Released CO2 is fixed by RuBisCO in bundle sheath (Calvin cycle), minimizing photorespiration.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ConcFirst stable product in C4 plants: oxaloacetic acid (OAA).
  • ConcCO2 is concentrated around RuBisCO in bundle sheath, suppressing oxygenation.
  • ConcDivision of labour: PEPcase in mesophyll; RuBisCO + Calvin cycle in bundle sheath.
  • ConcBundle sheath cells typically have thick/suberised walls and few intercellular spaces, helping reduce CO2 leakage.
  • ConcTransport forms: malate and/or aspartate shuttle carbon to bundle sheath.

🔎KRANZ ANATOMY (STRUCTURAL BASIS)

  • Wreath (Kranz) arrangement: bundle sheath forms a ring around vascular bundle; mesophyll surrounds it.
  • Bundle sheath chloroplasts are prominent; site where Calvin cycle mainly operates in C4 plants.
  • Thick/suberised bundle sheath walls help reduce CO2 leakage; intercellular spaces are few.
  • Close mesophyll–bundle sheath connectivity supports rapid metabolite exchange (C4 acids ↔ 3C acids).

⚙️HATCH–SLACK PATHWAY (WORKING, STEPWISE)

  • In mesophyll: CO2 + PEP (3C) → OAA (4C) catalysed by PEP carboxylase.
  • OAA is converted to malate or aspartate and transported to bundle sheath.
  • In bundle sheath: C4 acid is decarboxylated → releases CO2 near RuBisCO.
  • Released CO2 enters Calvin cycle in bundle sheath → sugars formed.
  • The 3C residue (often pyruvate) returns to mesophyll; regenerated back to PEP to continue the cycle.

PHOTORESPIRATION AND C3–C4

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Photorespiration is the pathway that metabolises 2-phosphoglycolate formed when RuBisCO uses O2 (oxygenase activity), leading to CO2 release and energy cost with no net sugar gain.
  • RuBisCO has dual affinity: acts as carboxylase (CO2) or oxygenase (O2) on RuBP at the same active site.
  • Oxygenase reaction forms one 3C PGA and one 2C 2-phosphoglycolate, initiating the photorespiratory pathway.
  • Photorespiration does not produce ATP/NADPH and does not directly synthesize sugars.
  • It is prominent in C3 plants under high temperature, high O2, and low CO2 (often due to stomatal closure).

🖼️Diagram

RuBisCO can fix CO2 (Calvin cycle) or add O2 (photorespiration).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧬RuBisCO: competitive binding of CO2 vs O2 at the same active site.
  • 🧪RuBP + O2 ⇒ PGA (3C) + 2-phosphoglycolate (2C) (requires salvage; no direct carbohydrate formation).
  • 🔁Photorespiration involves chloroplast–peroxisome–mitochondrion cooperation and returns part of the carbon back to Calvin cycle as PGA.
  • 🔋Costly: CO2 is released and ATP/reducing power is spent; no ATP/NADPH is generated by this pathway.
  • 🏜️C4 plants use a CO2-concentrating mechanism, keeping RuBisCO in a high-CO2 microenvironment.

🔎WHY RuBisCO CAUSES PHOTORESPIRATION (C3 CONTEXT)

  • RuBisCO active site cannot perfectly discriminate CO2 from O2 (competitive binding).
  • High temperature decreases CO2 solubility more than O2, raising the O2:CO2 ratio around the enzyme.
  • Stomatal closure (heat/water stress) lowers internal CO2, favouring oxygenation of RuBP.
  • Oxygenation yields 2-phosphoglycolate (2C), which cannot enter the Calvin cycle directly.

⚙️PHOTORESPIRATION: PATHWAY OUTCOME (WHY COSTLY)

  • Occurs via chloroplast → peroxisome → mitochondrion → chloroplast metabolite shuttle.
  • Net effect: CO2 is released and carbon is partially recovered back as PGA.
  • No ATP or NADPH is produced; instead ATP and reducing power are spent to salvage carbon.
  • Thus, it reduces photosynthetic efficiency and biomass in many C3 plants, especially under hot, dry conditions.

TOPIC13
Internal + external controls of photosynthetic rate with Blackman’s limiting factor law

FACTORS AFFECTING PHOTOSYNTHESIS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Rate of photosynthesis is the speed of CO2 fixation / O2 evolution / carbohydrate formation per unit time.
  • Photosynthetic rate is jointly controlled by internal (leaf/chloroplast traits) and external (environmental) factors.
  • At any instant, the factor in minimum effective supply acts as the limiting factor (Blackman, 1905).
  • When the limiting factor is increased, rate rises until another factor becomes limiting (rate shows a plateau).
  • Many factors interact; hence field photosynthesis rarely reaches the theoretical maximum.

🖼️Diagram

Photosynthetic rate is controlled by internal + external factors; the limiting factor governs the immediate rate.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩Blackman’s law: only the limiting factor controls rate at that moment; others show little effect.
  • 🍃Internal controls include leaf age/orientation, mesophyll, stomata, chloroplast number/distribution, chlorophyll amount.
  • ☀️Light affects rate via quality (wavelength), intensity, and duration (photoperiod).
  • 🫧CO2 is often limiting because atmospheric CO2 is low (around ~0.04%, i.e., ~400 ppm).
  • 🌡️Temperature mainly affects enzyme-driven carbon reactions; light reactions are less temperature-sensitive.
  • 💧Water affects rate indirectly: stomatal closure lowers internal CO2; dehydration damages metabolism.

🔎BLACKMAN’S LAW OF LIMITING FACTORS (HOW TO APPLY)

  • Statement: When a process depends on several factors, rate is limited by the slowest (most limiting) factor.
  • If light is low, increasing CO2 or temperature has little effect until light increases.
  • At high light, CO2 often becomes limiting because air has only about ~0.04% CO2.
  • A plateau in rate vs one factor suggests another factor has become limiting.
  • In exam answers, name the limiting factor and justify with given condition (low/high).

⚙️INTERNAL FACTORS (PLANT/LEAF CONTROL)

  • Leaf age: young mature leaves show higher rate than very young or senescent leaves.
  • Leaf orientation: incident light interception depends on angle; chloroplast alignment optimises capture.
  • Mesophyll abundance: more photosynthetic cells and surface for diffusion supports higher rate.
  • Chloroplast number & distribution: more chloroplasts in mesophyll increases light absorption capacity.
  • Internal CO2 (iCO2): depends on stomatal aperture and diffusion path; low iCO2 limits Calvin cycle.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
A plant is kept in bright light. Increasing CO2 concentration increases photosynthetic rate strongly, but further increase in light intensity shows little change. Which factor was limiting first, and what is limiting now?
Answer:
Bright light suggests light is not limiting initially.
Strong rise in rate on increasing CO2 indicates CO2 was the limiting factor first.
After CO2 is raised, no response to more light implies another factor (e.g., temperature, enzyme capacity, or water status) is now limiting.
✓  Initially CO2 was limiting; after CO2 enrichment, limitation shifts to a non-light factor (commonly temperature/biochemical capacity, depending on conditions).
2
During drought, leaves show reduced photosynthetic rate even on sunny days. Explain using internal CO2 concentration and Blackman’s law.
Answer:
Water deficit causes stomatal closure to reduce water loss.
Closed stomata reduce CO2 entry, lowering internal CO2 in mesophyll.
Even if light is abundant, low internal CO2 becomes the limiting factor controlling the rate (Blackman’s law).
✓  Drought lowers internal CO2 via stomatal closure; CO2 becomes limiting, so high light cannot raise the rate.
