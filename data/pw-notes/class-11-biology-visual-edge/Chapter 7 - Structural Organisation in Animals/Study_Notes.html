Structural Organisation in Animals

TOPIC1
From single-cell life to organ systems: specialisation + coordination = division of labour

LEVELS OF ORGANISATION (ANIMALS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Levels of organisation describe how functions are distributed from cells → tissues → organs → organ systems in multicellular animals.
  • Unicellular organisms: one cell performs all life processes (nutrition, respiration, excretion, reproduction).
  • Multicellular animals: the same basic functions are performed by different groups of cells in a coordinated, organised way.
  • Tissue: group of similar cells + intercellular substance performing a specific function.
  • Organ: structure made of ≥1 tissue types arranged to perform a particular function.
  • Organ system: two or more organs interacting physically and/or chemically to perform a common function.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧫Unicellular: no tissues/organs; the cell itself is the functional unit.
  • 🧩Multicellular: specialisation reduces load on each cell and increases efficiency.
  • 🏗️Hierarchy: cells assemble into tissues; tissues build organs; organs form organ systems.
  • 🤝Division of labour: work split among units; requires coordination (nervous/endocrine).
  • 🧠Scale idea: Hydra has thousands of cells, while humans have billions—so higher organisation is essential for coordination.
  • 🫀Example: heart contains all four basic tissue types working together.

🔎FOUR BASIC TISSUE TYPES (IN COMPLEX ANIMALS)

  • Epithelial: coverings/linings; protection, absorption, secretion.
  • Connective: support, binding, transport (e.g., blood, bone, cartilage).
  • Muscular: contraction for movement and organ motility.
  • Neural: impulse conduction; control and coordination.

⚙️DIVISION OF LABOUR: WHY MULTICELLULARITY NEEDS LEVELS

  • Specialised cells perform one task better than a generalist cell.
  • Tissues coordinate similar cells to produce a bigger functional output.
  • Organs integrate multiple tissues to run a complex function as one unit.
  • Organ systems coordinate organs for whole-body functions (digestion, respiration, etc.).
  • More cells and complexity demand stronger coordination across the body.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Why is division of labour a key advantage of multicellular organisation?
Answer:
In multicellular animals, similar cells group into tissues to specialise in one function.
Different tissues combine to form organs, allowing complex functions to be performed efficiently.
Organs form organ systems, enabling coordinated whole-body functioning and survival.
✓  Division of labour increases efficiency by distributing functions among specialised cells, tissues, organs and organ systems with proper coordination.
2
A student says: "Since the heart is an organ, it must be made of only one tissue." Correct the statement with reasoning.
Answer:
An organ is formed by one or more tissue types arranged to perform a particular function.
The heart contains epithelial (lining), connective (support and blood), muscular (pumping), and neural (control) tissues.
✓  Incorrect; the heart is an organ made of multiple tissues, not a single tissue.

⭐Important Points

  • Tissue = similar cells + intercellular substance performing a specific function.
  • Only four basic tissue types form complex animals: epithelial, connective, muscular, neural.
  • Organ = arrangement of ≥1 tissue type; organ system = ≥2 organs interacting for a common function.
  • Hierarchy: cells → tissues → organs → organ systems.
  • As cell number increases (Hydra → humans), higher organisation is needed for coordination.

MORPHOLOGY AND ANATOMY (FROG AS REPRESENTATIVE VERTEBRATE)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Morphology is the study of form and externally visible features of body/parts.
  • In animals, morphology focuses on the external appearance of organs or body parts.
  • Anatomy is the study of the internal structure, especially internal organs and systems.
  • This chapter uses frog as a representative vertebrate to learn external and internal features.
  • A representative vertebrate helps connect structure → function across major organ systems.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 👀Morphology answers: What is seen outside? (shape, body regions, limbs, surface features).
  • 🫀Anatomy answers: What is inside? (organs, body cavity, organ systems, internal connections).
  • 🧭Frog is a standard vertebrate model (Chordata; Amphibia) for integrated structure-function study.
  • 🧩External observations can hint at adaptations (e.g., webbed feet → swimming).
  • ⚠️Exam framing: write morphology for external features; write anatomy for internal organs/systems.

🔎WHAT TO WRITE UNDER MORPHOLOGY (FROG: EXTERNAL FEATURES)

  • Body divisions: head and trunk; neck absent; tail absent (adult frog).
  • Skin: smooth, slippery due to mucus; kept moist; absorbs water through skin.
  • Colour: dorsal usually olive green with dark spots; ventral pale yellow.
  • Head features: nostrils above mouth; bulged eyes with nictitating membrane; tympanum visible.
  • Limbs: forelimb 4 digits; hind limb 5 digits; webbed feet aid swimming.

⚙️WHAT TO WRITE UNDER ANATOMY (FROG: INTERNAL ORGAN SYSTEMS)

  • Anatomy describes internal organs in the body cavity and their arrangement into systems.
  • Major systems: digestive, respiratory, circulatory, nervous, excretory, reproductive.
  • Digestive plan: alimentary canal ends in cloaca (common chamber), not a separate anus.
  • Frog is carnivorous, so intestine is relatively short compared with herbivores.
  • Use system names + 1 key organ feature in answers (e.g., heart has 3 chambers: 2 atria + 1 ventricle).

TOPIC3

FROG HABITAT AND ADAPTATIONS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Frog (Rana tigrina) is an amphibian chordate adapted to live on land and in freshwater.
  • It is poikilothermic (cold-blooded): body temperature varies with environmental temperature.
  • It shows camouflage by changing colour to hide from enemies; in this chapter, this protective coloration is termed mimicry (not copying another animal).
  • Survives extreme seasons by dormancy in deep burrows: aestivation (summer) and hibernation (winter).
  • During aestivation/hibernation, gaseous exchange continues mainly through moist skin (cutaneous respiration).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧬Classification: Phylum Chordata → Class Amphibia → frog (Rana tigrina).
  • 🌡️Poikilothermy: metabolism and activity depend strongly on ambient temperature.
  • 🎭Protective coloration: colour change + dorsal spots reduce detection; used for protection and ambush.
  • 🏜️Aestivation: summer dormancy in burrows to avoid dehydration and heat stress.
  • ❄️Hibernation: winter dormancy in burrows to survive extreme cold and low activity.
  • 🕳️Burrowing habit: shelter + moisture conservation; supports survival during harsh seasons.

🔎HABITAT + CLASSIFICATION (EXAM LINES)

  • Habitat: lives both on land and in freshwater (amphibious).
  • Classification: Phylum Chordata, Class Amphibia.
  • Common Indian species used in textbooks: Rana tigrina (Indian bullfrog).
  • Seen near ponds, paddy fields, and moist terrestrial regions; needs moisture for skin.

⚙️ADAPTATIONS: HOW THEY HELP SURVIVAL

  • Poikilothermy: no constant body temperature; activity slows in cold, increases in warmth.
  • Camouflage: dorsal olive-green with dark spots helps blending in vegetation and muddy water.
  • In this chapter, mimicry means protective coloration that enables camouflage (not imitation of another animal).
  • Aestivation: hides in deep burrows during peak summer to reduce water loss and heat stress.
  • Hibernation: shelters in deep burrows during winter to avoid extreme cold.

DIGESTIVE SYSTEM IN FROG

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Frog digestive system = short alimentary canal (mouth → cloaca) + digestive glands aiding digestion.
  • Frog is carnivorous, so intestine is relatively short for quicker passage of protein-rich food.
  • Food capture uses a muscular, bilobed tongue; swallowing begins in buccal cavity and pharynx.
  • Stomach secretes HCl + gastric enzymes and converts food into chyme (semi-fluid, partially digested).
  • Duodenum receives bile (fat emulsification) and pancreatic juice (digests carbohydrates and proteins).
  • Absorption occurs mainly in intestine via villi + microvilli; undigested matter exits via rectum → cloaca.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧭Pathway: Mouth → Buccal cavity → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Intestine → Rectum → Cloaca.
  • 👅Bilobed tongue helps capture prey; frog feeds on insects and small animals.
  • 🧪Stomach: HCl + gastric juice start digestion; forms chyme.
  • 🟩Liver secretes bile; bile stored in gall bladder and released into duodenum.
  • 🟨Pancreas releases pancreatic juice into duodenum; digests carbohydrates + proteins.
  • 🧬Villi/microvilli increase surface area for absorption of digested nutrients.

🔎ALIMENTARY CANAL: MOUTH TO CLOACA

  • Mouth: entry of food; prey seized and pushed in by bilobed tongue.
  • Buccal cavity → pharynx: passage for swallowing.
  • Oesophagus: short tube connecting pharynx to stomach.
  • Stomach: muscular bag; churns food and mixes with gastric juice.
  • Intestine: site of final digestion and absorption; relatively short due to carnivory.

⚙️DIGESTION → CHYME → DUODENUM → ABSORPTION → EGESTION

  • Stomach digestion: HCl creates acidic medium; gastric enzymes act; food becomes chyme.
  • Duodenum: receives secretions through a common bile duct region; mixing completes major chemical digestion.
  • Bile (from liver; stored in gall bladder): emulsifies fats, aiding lipase action.
  • Pancreatic juice (from pancreas): digests carbohydrates and proteins.
  • Intestinal wall: villi and microvilli greatly increase surface area for absorption.

RESPIRATION IN FROG

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Respiration in frog occurs by different organs in water vs on land.
  • In water: cutaneous respiration—dissolved O2/CO2 exchange through moist skin by diffusion.
  • On land: buccal cavity, skin and lungs all act as respiratory surfaces.
  • Pulmonary respiration means gas exchange through lungs; major mode on land.
  • During aestivation and hibernation, gaseous exchange occurs mainly through skin.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 💧In water, skin is the chief respiratory organ (cutaneous respiration).
  • 🫧Skin must stay moist; gases diffuse across vascularised skin surface.
  • 🌿On land, respiration is tripartite: buccal cavity + skin + lungs.
  • 🫁Lungs are elongated, pink, sac-like structures in the upper part of trunk region.
  • ➡️Air pathway: nostrils → buccal cavity → lungs.
  • 🛌Aestivation/hibernation: lung ventilation drops; cutaneous respiration sustains.

🔎AQUATIC (WATER) RESPIRATION: CUTANEOUS

  • Skin acts as the aquatic respiratory organ; O2 is taken from dissolved oxygen in water.
  • Exchange occurs by diffusion across thin, moist, vascular skin.
  • Mucus keeps skin slippery and moist, helping diffusion and preventing drying.
  • CO2 produced in tissues diffuses out through the same skin surface.

⚙️TERRESTRIAL (LAND) RESPIRATION: BUCCAL + CUTANEOUS + PULMONARY

  • Buccal cavity respiration: gas exchange across moist lining of buccopharyngeal cavity.
  • Cutaneous respiration continues on land, but becomes limited if skin dries.
  • Pulmonary respiration via lungs is the major mode on land.
  • Lungs are elongated, pink, sac-like and lie in the upper part of trunk region.
  • Air route: nostrils → buccal cavity → lungs; CO2 exits along reverse path.

TOPIC7

CIRCULATORY AND LYMPHATIC SYSTEMS (FROG)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Frog has a closed type blood vascular system: blood remains within heart and vessels.
  • Components: heart + blood vessels + blood; circulation maintained by heart pumping.
  • Heart is muscular, in upper body cavity, and covered by pericardium.
  • Heart has two atria and one ventricle; receives blood and pumps into arterial trunks.
  • Lymphatic system consists of lymph + lymph channels + lymph nodes for tissue fluid return.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔒Closed circulation: higher pressure, faster transport; blood does not directly bathe tissues.
  • 🏛️3 chambers: right atrium mainly receives deoxygenated; left atrium oxygenated (from lungs/skin).
  • 🔺Sinus venosus is triangular; joins right atrium; receives blood via vena cavae.
  • 🧪Conus arteriosus is sac-like; arises ventrally; ventricle opens into it.
  • 🛣️Arteries carry blood away from heart; veins return blood to heart.
  • 🧭Portal systems: hepatic (intestine→liver), renal (lower body→kidney) special venous routes.

🔎CLOSED CIRCULATION + VESSEL TYPES

  • Closed type: blood flows heart → arteries → capillaries → veins → heart.
  • Arteries: carry blood away from heart; usually thicker, narrower lumen, higher pressure.
  • Veins: carry blood towards heart; usually thinner, wider lumen, valves more common.
  • Capillaries: microscopic exchange vessels; connect arterioles to venules.

⚙️PORTAL SYSTEMS + BLOOD & LYMPH COMPOSITION

  • Hepatic portal system: blood from intestine passes to liver before returning to heart.
  • Renal portal system: blood from lower body passes to kidneys before reaching heart.
  • Blood = plasma (fluid matrix) + formed elements (RBCs, WBCs, platelets).
  • Frog RBCs are nucleated and contain haemoglobin for O2 transport.
  • Lymph lacks RBCs and has fewer proteins than blood; drains tissue fluid to blood.

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Frog excretory system removes nitrogenous wastes (mainly urea) via kidneys, ureters, urinary bladder and cloaca.

  • Urine passes through ureters and may be stored temporarily in a thin-walled urinary bladder.

NEURAL AND ENDOCRINE CONTROL IN FROG

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Control and coordination in animals occurs through neural system and endocrine (hormonal) system.
  • Neural control uses electrical impulses for rapid, short-lived, precise responses.
  • Endocrine control uses hormones in blood for slower, longer-lasting, often widespread regulation (only receptor-bearing tissues respond).
  • In frog, nervous system has CNS (brain, spinal cord), PNS (cranial, spinal nerves) and ANS (sympathetic, parasympathetic).
  • In frog, brain is protected by cranium and divided into forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⚡Neural: fast signalling; synapses + nerves coordinate muscles and glands.
  • 🧪Endocrine: ductless glands; hormones act on specific target tissues with receptors.
  • 🧠Forebrain (frog): olfactory lobes, cerebral hemispheres, diencephalon.
  • 👁️Midbrain (frog): optic lobes; major visual reflex centre.
  • 🧩Hindbrain (frog): cerebellum + medulla oblongata; medulla continues as spinal cord.
  • 🔟Frog has 10 pairs of cranial nerves arising from brain.

🔎DIVISIONS OF NERVOUS SYSTEM (FROG)

  • CNS: brain + spinal cord; integrates and coordinates responses.
  • PNS: cranial nerves + spinal nerves; connects CNS to body parts.
  • ANS: autonomic control of viscera; sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions.
  • Cranial nerves: 10 pairs arise from brain in frog (humans: 12 pairs).

⚙️BRAIN REGIONS + CONTINUITY WITH SPINAL CORD (FROG)

  • Brain enclosed in cranium; divided into forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain.
  • Forebrain: olfactory lobes, paired cerebral hemispheres, unpaired diencephalon.
  • Midbrain: paired optic lobes (visual processing/reflexes).
  • Hindbrain: cerebellum (balance/coordination) + medulla oblongata (vital centres).
  • Medulla oblongata passes through foramen magnum and continues as spinal cord (in vertebral column).

TOPIC10

SENSE ORGANS IN FROG

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Sense organs in frog are specialised receptors/structures that detect stimuli and send impulses to the CNS.
  • Main types: touch (sensory papillae), taste (taste buds), smell (nasal epithelium), vision (eyes), hearing/balance (tympanum + internal ear).
  • Eyes and internal ears are well-organised; other sense organs are mainly cellular aggregations near nerve endings.
  • External ear is absent; only tympanum is visible externally.
  • Ear performs both hearing and balancing (equilibrium).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ✋Touch: sensory papillae in skin act as touch receptors.
  • 👅Taste: taste buds in buccal cavity/tongue detect dissolved chemicals.
  • 👃Smell: nasal epithelium (olfactory lining) perceives odours.
  • 👁️Eyes: pair of spherical simple eyes; located in orbits of skull.
  • 👂Ear: tympanum visible externally; internal ear handles hearing + equilibrium.

🔎EYES (VISION): STRUCTURE HIGHLIGHTS

  • Eyes are a pair of spherical structures; bulged appearance on head.
  • Situated in the orbit of the skull (bony socket).
  • They are simple eyes: only one functional unit (not compound like insects).
  • Nictitating membrane protects eye surface, especially during swimming.
  • Vision is coordinated via optic nerve to CNS (optic lobes prominent in midbrain).

⚙️EAR (HEARING + EQUILIBRIUM): EXAM-RELEVANT FEATURES

  • External ear absent: no pinna in frog.
  • Tympanum is a circular membrane on either side of eyes; visible externally.
  • Tympanum receives sound vibrations and transmits them to internal ear.
  • Internal ear is the main organ for hearing and balancing (equilibrium).
  • Balance: internal ear detects head position/movement, helping posture and coordinated jumping/swimming.
