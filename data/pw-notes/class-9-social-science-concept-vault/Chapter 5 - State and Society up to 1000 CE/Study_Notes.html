Chapter 5: State and Society up to 1000 CE — Complete Short Notes

Sources and Scope of the Period

Scope (up to 1000 CE): How states + societies were organised, how they emerged/spread across the subcontinent, and how shared ideals shaped governance + social life.
  • From early communities → settled civilisations: Human groups grew from early communities into settled societies/civilisations, incl. the Sindhu–Sarasvatī Civilisation.
  • Sources used: From at least the second millennium BCE, literary sources start adding to archaeological evidence.
  • Earliest literary source: Ṛig Veda.
  • What evidence tells us: Using literary + archaeological sources together helps us study:
    • social + political realities
    • moral + cultural ideas
    • institutions
    • ideologies
    • economic practices
    • systems of governance
  • Evolution of political organisation: From kinship units like jana and kula →
    • janapadas
    • mahājanapadas
    • regional kingdoms
    • large empires
  • Examples of major imperial powers: Mauryas, Guptas, Cholas.
  • Continuities despite political change: Some ideas kept recurring for centuries:
    • dharma
    • chakravarti samrāṭ (ideal universal emperor)
    • aśhvamedha yajña (horse-sacrifice ritual linked with sovereignty)
    • ideal of pan-Indian political authority
Exam focus: Explain how historians reconstruct early state/society: shift from mostly archaeology → archaeology + texts (esp. the Ṛig Veda), and what each source type tells us.

Understanding Early Indian State and Society

Meaning of Society

Society: A web/system of relationships among people sharing a territory, a culture, and a sense of belonging.
  • Why societies need norms: Big groups need rules + shared norms for order + harmony.
  • Norms create organisation: Shared norms link different groups into an organised social system.
  • How early Indian social organisation developed: Via customs, traditions, and agreed practices.
  • What society includes:
    • families + households
    • institutions like marriage
    • customs + practices
    • social relationships
  • How society is regulated: Mainly by customs + accepted practices, not just formal laws.

Meaning of State

State: An organised political system that works through rules + laws.
  • What a state contains:
    • rights + duties of rulers and subjects
    • institutions of government
    • administrative mechanisms
    • systems to maintain law + order
  • How states developed: Slowly/gradually as societies became larger + more complex.
  • How we study state formation: Sources help track changes in political authority + governance.
Don’t mix up society (relationships + shared culture/custom) vs state (political authority + laws + administration). In exams, define both separately.

The Beginnings

The Vedas as Historical Sources

Vedas: Earliest known body of Indian literature; key source for reconstructing early Indian life since Harappan writing is undeciphered.
  • Why especially important: Harappan writing remains undeciphered → Vedic literature becomes vital for early history.
  • Transmission: Preserved orally for centuries before being written.
  • Date of the Ṛig Veda: Debated; estimates from fifth to second millennium BCE.
  • Place of composition: Composed in north-western India, where the Sindhu–Sarasvatī Civilisation had flourished.
  • Technological overlap: Vedic society + Sindhu–Sarasvatī Civilisation both knew copper.
Q: “Why are the Vedas important sources?” Include: (i) antiquity, (ii) oral preservation, (iii) Harappan script undeciphered, (iv) info range (society, polity, economy, religion, daily life), (v) cultural continuity.

State and Administration

The Four Vedas

Ṛig Veda

Ṛig Veda: earliest Veda; key source for early Vedic society + ideas.
  • Status: earliest Veda.
  • Literary form: some of the world’s oldest surviving poetry.
  • Structure: 1,028 hymns (= sūktas).
  • Themes: praise of deities + big questions on creation, cessation, birth, death, etc.

Yajur Veda

  • Function: “how to do” the practical performance of yajñas (ritual sacrifices).
  • Relation to Ṛig Veda: builds/elaborates on hymns mentioned in Ṛig.
  • Style: mostly prose explanations of rituals.

Sāma Veda

  • Content source: hymns mainly taken from the Ṛig Veda.
  • Arrangement: organised for musical recitation.
  • Cultural contribution: chanting tradition helped shape the seven svaras (core of Indian music).

Atharva Veda

  • Variety: wide range of hymns.
  • Protective hymns: some to ward off evil.
  • Healing: others on physical + mental illnesses and treatments.
A common error: calling all Vedas only “religious hymns”. Keep them distinct → Ṛig (hymns/poetry), Yajur (ritual procedure/prose), Sāma (musical chanting), Atharva (misc. incl. protection + healing).

Four Parts of Vedic Texts

Each Veda has 4 broad parts, moving from ritual → philosophical inquiry.
  1. Saṁhitā: hymns to invoke deities + offer oblations in yajñas.
  2. Brāhmaṇa: prose explanations of performing rituals.
  3. Āraṇyaka: philosophical reflections (linked to forest-dwelling sages).
  4. Upaniṣhad: discussions on the Self (Ātman) + the Ultimate Universal Being (Brahman).
Often tested: Brahman = philosophical concept; Brāhmaṇas = a type/category of Vedic texts; Brāhmaṇas/Brahmins = a varṇa group.

Importance of Vedic Literature

  • Where earliest hymns formed: earliest Ṛig Vedic hymns composed in Sapta-Sindhu.
  • What Sapta-Sindhu included:
    • Indus River
    • its five tributaries
    • Sarasvatī River
  • What Vedic texts help reconstruct:
    • geography
    • political institutions
    • social structures
    • economic activities
    • religious practices
    • everyday life
  • Why foundational: because of their antiquity + cultural continuity, they’re core sources for Indian culture/tradition.

Political Institutions in the Vedic Period

Jana and Pañchajana

Jana: kinship-based clan unit; base of early Vedic social + political organisation.
  • Organisation: early Vedic society = organised into janas (kin-connected clans).
  • Number mentioned: Ṛig Veda mentions about thirty janas.
  • Pañchajana: 5 major janas called pañchajana:
    • Yadu
    • Turvaśha
    • Puru
    • Anu
    • Druhyu

Bharata Jana

  • First appearance: name Bharata first shows up in the Ṛig Veda.
  • Meaning in context: referred to the Bharata jana ruled by the Bharata family.
  • Historical inference: early communities identified via kinship, clan ties, + ruling families.

Role of the Rājā

Rājā (early Vedic): mainly a clan chief at first, not an absolute monarch.
  • Main responsibilities:
    • leading the clan in warfare
    • protecting clan members
    • representing the community
  • Limits on power: authority checked by assemblies; not always absolute.
Exam phrasing: early Vedic kingship = kin-based leadership + assembly checks, NOT a later centralized monarchy.

Popular Assemblies in Early Vedic Polity

Sabha

Sabha = early political assembly with:
  • A relatively small membership
  • Mainly selected elites
  • Main role: judicial functions (disputes/justice matters)

Samiti

Samiti = larger assembly that:
  • Included a broader population than sabha
  • Discussed policies + political matters

Vidhata

Vidhata = popular gathering:
  • Attended by members of the jana (people/tribal community)
  • Discussed warfare + other political issues
  • These assemblies promoted consultation + public participation.
  • They may have acted as checks on the rājā’s power, like (in principle) modern legislatures limiting executive authority.

Iron, Agriculture and New Settlements

Changes in the First Millennium BCE

Archaeological maps show an increased settlement concentration in the Ganga region in the first millennium BCE.
Key archaeological indicators:
  • wider use of iron tools
  • greater variety of grains
  • Painted Grey Ware (PGW) pottery
  • expanded cultivation
  • increased production of paddy

Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW)

  • Second half of first millennium BCE: middle Ganga region develops Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW).
  • NBPW = smooth, glossy black surface.
  • Its arrival suggests a richer, more advanced material culture.

Factors Behind the Changes

Major drivers of first-millennium BCE changes:
  • iron implements for clearing land + cultivation
  • fertile Ganga plains
  • rise in agricultural production
  • crop diversification
  • surplus food creation
  • population growth
  • growth of trade
  • formation of larger settlements + political units

Early Kingdoms and Republics

From Jana to Janapada

  • c. 1000 BCE → 600 BCE: kin-based janas slowly turned into territorial units called janapadas.
  • Janapada = “place where a jana set its feet”.
  • Identity now tied to kinship + territory (not just clan ties).
  • Land, farming output, and trade routes became key.
  • This shift helped create more complex administration.

Mahājanapadas

c. 600 BCE → 300 CE: bigger states formed; texts often list 16 mahājanapadas:
  1. Kamboja
  2. Gandhāra
  3. Matsya
  4. Kuru
  5. Śhūrasena
  6. Pañchāla
  7. Vatsa
  8. Kosala
  9. Kāśhī
  10. Malla
  11. Vṛijji
  12. Magadha
  13. Anga
  14. Chedi
  15. Avanti
  16. Aśhmaka

Rise of Magadha

Magadha (mostly present-day Bihar) became the strongest mahājanapada because of:
  • Fertile plains
  • Strategic location
  • Access to resources
  • Strong rulers
  • Agricultural surplus
  • Control of trade routes
Magadha’s growth later led to the Mauryan Empire (one of early India’s biggest empires).

Expansion into the Ganga Plains

  • Early Vedic culture = mainly the Indus-Sarasvatī region.
  • Later Vedic culture spread into the Ganga plains.
  • By c. 600 BCE, the region saw:
  • Agricultural expansion
  • Larger political formations
  • Complex social institutions
  • More settlement
  • New forms of government

Monarchies and Republics

Northern India had different political setups:
  • Rājyas = monarchies ruled by kings
  • Gaṇas / saṁghas = republican/oligarchic political organisations

Regional Kingdoms

Satavāhanas

  • Satavāhanas replaced Mauryan power across much of the Deccan.
  • Ruled for ~500 years: about 2nd century BCE → 3rd century CE.

Early Tamilakam Polities

Main early southern polities + their core regions:
  • Cholas: lower Kaveri valley
  • Pandyas: Tamraparni and Vaigai valleys
  • Keralaputras / Cheras: Kerala
  • Satiyaputras: northern Tamil Nadu
Also named in Aśhoka’s inscriptions.

Royal Emblems

  • Cholas: Tiger
  • Cheras: Bow
  • Pandyas: Fish

Sangam Literature

  • Sangam literature = earliest Tamil literary tradition.
  • Broad dates: 300 BCE → 300 CE.
  • Major source for early Chera, Chola, Pandya kingdoms.
  • These rulers = three crowned kings / Vendar of Tamilakam.
  • Prosperity came from fertile rice-growing regions.
  • Poems talk about:
  • Brave, ambitious rulers
  • Warfare
  • Trade
  • Society
  • Vedic yajñas
  • Links with epic traditions
  • One Chera king earned the title adhirāja after defeating the other crowned kings.
Don’t mix up the early Sangam-age Chera/Chola/Pandya kingdoms with the later imperial Chola, Chera, and Pandya states.

Duties and Ideals of the King

Titles of Rulers (from the sixth century BCE)

From about the 6th century BCE, rulers used titles like:
  • Rājā
  • Mahārāja
  • Samrāṭ
  • Adhirāja
These titles show regional traditions and different levels/claims of sovereignty.

Responsibilities of the King

What texts expect from a king:
  • Arthaśhāstra: an energetic king creates energetic officers (good rule depends on the ruler’s vigour).
  • Yajur Veda (coronation oath): king should—
  • Judge strong + weak impartially
  • Protect the land from calamities
  • Work for public welfare
  • Śhānti Parva (Mahābhārata): guidance on—
  • Ethical conduct
  • Justice
  • Public responsibility
  • Protection of subjects
King as protector: must guard people from—
  • External enemies
  • Internal disorder
  • Robbery
  • Theft
  • Abduction
  • Adultery
  • Treachery
  • Major crimes could bring harsh punishments, even capital punishment.
  • Kingship usually hereditary, but texts also mention elected kings + kings being expelled.
  • So succession wasn’t always strictly hereditary, and royal power wasn’t always absolute.

Pan-Indian Political Ideals

Geographical and Political Unity

Political thinking often went beyond one kingdom → imagined the whole subcontinent. Common terms/expressions:
  • Jambudvīpa
  • Bhāratavarṣha
  • Prithivi
  • Aśhvamedha yajña
  • Rājasūya yajña
  • Chakravarti kśhetra
  • Chakravarti samrāṭ

Jambudvīpa and Prithivi

  • Aśhoka said his efforts brought spiritual change across Jambudvīpa.
  • Arthaśhāstra: Prithivi = land between the Himalayas and the sea.
  • It equates Prithivi with chakravarti kśhetra = realm of a universal sovereign.

Examples of Pan-Indian Aspiration

  • Chera ruler Nedunjeral Adan is described as campaigning up to the Himalayas.
  • In the 11th century, Chola ruler Rajendra I took the title Gangaikonda after victories near the Ganga.
  • Both show a repeated aim to hold authority across the Indian subcontinent.

Council of Ministers and the State

Saptāṁga Theory (Kauṭilya)

Kauṭilya’s Saptāṁga theory = State as an organic whole made of 7 interlinked parts:
  1. Swāmi — King
  2. Amātya — Councillors/ministers/high officials
  3. Janapada — Territory + population
  4. Durga — Fortified towns/cities
  5. Koṣha — Treasury/wealth
  6. Daṇḍa — Defence forces + law & order machinery
  7. Mitra — Allies
State runs well only when all 7 are co-ordinated.

Mantri-Pariṣhad (Council of Ministers)

  • Arthaśhāstra: “One wheel does not move the carriage.” → govt needs cooperation.
  • So, good governance needs organised administration + teamwork.
  • King ruled with help of the mantri-pariṣhad (council of ministers).
  • Usually a small group of experienced statesmen.
  • Key members included:
  • Treasurer
  • Chief tax collector
  • Chief legal adviser
  • Commander-in-chief
An Aśhokan inscription shows ministers could take independent decisions if the emperor was absent and public interest required it.

Inscriptions as Historical Sources

Junagadh Rock Inscription (Girnar, Gujarat)

Junagadh rock near Girnar (Gujarat) has inscriptions of 3 rulers, spread across ~700 years.

Aśhoka

  • Date: 3rd century BCE
  • Inscriptions: Major Rock Edicts in Prakrit
  • Core message: Dhamma + moral governance

Rudradaman I

  • Identity: Western Satrap ruler
  • Date: c. 150 CE
  • Contribution: major inscription in Sanskrit
  • Content: military achievements + repairs of Sudarshana Lake

Skandagupta

Skandagupta = the 3rd ruler linked to the Junagadh rock inscriptions (overall time span ~700 years).

Rock Inscriptions as Public Records (Evidence Across Dynasties)

Some rock sites were reused by later rulers because they were already known public sites tied to earlier authority/achievements. Reuse = a way to boost legitimacy by linking yourself to respected older public works + governance.
  • A 5th-century Gupta emperor recorded restoration of a lake after major damage.
  • Same site gives evidence for many periods/themes:
    • Mauryas
    • Western Satraps
    • Guptas
    • Public works (e.g., lake building/repair)
    • Administration
    • Political history
    • Language change over time (inscriptions show shifting usage)
Exam angle: One inscriptional site can be a multi-period source—note which dynasty did what (esp. public works) + what it suggests about administration, legitimacy, and language change.

Administration of Empires

Early Administrative Divisions

Big kingdoms used a hierarchical (layered) admin system—from the centre down to local communities.
  • Typical divisions:
    • Provinces
    • Divisions
    • Districts
    • Groups of villages
    • Villages

Satavāhana Administration

  • Empire split into āḥāras (admin units).
  • Each āḥāra had officials called amātyas.
  • Village head = grāmika.
  • King had supreme authority, but day-to-day work done by provincial + local officials.
  • District governors = pradeśhikas, doing:
    • Judicial duties
    • Administrative duties
  • Officials consulted key locals like:
    • Bankers
    • Caravan leaders
    • Artisans
    • Scribes
  • City governors handled:
    • Revenue collection
    • Law & order
Don’t mix levels: āḥāra = Satavāhana territorial unit; amātya = official category; grāmika = village head; pradeśhika = district governor (judicial + admin).

Decentralisation, 300–800 CE

Decentralisation (c. 300–800 CE) = growing role of provincial, district, and village bodies in running the kingdom → layered governance, not purely centre-controlled.
  • Levels + terms used:
    • Kingdom: North Rājya; South Rājya
    • Province: North Bhukti; South Maṇḍala / maṇḍalam
    • Division: North Viṣhaya / bhoga; South Koṭṭam / valanāḍu
    • District / local unit: North Adhiṣhṭhāna / paṭṭana; South Nāḍu
    • Group of villages: North Vithi; South Paṭṭalā / kūṛram
    • Lowest unit: Village (both)
  • Why break into smaller units?
    • To make large areas easier to govern.
  • How it functioned:
    • Provincial governors + district officers + village institutions = layered admin.
  • Modern comparison in text: like states → districts → tehsils → villages.
Exam focus: (1) define decentralisation here, (2) match North vs South terms for same level (e.g., Bhukti vs Maṇḍalam; Viṣhaya vs Valanāḍu).

Gupta, Pallava and Chālukya Administration

Gupta Administration

  • Guptas kept many earlier institutions → clear continuity with older admin systems.
  • Mantri = head of civil administration.
  • Other key officers:
    • Commander-in-chief
    • General
    • Chief of palace guards
    • Sāndhivigrahika = minister of peace & war
  • Earlier amātyas category expanded → included kumārāmātyas (handled provincial/local units).

Damodarpur Copper Plates (Gupta Period Evidence)

Damodarpur Copper Plates (time of Kumaragupta I) = documentary proof of district office composition → district governance included both officials + occupational leaders.
  • District office had:
    1. Head district officer
    2. Chief banker
    3. Chief caravan trader
    4. Chief artisan
    5. Chief revenue collector
  • What it shows (exam-style inference): district governance = participation of admin + occupational leaders.
Don’t present Damodarpur only as a “land grant source” here—use it to show who sat in district-level administration (banker, caravan trader, artisan, revenue collector + head officer).

Pallava Administration

  • Pallavas ruled c. 275–897 CE.
  • Their setup mixed:
    • Centralised monarchy
    • Decentralised local government
  • Admin divisions: provinces → districts → taluks → villages.
  • Granted tax-free land to Brahmadeya villages.
  • Village assemblies worked fairly independently.
  • Committees called variyams managed:
    • Irrigation
    • Gardens
    • Temples
    • Other local responsibilities
Pattern to remember: Pallava rule = monarchy + strong village institutions; specialised variyams handle fixed local jobs (esp. irrigation + temples).

Chālukya Administration

  • Badami Chālukyas ruled c. 543–753 CE.
  • System = monarchy + decentralised administration.
  • Provincial divisions + autonomous village assemblies followed the wider contemporary pattern.
  • Granted land to Brahmin settlements called agrahārams.
  • Agrahārams like Aihole and Badami became major centres of learning.
Don’t mix the grants: Pallavas → Brahmadeya villages; Chālukyas → agrahārams (Brahmin settlements). Also: Aihole/Badami = learning centres.

Major Powers

Tripartite Struggle

Tripartite struggle = rivalry among three big powers to control Kannauj (a highly prized political capital).
  • Gurjara-Pratihāras dominated much of north & west India (c. 8th–10th centuries).
  • Main capital Kannauj = politically very valuable.
  • Three contenders for Kannauj:
    • Gurjara-Pratihāras
    • Pālas
    • Rāṣhṭrakūṭas
  • All three typically used:
    • Monarchy
    • Decentralised administration
  • Village = smallest unit; largely self-reliant.
  • Villages handled:
    • Infrastructure
    • Social welfare
    • Education
    • Local resources
Exam line: “Tripartite struggle” = Pratiharas vs Palas vs Rashtrakutas for Kannauj. Add the theme: decentralised village-level governance stays strong across these kingdoms.

Imperial Cholas and Village Governance

Chola Administrative Divisions

Imperial Cholas (c. 9th–11th centuries) = known for efficient administration + strong revenue management, backed by a clear territorial ladder + powerful village institutions.
  • Territorial divisions (big → small):
    1. Maṇḍalams = provinces
    2. Valanāḍus = districts
    3. Nāḍus = group of villages
    4. Urs = single villages
  • Village assemblies ran major local work:
    • Revenue collection
    • Land administration
    • Irrigation
    • Roads
    • Records
    • Disputes
    • Local affairs (overall)
  • These assemblies were self-reliant—not fully dependent on the king’s machinery.
Common test order: Maṇḍalam → Valanāḍu → Nāḍu → Ur. Don’t interchange Nāḍu (group) with Ur (single village).

Uttaramerur Inscription

Uttaramerur inscription (10th c., reign of Parantaka I) = major source for Chola village governance, esp. Kudavolai (“ballot pot”) + committee system.
  • Site: Vaikunṭha Perumal Temple, today’s Kanchipuram district.
  • Focus: explains village governance + Kudavolai selection.

Kudavolai (“Ballot Pot”) Process

  1. Names of eligible candidates written on palm leaves.
  2. Leaves put into a pot.
  3. A young child draws the leaves in a public gathering.
  4. Done openly, often in a temple setting.
  5. Chosen members sent into specialised variyams (committees).
  • Committees looked after:
    • Irrigation tanks
    • Justice
    • Tax collection
    • Other village responsibilities
Kudavolai aims at transparency + fairness: public venue, open steps, and random draw by a child → less scope for manipulation.

Ethics: Foundation of Law and Life

Ethical Thought

  • Vedas + epics talk about:
    • Reality
    • Right conduct
    • Origins of existence
    • Nature of the universe
    • Ideal ways of living
  • Bhagavad Gītā emphasises:
    • Knowledge
    • Faith
    • Action
    • Virtue
    • Ethical life
  • Ethical principles guided both State + society.

Samatva

Samatva = sameness / equality at the basic level → materially + spiritually, reality is one, even if social forms look different.
  • Samatva says:
    • All bodies are made of the same matter.
    • All consciousness shows one supreme consciousness.
  • Vedas name this reality as:
    • Satya = the real/true
    • Ṛita = right/ordered reality
  • Mahābhārata: people from different varṇas still uphold samatva.
  • The epics:
    • Critique discrimination.
    • Good person/ruler = works for welfare of all beings.
Don’t shrink samatva to only “social equality.” Here it’s mainly basic equality of matter + consciousness, and it’s used to question discrimination in ethical stories.

Ṛita

Ṛita = cosmic order (in the Ṛig Veda) → ties nature + society + morality into one balanced, stable system.
  • Ṛita stands for:
    • Harmony in nature
    • Balance in society
    • Moral order
    • Regulation of natural forces
    • Universal stability

Dharma

Dharma ≠ just “religion.” It’s a wide ethical-legal idea: duty, obligation, righteousness, moral conduct, justice.
  • Dharma includes:
    • Duty
    • Obligation
    • Righteousness
    • Moral conduct
    • Justice
  • People were expected to do duties linked to their social roles.
  • Buddhism (Pāli) uses the equivalent word: dhamma.

Meaning of the Word “Dharma”

  • Dharma comes from Sanskrit root dhri = “to uphold.”
  • Bhishma (in Mahābhārata): dharma is whatever upholds all beings.
  • Dharma can also mean an inherent property of something, e.g.:
    • Iron sinking
    • Wood floating
Exam trap: writing “dharma = religion.” Better: dharma = an upholding principle covering ethics, duty, justice, and even inherent properties.

Ethics in Governance

  • Aśhoka’s Dhamma (from edicts) promoted:
    • Moral conduct
    • Family respect
    • Ethical everyday behaviour

Religious Life and Bhakti

Vedic Religious Life

  • Vedic pantheon = no fixed/rigid hierarchy.
  • Different hymns praise different gods as “supreme”.

    • Yajñas
    • Offerings into the sacred fire

Renunciatory Traditions

By mid–first millennium BCE, renunciants rejected attachment to material comforts + household life. Key terms:
  • Parivrājaka: wanderer
  • Bhikṣhu: lives on alms
