SHAPING OF THE EARTH’S SURFACE

Shaping of the Earth’s Surface

Landform = natural feature on Earth’s surface formed by crustal movement, weathering, erosion, or deposition. Examples: mountains, valleys, plateaus, plains, deserts, coastal features.

Plate Tectonics

Plate tectonics (linked here with W. J. Morgan) = Earth’s outer layer is broken into big + small tectonic plates that move slowly over a semi-molten layer → creates/controls mountains, earthquakes, volcanoes.

Interior of the Earth

Crust: outermost layer (where we live); ~30–40 km under continents, ~5–7 km under oceans. Mantle: very thick, hot, mostly solid layer between crust + outer core; ~2,900 km. Core: extremely hot + heavy innermost region. Outer core: fluid, mainly iron + nickel; ~2,200 km. Inner core: solid, hot, spinning metal ball; densest part; ~1,250 km. Lithosphere: rigid crust + upper mantle; ~100 km; broken into plates. Asthenosphere: hot, mobile, partly molten layer under lithosphere; ~200 km; lets plates move.

Tectonic Plates and Their Movement

Tectonic plates: huge slabs of solid rock moving only a few cm/year.
Types of plates:
  • Continental plates: carry continents
  • Oceanic plates: carry ocean floors
  • Mixed plates: carry both continents + ocean floors
Major plates: Pacific, Eurasian, African, North American, South American, Indo-Australian, Antarctic.
Cause of plate movement: core heat drives mantle convection (hot rises, cool sinks). These convection currents push/pull plates. Heat transport modes: advection, convection, conduction.

Plate Boundaries

Convergent boundary: plates move together.
  • Continental–continental collision → fold mountains
  • Oceanic–continental collision → denser oceanic plate subducts → volcanoes + earthquakes
Example: Himalaya.
Divergent boundary: plates move apart; magma rises → new crust + mid-ocean ridges. Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
Transform boundary: plates slide past; no crust made/destroyed; friction → earthquakes. Example: San Andreas Fault, USA.

Importance and Hazards of Plate Tectonics

  • Plate movement builds mountains, valleys, ocean basins, volcanoes + explains layout of continents and oceans.
  • Most earthquakes + active volcanoes occur along plate boundaries.
  • Many circle the Pacific Ocean = “Ring of Fire”.
  • Comparing plate/earthquake/volcano maps shows close correlation (they mostly occur together at boundaries).
  • Plate-tectonic knowledge helps spot earthquake- + volcano-prone regions and improves disaster management.
  • India has faced major quakes, incl. Gujarat (2001). Losses rise due to dense population, weak construction, and settlements in high-risk zones → higher loss of life + environmental damage.

Knowledge Windows: Earthquakes and Volcanoes

  • Early Indian thought called earthquakes bhūkampa (“shaking of the Earth”).
  • In the Bṛihatsaṁhitā, Varāhamihira linked possible signs—wind, rain, clouds, animal behaviour, planetary alignments—to 4 forces: Vāyu, Agni, Indra, Varuṇa → early mix of observation + cosmological reasoning.
  • Volcanic eruptions show strong internal forces; may deposit grey ash + other material.
  • Baratang Island (Andaman & Nicobar) has India’s only mud volcano: mud bubbles out (not fiery lava) due to underground gases + pressure.

Weathering and Erosion

Over long time, weathering + erosion together: wear down mountains, carve valleys, form plains, make caves, cliffs, river deltas.

Weathering

Meaning: breaking surface rock into smaller pieces without movement of the broken material. Importance: shapes land + helps form soil.
Types of weathering:
  • Physical weathering: mechanical breaking by heating/cooling, frost/ice expansion, water, wind.
  • Chemical weathering: water/air/acids react with minerals → new substances.
  • Biological weathering: plants/animals/microbes break rock; e.g., roots widen cracks and split rock.
Exam trap: No movement of broken material = weathering. Once it’s carried away = erosion.

Erosion

Meaning: wearing away + transport of soil/rock/surface material by water, wind, ice, waves. Key distinction: erosion includes movement (unlike weathering).
Major types (by agent):
  • Water erosion: rain, rivers, ocean waves
  • Wind erosion: common in dry, sandy areas
  • Glacial erosion: moving ice scrapes + carries rock
  • Coastal erosion: sea waves
Effects on people:
  • Loss of fertile topsoil → lower crop yield
  • River/coastal erosion can remove land, homes, roads
  • Unstable ground threatens construction + mining
  • Damage to beaches/rivers/fertile land hurts tourism + fishing

Conservation Knowledge

Historical practices: the Sindhu-Sarasvatī civilisation used contouring, bunding, terracing, dams, canals. Recorded in the Vedas, Kṛiṣhiparāśhara, Kauṭilya’s Arthaśhāstra, Vṛikṣhāyurveda. Arthaśhāstra: guidance for assessing + classifying land by fertility + productivity.
  • Contouring / continuous contour trenches: along contour lines → slow, store, infiltrate rainwater; cut erosion; recharge groundwater.
  • Bunding: earthen embankments on contours → slows run-off; reduces erosion; improves infiltration + soil moisture.
  • Terracing: hillside into level/gently sloping steps → prevents erosion.
  • Nagaland’s Zabo system: farming integrated with earthen hill-slope bunds.
  • Check dams (across small streams): slow water, stop erosion, trap sediment, boost groundwater recharge.

Agents of Gradation

Agents of gradation = processes/agents that wear down, carry, and deposit materials → slowly lower highlands + fill lowlands. Chief agents: running water; waves & currents; glaciers; wind; underground water.

Running Water

Rivers do 3 main jobs: erosion + transportation + deposition.
  • Upper course: steep gradient; strong erosion → V-shaped valleys, waterfalls, rapids.
  • Middle course: less energy → meanders, oxbow lakes, floodplains.
  • Lower course: lots of deposition → deltas, levees, alluvial fans.
Waterfall: sudden steep/vertical drop in a river, usually in the upper course. Formation (reasoning): hard rock resists erosion but softer rock below gets worn away → drop forms + a plunge pool. Importance to people: tourism, recreation, photography, cultural/religious uses, sometimes hydroelectric power.
Meander: river bend made by lateral erosion on the outer bank + deposition on the inner bank → loops grow; may cut off → oxbow lakes. Importance to people: fertile banks → farming; gentle nearby slopes → settlements; rivers help navigation, irrigation, tourism. Example of river use: Grand Anicut (Kallanai), Tamil Nadu — river used for irrigation.
Delta: fan/triangular depositional landform at a river mouth where it enters a sea, ocean, or lake. Importance to people:
  • rich alluvium → crops like rice + jute
  • mix of fresh + salt water → good fishing
  • navigable routes → dense settlements, trade + transport
  • also flood-prone
Example: Sundarbans — famous delta + tourist region.

Waves and Currents

Moving ocean water reshapes coasts → forms beaches, sand bars, cliffs, caves, arches, stacks, bays, and wave-cut/shore platforms.

Beaches and Coastal Erosion Landforms

Beach: shoreline deposit of sand/pebbles/rock, built + constantly reshaped by waves, tides, currents.

Importance of Beaches to Humans

  • Support tourism + recreation.
  • Support fishing.
  • Allow shell/sand collection.
  • Boost local economies (jobs, services, trade).
  • Work as a buffer protecting settlements from waves + coastal erosion.

Coastal Erosion Sequence (Cliff–Platform–Cave–Arch–Stack)

Typical wave-erosion landform sequence on rocky coasts — learn the order + how each stage leads to the next.
  1. Undercutting and cliff formation: waves undercut the base → a steep cliff forms.
  2. Wave-cut platform: cliff keeps retreating (erosion + collapse) → leaves a flat wave-cut platform at the base.
  3. Cave formation: weak rock zones get enlarged by erosion → caves.
  4. Arch formation: caves extend and meet through a headland (or along weaknesses) → arch.
  5. Stack formation: arch roof becomes unstable + collapses → isolated pillar = stack.
  • Often attract tourism.
  • Vulnerable coasts may need protection as erosion threatens land, property, infrastructure.
Exam tip: Don’t list landforms randomly—write as a linked process sequence: undercutting → cliff retreat → wave-cut platform → caves → arch → stack.

3.3 Glaciers

Glacial Erosion and Resulting Landforms

Moving glaciers erode valley sides + floors → river valleys become wider + deeper, shape changes.
  • U-shaped valleys: glaciers widen/deepen old river valleys (V-shape → U-shape).
  • Cirques: bowl-shaped hollows carved high on mountainsides at glacier heads.
  • Arêtes: sharp ridges between two glaciated valleys (or between cirques).
  • Hanging valleys: smaller glaciers join a larger glacier; after retreat the smaller valley stays “hanging” above the main valley.
  • Fjords: sea floods deep glacial valleys → long, narrow, steep-sided inlets.

Human Value of Glacial Landscapes

  • Trekking, skiing, mountaineering (tourism + recreation).
  • Fjords → good harbours + rich fishing grounds.
  • Fertile glacial soil can support farming.
  • Glaciers store fresh water and feed downstream rivers.

Moraines (Deposits of Glacial Till)

Moraines: deposits of glacial till (mix of rock + soil + debris) left when glacier ice melts.
  • Lateral moraines: till along the sides of a glacier.
  • Terminal moraine: marks glacier’s furthest advance (end position).
  • Medial moraine: where two glaciers meet; their lateral moraines join → debris line down the centre.

Human Uses/Effects of Moraines

  • Create fertile soil in some areas.
  • Can form natural dams.
  • May trap/create water → lakes used for water supply, irrigation, hydropower.

Case Note: February 2021 Chamoli Flood (Uttarakhand)

February 2021 Chamoli flood caused deaths, livestock loss, and heavy damage to buildings, roads, bridges, hydel projects, and village connectivity. Shows risk of sudden hazards in glaciated mountain regions.

3.4 Wind

Wind as an Erosional and Transporting Agent

Strong winds can lift + carry loose sand/soil → create clear erosion + deposition landforms (esp. in dry regions).

Wind Erosion Landforms

  • Yardangs: streamlined ridges cut by wind erosion; lined up with the prevailing wind.
  • Ventifacts: rocks polished + shaped by wind-blown sand (sandblasting).
  • Deflation hollows / blowouts: dips formed when wind removes loose material.
  • Desert pavement: top layer of coarse pebbles/stones left after finer particles are blown away (= lag deposit).

Effects and Importance of Wind Landforms

  • Affect farming + settlement (fertile topsoil loss, shifting sands, exposure).
  • Attract tourists + geologists (recreation + scientific value).
  • Oasis formation: a deflation hollow may deepen until it hits the water table → water appears at/near the surface → oasis.

Dunes (Wind-Deposited Sand Landforms)

Dunes = wind-built sand hills/ridges in deserts or on sandy coasts.
Dune types mainly depend on: wind-direction pattern + sand supply. Be ready to match each dune to its conditions.
  • Barchans: crescent-shaped; form with one main wind direction + limited sand.
  • Longitudinal dunes: long ridges parallel to the prevailing wind.
  • Star dunes: many arms; form with shifting winds (several directions).
  • Parabolic dunes: U-shaped; often stabilised by vegetation.
  • Transverse dunes: ridges across the wind direction (perpendicular to prevailing wind).

Human Significance of Dunes

  • Can help reduce desertification + wind erosion in some areas.
  • On coasts: can protect some settlements from wind + waves.
  • Support tourism + adventure sports.
  • May provide construction sand.
Common mistake: dune orientation mix-up — longitudinal = parallel to wind; transverse = across (perpendicular) to wind.

3.5 Underground Water (Karst Topography)

Formation of Karst by Underground Water

In limestone + other soluble rocks, slightly acidic groundwater causes chemical weathering/erosion → forms karst topography.

Karst Landforms

  • Caves: dissolved hollow spaces inside soluble rock.
  • Stalactites: mineral deposits that hang from cave roofs.
  • Stalagmites: mineral deposits that grow up from cave floors.
  • Pillars: when stalactites + stalagmites join → one continuous column.
  • Sinkholes / dolines: depressions formed when ground collapses into an underground cavity.
  • Underground rivers: streams flowing through cave systems.

Human Uses/Importance of Karst Areas

  • May supply fresh water (springs + underground flow systems).
  • Support tourism.
  • Enable geological study + adventure (caving).
  • Can have cultural/religious importance.
Exam tip: don’t confuse them — stalactites = ceiling; stalagmites = floor; pillars = when they join.

4. Landforms and Human Civilisation

Physical features strongly shape settlements, livelihoods, war, trade, and cultural growth (past + present).
  • Fertile river plains → farming + early cities (e.g. plains of the Ganga, Nile, Brahmaputra, Indus).
  • Mountains = barriers + protectors:
    • The Himalayas protected India.
    • Passes (e.g. Khyber Pass) allowed cultural exchange.
  • Deserts (e.g. Thar) limited big settlements but encouraged trade routes like the Silk Route.
  • Coasts + harbours boosted trade, travel, cultural contact → helped southern Indian kingdoms flourish.

5. Landform-Associated Disasters

Landslides

Landslide = downslope movement of rock/soil due to loss of slope stability; often linked to water saturation, steep slopes, and trigger events.
  • Main causes/triggers:
    • Water-saturated soil after long rain
    • Earthquakes or eruptions
    • Steep slopes + loose/weathered rock
  • Human activities that destabilise slopes:
    • Deforestation
    • Mining
    • Road cutting
    • Unplanned hillside construction
    • Poor drainage
    • Improper land use

Avalanches

  • Main causes/triggers:
    • Unstable snow on steep slopes
    • Rapid heavy snowfall
    • Weak snow layers inside the snowpack
    • Warming + partial melting
    • Wind-loaded snow (wind adds extra snow onto slopes)
    • Earthquakes + vibration
  • Human disturbance triggers: skiing, trekking, or construction can disturb the snowpack and start sliding.

GLOFs (Glacial Lake Outburst Floods)

GLOF = sudden, destructive flood when a glacial lake drains fast due to failure of its natural dam.
  • Build-up processes:
    • Rapid glacier melt enlarges lakes + increases pressure on ice dams or loose-moraine dams.
    • Heavy rain/snow adds more water.
  • Failure triggers:
    • Earthquakes
    • Avalanches
    • Landslides
    These can weaken the dam or hit it → sudden breach → destructive flood downstream.

Dust Storms

  • Main cause: strong winds lift loose, dry soil + sand.
  • Factors increasing risk/intensity:
    • Drought + low rainfall
    • Desert/semi-arid conditions
    • Sparse vegetation
    • Deforestation
    • Overgrazing
    • Poor farming practices
    • Climate change
    • Extreme weather

What Disaster Study Should Cover (Exam-Oriented)

  • Mark prone areas in India + the world.
  • Add recent examples for each disaster.
  • Write effects on life + the environment.
  • Suggest suitable mitigation, incl.:
    • Hazard mapping
    • Monitoring + early warning
    • Vegetation + slope/watershed management
    • Safe land use + safe construction
    • Protective structures
    • Evacuation planning
    • Public awareness

6. Integrated Summary (How the Chapter Fits Together)

  • Internal (endogenic) forces — earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, folding, faulting → move the crust + build major relief: mountains, valleys, ocean basins.
  • External (exogenic) forces — weathering, erosion, transportation, deposition → wear down + reshape landforms using agents: running water, waves and tides, glaciers, wind, groundwater.
  • Landforms affect climate, resources, occupations, settlements, cultures, and exposure to hazards.
  • Knowing landforms helps with sustainable resource use + disaster preparedness.
⚠ Major related disasters to remember: landslides, avalanches, glacial lake outburst floods, dust/sandstorms.

7. Learning and Application Checklist

Map Work

  • Identify major plates.
  • Identify the continents and oceans they carry.
  • Locate the Ring of Fire.
  • Mark current landform-related disasters.

Diagrams/Models

  • Draw + label a meander and a delta.
  • Model karst/underground-water landforms.

Field Inquiry

  • Observe local landforms → identify the agent that formed them.
  • Assess local erosion → make a protection plan.

Disaster Preparedness

  • Identify hazards likely in your region.
  • Document a past disaster + its effects.
  • Plan mitigation.
  • List earthquake precautions.

Projects (Examples of Applications)

  • Investigate causes + impacts of water-, wind-, and glacier-related processes.
  • Explore the Sundarbans.
  • Investigate the 2021 Chamoli flood.
  • Translate a landslide-awareness poster.
  • Document sacred local landforms + related folk stories.
