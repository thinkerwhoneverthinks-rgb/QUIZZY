Chapter 1: Understanding Social Science — Complete Short Notes

Meaning and Scope of Social Science

Social Science = the systematic study of human society.
  • Grades 6–8: mostly stories about people, places, events. Grade 9: shift to a deeper understanding of Social Science + why it matters.
  • Humans live in societies and depend on one another in many ways.
  • Human life is shaped by many connected factors:
    • Natural environment
    • Institutions of governance (how societies are ruled/managed)
    • Economic activities to meet needs
    • Traditions and ideas from earlier generations
    • Relationships + cooperation among people
Social Science is more than describing events/locating places: it tries to explain what happens and why it happens, and shows how life is interconnected.
  • Social Science explains:
    • Why events occur
    • How people live together
    • How the environment affects human life
    • How governments function
    • How economies operate
    • How past + present shape the world
  • Natural sciences (Physics, Chemistry, Biology, etc.) mainly study the natural world.
  • Social Science focuses on human society, including:
    • Institutions
    • Cultures
    • Interactions among people/groups
Exam tip: Natural Science vs Social Science → write both the focus (natural world vs human society) AND the goal (Social Science explains causes + connections, not just description).

Social Science in Everyday Life

Everyday Life and Social Systems

  • Even an ordinary day shows Social Science links to daily life.
  • Housing: houses use materials from different places (resources + transport + economy link).
  • Food: often comes from different regions of the country.
  • Before food reaches the consumer, it is:
    1. Cultivated
    2. Harvested
    3. Processed
    4. Transported
    5. Bought and sold
  • Roads: planned, built, maintained by public authorities (governance + planning).
  • Schools: shaped by government education policies/programmes.
  • Electricity: generated at distant power stations and supplied through a vast network.
  • Even simple daily activities depend on:
    • Systems of governance
    • Economic production
    • Social cooperation
    • Natural environment
Daily life works because many systems work together—Social Science studies these systems and their interdependence.

Questions Studied in Social Science

Social Science looks for patterns in society and asks why they happen—across people, places, environment, economy, and government.
  • Typical Social Science questions:
    • Why do some people live in crowded cities while others live in scattered villages?
    • Why do communities speak different languages and follow different traditions?
    • Why do some regions depend on agriculture while others specialise in industry/trade?
    • How do governments make decisions affecting millions of people?
    • Why are certain regions more vulnerable to floods?
    • Why does agriculture flourish in some regions but not in others?
    • How does climate change affect human life?

Society as an Interconnected System

  • Society doesn’t run by chance.
  • It is shaped by:
    • History
    • Geography
    • Institutions
    • Resources
    • Human choices
  • Social scientists answer questions using:
    • Careful observation
    • Evidence collection
    • Comparison
    • Logical reasoning
  • Daily experiences + local changes can show links between people, places, and systems.
Core idea: Social Science helps us see society as one connected story, not unrelated bits.

Activity: Analysing Local Change

  • Find a change in your locality in the last 5 years related to:
    • Transport
    • Housing
    • Education
    • Technology
    • Environment
  • Check:
    • Earlier situation
    • What changed
    • Possible causes
    • Effects on people’s lives
  • Group discussion + present as a short report.
Exam writing tip: “Local change” answers = Before → Change → Causes → Effects (matches how social scientists use evidence).

Understanding Society Through Time and Traditions

Evolution of Human Society

  • Society has changed a lot over thousands of years.
  • Early human communities depended directly on nature.
  • Over time, people learnt to:
    • Grow crops
    • Domesticate animals
    • Build settlements
    • Develop tools
    • Exchange goods
    • Establish systems of governance
  • Villages → towns → cities.
  • Inventions, cultural exchanges, and new ideas kept transforming human life.

India’s Knowledge Traditions

  • The spirit of questioning and inquiry runs deep in Indian knowledge traditions.
  • Early Indian thinkers valued:
    • Discussion
    • Questioning
    • Logical reasoning
    • Observation
    • Reflection
    • Dialogue

Pañchamahābhūtas

Pañchamahābhūtas: Indian philosophical idea that the world is made of five fundamental elements.
  • The five elements:
    1. Pṛithvī — earth
    2. Āpaḥ — water
    3. Agni — fire
    4. Vāyu — air
    5. Ākāśha — space
  • Shows nature as an interconnected system where human life is embedded.
  • Used by scholars to explain:
    • Natural processes
    • The human body
    • Relationships between living beings and the environment
  • Helps explain how environment shapes:
    • Settlement patterns
    • Occupations
    • Architecture
    • Food habits
    • Health practices
  • This interconnected view still matters for modern environmental challenges.
Common mistake: Writing the 5 elements but not linking them to the Social Science idea of human–environment interconnection. Add at least one application (settlement/occupation/architecture/food/health).

Vasudhaiva Kuṭumbakam

Vasudhaiva kuṭumbakam = “the world is one family.”
  • Highlights the interconnectedness of:
    • Societies
    • Regions
    • Cultures
  • This interdependence is central in Social Science for studying global relationships and interactions.

Early Indian Thought on Governance

  • Early Indian thinkers linked political authority with:
    • Public welfare
    • Ethical responsibility
    • Sound administration
    • Economic management

Arthaśhāstra

Arthaśhāstra: text attributed to Kauṭilya, written about 2,300 years ago; gives systematic ideas on governance + economy.
  • Examined:
    • Administration
    • Economic management
    • Taxation
    • Duties of rulers
    • Welfare of the people
  • Significance: proves systematic thinking on governance + economy existed long before modern academic disciplines.

Continuity and Change in Society

  • Understanding society through observation, reasoning, and reflection has a long history.
  • Modern Social Science continues this using newer tools and methods.
  • To understand the present, study:
    • How societies evolved
    • How institutions developed
    • How human–environment relationships changed
    • What has continued over time (continuity)
    • What has transformed (change)
Many exam answers need BOTH: (1) continuities (what stays) and (2) changes (what shifts). Social Science explains both.

Methods Used by Social Scientists

Social scientists explain society using evidence from many sources, tested through comparison + reasoning.
  • Social scientists:
    • Observe people’s lives
    • Conduct interviews
    • Carry out surveys
    • Examine documents
    • Compare different places
    • Compare different historical periods
    • Gather evidence from multiple sources
    • Develop explanations supported by evidence
  • Social Science explains:
    • What remains continuous
    • What changes
    • How the past influences the present
    • How present actions will shape the future
Common mistake: Listing methods (survey/interview) but not the purpose: building evidence-based explanations about continuity, change, and past–present–future links.

Social Science as a Group of Disciplines

Need for Different Disciplines

  • Human society is too complex to understand using only one field.
  • Example: a drought hits many dimensions:
    • Crops — environmental dimension
    • Farmers’ income — economic dimension
    • Government relief measures — political dimension
    • Migration to cities — social dimension
    • Traditional methods of managing scarcity — cultural dimension
  • So Social Science is not one subject; it’s a group of related disciplines studying different sides of human life.
  • Together they give a comprehensive, interconnected view of society.
Use the drought example to show interdisciplinarity: environment + economy + politics + society + culture.

Major Disciplines of Social Science

  • In Grades 9–10, Social Science mainly includes:
    1. Geography
    2. History
    3. Political Science
    4. Economics
  • Other disciplines in the wider Social Science family:
    • Sociology
    • Philosophy
    • Anthropology
    • Psychology
  • These go deeper into social relationships, human behaviour, and ethical reasoning.
  • Each discipline asks different questions, but the 4 core ones are interconnected and together give a holistic picture of society.

Basic Focus of the Four Disciplines

The 4 core disciplines differ in main focus, but link up to make a full picture of society.
  • Geography: Earth, environments, and people–surroundings relationships.
  • History: Human past + how societies change over time.
  • Political Science: Governance, power, rights, responsibilities.
  • Economics: Production, distribution, and use of resources to meet needs.
Exam tip: When defining a discipline, write both its focus (what it studies) and its key concerns (e.g., Political Science: power + rights + responsibilities).

Geography

Meaning and Scope of Geography

Geography studies the location and distribution of Earth’s features, and the relationship between people and their surroundings.
  • Geography looks at the location + distribution of:
    • Places
    • Objects
    • Materials

Geography: People and Human–Environment Relationships

Geography = studies the link between human societies and their surroundings. It looks at both:
  • Physical features of the Earth
  • Human communities living on it
Geography asks:
  • Where things are
  • Why they are there
  • How people interact with nature
  • How places influence human life
  • How nearby + distant places affect each other over time

Geography as an Interdisciplinary Subject

Geography sees the world as a system of interdependencies (everything is connected + influences other things).
Two core perspectives:
  • Spatial perspective → focus on location + the importance of where something is.
  • Temporal perspective → focus on change over time.
Geography borrows ideas from:
  • Natural sciences: Physics, Chemistry, Biology
  • Social sciences: Political Science, Economics, History
Illustrative explanations geography helps with (know the logic):
  • Why India historically became a centre of global interaction → explained using location, routes, and connections across regions.
  • How the Indian peninsula’s long coastline encouraged contact with Africa, Europe and Southeast Asia → coastlines support ports, sea routes, trade, migration + cultural exchange over time.

Tools of Geography

Geographers use tools to understand concepts + answer spatial questions (where things are + patterns of location).
  • Maps
  • Globes
  • Atlases
  • Geographical Information System (GIS)
  • Infographics
  • Other geographical instruments
Exam tip: Don’t just list tools—add the purpose: they help analyse location, distribution, and spatial relationships.

School Bhuvan Portal

  • NCERT’s School Bhuvan portal lets students explore + map their village or city.
  • Shows educational use of digital maps + geospatial technology.

Geography Topics in Grades 9–10

Students will study:
  • Processes shaping the Earth’s surface
  • Formation of landforms
  • Atmosphere and climate
  • Oceans
  • Major biomes
  • India’s biosphere reserves
  • Geospatial technologies
  • Life in different regions of India and the world
  • Geographical and socio-economic perspectives on selected issues
  • Historical development of geographical knowledge

History: Meaning and Purpose

History = study of the human past.
History helps societies understand:
  • Human experiences
  • Values
  • Cultural traditions
  • Changes over time
  • The past can be interpreted in different ways.
  • Societies use different methods to pass cultural values from one generation to the next.

Itihāsa-Purāṇa Tradition

Itihāsa-purāṇa tradition = one of Bharat’s oldest + most influential ways of preserving cultural memory.
Through stories, it:
  • Communicates historical information
  • Gives cultural meaning to events and personalities
  • Reinforces long-lasting ideals and values
  • Gives identity and purpose
Comparative point (exam framing): cultures build historical understanding differently:
  • Some focus on moral + philosophical insights.
  • Others stress documentary verification (checking records/evidence).

Modern Historiography

Modern historiography (modern ways of writing/studying history) increasingly depends on empirical evidence.
Scientific methods/tools include:
  • Human genetics
  • Carbon-14 dating
  • Archaeology
  • Laboratory testing
  • Scientific instruments
Purpose: helps build timelines and strengthen understanding of the past.

Empirical Evidence

Empirical evidence = information gained through real observation or experimentation.
Common mistake: mixing myths/assumptions with empirical proof. Empirical = observed / measured / tested.

Sources of History

History is rebuilt using different categories of evidence → called sources.

A. Literary Sources

Literary sources = written or spoken (recorded) evidence about the past, like:
  • Manuscripts
  • Travelogues
  • Memoirs
  • Correspondence
  • Revenue documents
  • Folklore
  • Oral traditions
  • Genealogical records
Chapter examples (learn these as literary sources):
  • Sāmaveda manuscript preserved at the National Museum, New Delhi
  • Traditional palm-leaf manuscript of the Tirukkuṟal (ancient Tamil work of ethical wisdom)

Genealogical Records

Genealogical records = track family lineage + ancestry.
They record:
  • Relationships across generations
  • Events like births, marriages, deaths

B. Archaeological Sources

Archaeological sources = material remains used to study the past.
They include:
  • Monuments
  • Architectural structures
  • Excavated sites
  • Artefacts
  • Everyday objects
  • Sculptures
  • Paintings
Study/analysis may use scientific instruments + laboratory testing.
Chapter examples (learn these as archaeological examples):
  • Terracotta figurine from the Sindhu-Sarasvatī Civilisation
  • 12th-century CE sculpture of Viṣhṇu

C. Epigraphic Sources

Epigraphic sources = texts/decrees/records inscribed on long-lasting materials like:
  • Stone
  • Metal plates
  • Rock surfaces
Inscriptions give direct info about:
  • Historical events
  • Rulers
  • Administration
  • Society
Chapter examples:
  • Brahmi inscription on part of a Gupta-period pillar
  • Kannada inscription of Emperor Krishnadeva Raya at the underground Shiva / Prasanna Virupaksha Temple in Hampi

D. Numismatic Sources

Numismatic sources = coins, currency, medals. Their study is numismatics.
Numismatics helps study:
  • Economy
  • Chronology (dating/time order)
  • Rulers
  • Trade
  • Political boundaries
  • Culture
Info comes from coins’:
  • Metal content
  • Inscriptions
  • Symbols
Chapter examples:
  • Coin issued by King Samudragupta in the fourth century CE
  • Mughal coin from Jahangir’s reign, showing the zodiac sign Sagittarius

History Topics in Grades 9–10

Students will:
  • Study early human history
  • Trace developments in India from the start of civilisation to the present
  • Examine key developments in world history, incl.:
    • Greco-Roman civilisation
    • Reformation
    • Renaissance
    • Enlightenment
    • Industrial Revolution
  • Study colonialism + its global consequences
  • Examine anti-colonial struggles across the world
  • Understand histories of diverse peoples + cultures
  • Explore events/processes that shaped today’s world

Political Science: Meaning and Scope

Political Science = study of governance.
It explains:
  • How + why power is distributed
  • How decisions are made
  • How policies are implemented
  • How power is exercised, shared, and regulated
It studies:
  • Constitutions
  • Governments
  • Institutions of the State
  • Social movements
  • Nation-building
  • Foreign policy
  • Power in society + everyday life

Power Beyond Formal Institutions

Political power isn’t only in government institutions — it also works through society.
Political power is also found in:
  • Social relationships
  • Customs
  • Hierarchies
  • Accepted ideas of legitimacy
  • Struggles for accountable governance
So, studying politics also means studying society + its power relationships.

Panchayati Raj

Panchayati Raj = grassroots democracy in Indian villages.
  • Gives citizens a voice in local development + planning
  • Promotes:
    • Local self-government
    • Public participation
    • Accountable decision-making

Indian Traditions of Political Thought

Political thought grew early in India, alongside debates on organisation + governance of society.
Politics was linked with:
  • Dharma = moral duty
  • Artha = economic well-being
  • Rājadharma = duties of a ruler
Texts with political ideas include:
  • Vedas, Upaniṣads, Purāṇas: justice, authority, social order, responsibilities of rulers + citizens
  • Mahābhārata and Śhukranīti: governance, law, ethical leadership

Political Importance of the Arthaśhāstra

The Arthaśhāstra explains:
  • How a State should be administered
  • How taxes should be collected
  • How the army should function
  • How rulers should ensure public welfare

Holistic Nature of Indian Political Thought

Indian political traditions were holistic → politics connected to many parts of life, not kept separate.
Politics was connected with:
  • Economics
  • Social life
  • Morality
  • Defence
  • Public welfare
Power was seen as a responsibility, not just a privilege.
Continuity today: these ideas still influence democratic institutions like Panchayati Raj.

Political Science Topics in Grades 9–10

Students will study:
  • Democracy
  • Elections
  • Authority
  • Civil society
  • Governance and public policy
  • National security and its challenges

Economics

Meaning and Scope of Economics

Economics = study of how people + society use limited resources to satisfy needs.
Economics looks at how goods + services are:
  • Produced
  • Exchanged
  • Distributed
  • Consumed
These processes shape daily life, like:
  • Food
  • Clothing
  • Employment
  • Markets
  • Public and private services
  • Everyday standards of living
Exam-style definition: mention limited resources + satisfying needs, then list the 4 processes: production, exchange, distribution, consumption.

Economic Decision-Making

Different groups take different economic decisions:
  • Consumers → decide what to buy.
  • Producers → decide what to make + how much to make.
  • Governments → make policies + balance goals like:
    • Growth vs stability
    • Efficiency vs fairness
Economics isn’t just numbers/markets: it also deals with human well-being, equity, fairness, justice.
Common exam mistake: Writing “economics = money/markets only.” Always add: well-being, equity, fairness, justice.

India’s Economic History

For centuries, India was:
  • One of the world’s leading economies
  • An important trading centre
  • A centre of industry
  • Active in maritime commerce
Colonial rule interrupted development and led to:
  • Widespread poverty
  • Recurrent famines
  • Decline of traditional industries
After independence, India began rebuilding its economy.
Best answer flow: pre-colonial strength → colonial disruption + outcomes → post-independence rebuilding.

India’s Economic Progress and Challenges

Recent progress includes:
  • Improved infrastructure
  • Expansion of education
  • Technological development
  • Reduction in poverty
  • Increase in life expectancy
But challenges still include:
  • Raising people’s incomes
  • Ensuring economic growth benefits every section of society
  • Reducing inequality
  • Using resources wisely
  • Achieving fair and sustainable development
Exam tip: For “progress + challenges,” write at least 2 progress + 2 challenges. Use the words inequality and benefits every section.

Sustainable Economic Development

Economic development is still a key national goal. Economics helps us judge:
  • Economic achievements
  • Continuing challenges
Resources must be used wisely + fairly.
Sustainable growth = meeting current needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
Must-memorise phrase: meet present needs without weakening future generations’ ability to meet their needs.

Students study:

  • Entrepreneurship

These topics show how economic decisions affect:
  • Individuals

Why Should We Study Social Science?

Understanding Social Systems

Daily life runs on social systems made/managed by society, e.g.:
  • Houses
  • Water supply
  • Roads
  • Schools
  • Markets
  • Digital spaces
Social Science explains:
  • How these systems developed
  • How they function
  • How they affect different groups
In answers on “how Social Science helps,” connect to systems + include: development, functioning, impact on different groups.

Understanding Diversity and Unity

India shows diversity in:
  • Languages
  • Customs
  • Occupations
These differences come from:
  • Geographical conditions
  • Historical developments
  • Cultural traditions
  • Economic circumstances
Knowing diversity builds:
  • Mutual respect
  • Cooperation
  • Social harmony
Social Science also shows how India stays united through:
  • An underlying cultural unity
  • A shared identity
  • A sense of belonging
Exam tip: For “diversity + unity,” always write both: (1) sources of variation and (2) forces of unity (cultural unity, shared identity, belonging).

Responsible Citizenship

Citizens have an active role in a democracy. Laws, rights, and responsibilities guide how people live together. Knowing government + public decision-making helps people participate responsibly in civic life.
Citizenship (Social Science) = understanding laws, rights, responsibilities, and public decision-making for responsible participation.

Addressing Shared Challenges

Social Science helps us study issues like:
  • Environmental protection
  • Public health
  • Employment
  • Urban growth
It trains us to ask informed questions about:
  • Causes
  • Consequences
  • Different perspectives
  • Possible solutions
To solve social problems: use careful questioning + evidence + reasoning.

Connecting Past, Present and Future

Social Science shows how past decisions created today’s conditions. This helps people choose better for the future. It links historical experience, present life, and future possibilities.
Common exam mistake: Treating subjects as separate. Social Science connects across time: past → present → future.

The Future of Social Science

Importance in a Changing World

Social Science is becoming more important because society is changing due to:
  • New technologies
  • Expanding cities
  • Environmental concerns
  • Migration
  • Global connections
To understand these changes we need:
  • Careful observation
  • Questioning
  • Examination of evidence
  • Recognition of connections

Future Challenges

Social Science will help address:
  • Climate change
  • Sustainable development
  • Social harmony
  • Equitable distribution of resources
  • Responsible use of technology
  • Community adaptation to changing conditions

Creating Informed Citizens

Social Science prepares people not only to understand the world, but also to shape it. It builds informed, thoughtful citizens. It strengthens national unity while keeping people rooted in India’s cultural traditions. Studying Social Science is a journey:
  1. Understanding how societies evolved
  2. Analysing how they function today
  3. Imagining how they can improve in the future
Exam framework: the “3-step journey” → evolved → function today → improve in future.

Social Science as a Window to the World

Social Science ≠ just a school subject. It opens up the world around us. It explains past + present, and helps us see our responsibility in shaping the future.

Areas of Study

In Grades 9–10, students examine:
  • How historical events shaped the modern world

  • How political systems and democratic institutions function

  • Social diversity
  • Citizens’ rights and responsibilities

Skills Developed

Students learn to:
  • Analyse evidence
  • Compare perspectives
  • Identify relationships among disciplines
  • Connect the past, present and future
  • Think independently
  • Act responsibly
  • View society with curiosity and empathy
These skills help students become:
  • Thoughtful learners
