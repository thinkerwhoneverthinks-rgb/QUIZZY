Chapter 6: Democracy

The Big Questions (Scope of the Chapter)

  • How did democracy evolve and shape its present meaning?
  • What forms of democracy exist, and how are they practised in India and elsewhere?
  • What features are essential to democracy, including beyond government institutions?
  • What challenges does Indian democracy face?

Meaning and Foundations of Democracy

Democracy = system where citizens are the real source of power/authority and they elect representatives via free + fair elections.
  • Democracy stands on freedom, equality, justice + rights and duties.
  • In a democracy, citizens:
    • Vote to choose reps,
    • Express opinions, and
    • Participate in decisions that affect them.
India as a democracy: India is the world’s largest participatory democracy—population 140+ crore and 96.8+ crore registered voters (2024). Citizens elect the government, so India is a global example (though challenges remain).
Constitution = protects people’s rights + sets up the framework for democratic governance.
  • India adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949.
  • It came into force on 26 January 1950.
  • The Constitution:
    • Guarantees + protects Fundamental Rights,
    • Bans discrimination on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth,
    • Encourages participation via national, state, local elections.
Indian democratic ethos grew through a long historical journey. This chapter looks at origins, meaning, features, practice, and global forms of democracy.

Democratic Traditions from Early Times

Early Indian assemblies and consultative governance

  • Vedic assemblies—Sabhā, Samiti, Vidhata—worked through collective decision-making.
  • Early republican gaṇas/saṁghas also ran in a consultative way.
  • Even with kings, rule often happened with assemblies, ministers, officers (not solo rule).

Ṛig Veda 10.191.3 and the idea of consensus

Ṛig Veda 10.191.3 (from Aikyamatya Sūktam) urges common counsel, common assembly, common mind, common resolve.
  • Shows democratic ideas like:
    • Consultation
    • Consensus
    • Unity of purpose
    • Shared responsibility

Bauddha Saṁghas (Buddhist monastic communities)

  • Bauddha Saṁghas = monastic communities started by Gautama Buddha.
  • They promoted debate + discussion.
  • Members chose leaders and voted on decisions.

Disruptions and the return of democratic ideas

  • Foreign invasions disturbed parts of India’s political-social structure.
  • By the 19th century, British colonisation reshaped institutions and reduced participation.
  • The freedom movement brought back and strengthened democratic thinking.

Constituent Assembly and Constitution-making

  • Constituent Assembly formed in 1946 (pre-Independence).
  • It wrote the world’s longest written Constitution in 2 years, 11 months, 18 days.
  • Constituent Assembly Debates (CAD) drew from:
    • Indigenous traditions, and
    • the global spread of democracy.
  • Result: a Constitution seen as robust, flexible, transformative, responsive.

Constitution as a dynamic document: Article 368

The Assembly saw the Constitution as dynamic (not rigid).
Article 368 allows lawful amendments while protecting core values—so the Constitution can respond to changing socio-political needs.

Building democratic institutions and Dr B.R. Ambedkar

  • Democratic institutions were created to protect:
    • Liberty
    • Equality
    • Justice
    • Fraternity
  • Dr B.R. Ambedkar = Chairperson of the Drafting Committee.

Essential Principles of Democracy

Popular Sovereignty and Universal Adult Franchise

Popular sovereignty = the ultimate power of the state comes from the people.
  • Citizens choose the government through free + fair elections.
  • The government then frames policies + implements laws.
Universal Adult Franchise: every citizen aged 18+ can vote by secret ballot.
  • Voting:
    • chooses representatives, and
    • strengthens popular sovereignty by making govt answerable to people.

Rule of Law

Rule of law = everyone is equal before the law + gets equal protection of the law; no one is above the law.
  • Disputes are settled in courts using legally established procedure (not force/influence).
  • No punishment without due process.
  • Everyone gets a fair chance to defend against arbitrary action.
Rule of law stops misuse of power and supports justice, rights, accountability + trust.
Equality before law: no matter rank/status/identity → treated equally (no special privilege). Equal protection of law: people in similar circumstances get the same legal treatment (no discrimination). Procedure established by law: procedure laid down by state law/statute.
Exam trap: Don’t mix them up—equality before law = no special privilege; equal protection = similar cases treated similarly (no discrimination).

Fundamental Rights

Fundamental Rights protect citizens’ freedom, dignity + rights. If violated → citizens can take legal action.
List of Fundamental Rights + Articles (India):
  • Right to Equality — Articles 14–18
  • Right to Freedom — Articles 19–22
  • Right Against Exploitation — Articles 23–24
  • Right to Freedom of Religion — Articles 25–28
  • Cultural and Educational Rights — Articles 29–30
  • Right to Constitutional Remedies — Article 32
  • Citizens can seek constitutional remedies under:
    • Article 32, and
    • Article 226.
  • Rights are:
    • Enforceable (courts can enforce),
    • Indivisible (linked; can’t be meaningfully separated),
    • Inalienable (can’t be taken away arbitrarily),
    • subject to reasonable restrictions via lawful procedure.
Article 21A (added 2009) = free + compulsory education for children aged 6–14.
  • Constitution also includes:
    • Fundamental Duties, and
    • Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP).
  • Chapter expectation: be able to compare their significance + differences with Fundamental Rights (Rights = enforceable; Duties/DPSP = guide behaviour + governance).

Separation of Powers and Independent Judiciary

Separation of powers = govt split into:
  • Legislature — makes laws
  • Executive — implements laws
  • Judiciary — interprets laws
  • Separate roles stop concentration of power + create checks and balances.
  • Parliament can amend the Constitution; courts review amendments to see if they fit its spirit.
  • Executive/enforcement agencies work under parliamentary laws and are open to judicial review.
  • Courts can strike down/invalidate laws against the Constitution; ensure Union + State laws follow it.
An impartial, independent judiciary protects rights + democratic values.
Public Interest Litigation (PIL) = case filed to enforce public interest; increases access to justice.

Accountability and Transparency

Accountability: govt must answer to citizens. Transparency: govt work is open to checking via information + oversight.
  • Govt is answerable through:
    • Elections
    • Public debate
    • Civil-society engagement
  • Continuous participation builds trust.
RTI Act, 2005: citizens can ask info from govt agencies → boosts transparency + accountability.
Question Hour: the first hour of a Parliament session where members question the govt to check its work.
  • Government account audits + RTI are key tools for accountability + transparency.

Multi-Party System

Multi-party system: many parties contest elections + represent different socio-cultural, political, ideological + regional interests.
  • Each party follows certain ideas, values + principles.
  • In Parliament/State Assembly elections:
    • Party/coalition with > 50% seats forms the govt.
    • Parties with fewer seats become the opposition.
  • Gives more choice + matches India’s diversity.
Parties work under the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

Protection of Vulnerable Groups

Democracy means govt must protect all communities (irrespective of caste, gender, religion, region) + fix historically unequal opportunities/resources.
Article 46: special care for educational + economic interests of weaker sections (esp. SC + ST) + protection from social injustice and exploitation.
Exam focus: “Democracy’s real strength” isn’t only institutions—it's informed, honest, fair, responsible citizens who know rights + duties and participate actively.

Processes, Institutions and Democratic Values

Be ready to link each process → key institution(s) → democratic value(s) it strengthens:
  • Legislative process — Institutions: Parliament, State Legislatures, local bodies; Values: representation, deliberation, dissent
  • Electoral process — Institution: Election Commission of India; Values: participation, equality
  • Judicial process — Institutions: courts; Values: rule of law, equality, justice
  • Participatory process — Institutions: media and civil society; Values: debate/discussion, freedom of expression
  • Accountability process — Institutions: CAG, CIC, Lokpal, CVC; Value: transparency
  • Decentralisation — Institutions: rural and urban local bodies; Value: grassroots participation
  • Checks and balances — Institution/mechanism: written Constitution dividing Centre–State powers; Value/outcome: devolution of power

Civil society

Civil society = voluntary groups, NGOs, community associations working interdependently in society.

Media: The Fourth Pillar

Media (newspapers, news channels, social-media platforms) is key in modern life because it:
  • informs people about world developments
  • voices public concerns
  • raises issues
  • pushes issues towards resolution through proper mechanisms
Since it safeguards people’s voices + democratic principles, media is called the “fourth pillar of democracy”.

Types of Democracy

Democracy can work through different institutions. Main types: Direct, Representative/Indirect, Parliamentary, Presidential.

Direct Democracy

  • Core idea: Citizens take part directly in most decisions.
  • Note: Can still keep some representative features.
  • Limitation: Hard to practise in large countries.
  • Example: Switzerland.

Representative/Indirect Democracy

  • Core idea: People elect representatives (don’t govern directly).
  • Mechanism: Periodic elections.
  • Accountability: Government is accountable to the people.
  • Example: India.

Parliamentary Democracy

  • Core idea: Executive members are also part of the legislature.
  • Accountability: Executive is accountable to the legislature.
  • Election link: Voters elect the legislature (not the executive directly).
  • Examples: India, Canada.

Presidential Democracy

  • Core idea: Executive is independent of the legislature.
  • Election link: People elect the President.
  • Accountability: President is accountable to the people.
  • Example: USA.
Common democratic values (seen across democracies):
  • freedom
  • equality
  • participation
  • rule of law
  • rights and duties
Key exam point: There is no single institutional model of democracy. Forms differ based on:
  • citizen participation
  • power-sharing
  • political structure

Youth for Democracy: Four Team Models (Illustrations of Democratic Forms)

These examples show how systems change participation, power-sharing, and checks and balances.
  • Team A (mainly direct democracy): Students elect a weak president yearly; big issues (e.g., canteen menu) decided by direct ballot in a common meeting.
  • Team B (representative/parliamentary pattern): Each class elects a representative; reps help elect the Council President and decide together.
  • Team C (presidential pattern): Students directly elect President + Council; President is council head and chief decision-maker.
  • Team D (democratic appearance + authoritarian limitations): Elections happen but only approved candidates can contest; President dominates; Council is weak; opposition is discouraged.
Exam skill: If asked to pick/justify the “better” model, judge using 3 lenses:
  • Participation: How many people can meaningfully influence decisions?
  • Power-sharing: Is power spread out or concentrated?
  • Checks and balances: Are there ways/institutions to limit misuse of power?

Democracies Around the World

Comparative Examples (India, Canada, UK, Switzerland, USA)

  • India: Representative–parliamentary; executive = Council of Ministers led by PM; President = Head of State; PM = Head of Government; multi-party; written Constitution; Fundamental Rights/Duties; federalism.
  • Canada: Representative–parliamentary; PM leads executive; Governor-General appointed by UK Crown; PM heads government; federal; multi-party.
  • United Kingdom: Parliamentary constitutional monarchy; PM uses executive power; monarch = Head of State; unwritten constitution; parliamentary sovereignty; multi-party.
  • Switzerland: Direct democracy; Federal Council; President heads government; written constitution; multiple parties.
  • USA: Representative–presidential; President uses executive power and heads government; written constitution; two major parties.
Exam takeaway: Democracies differ by:
  • system type (direct/representative; parliamentary/presidential)
  • executive arrangement (PM vs President vs monarch/Head of State)
  • constitutional form (written vs unwritten)
  • party system (two-party vs multi-party)

Federalism

Federalism = power + responsibilities shared between central and state governments. India: This sharing happens through:
  • Union List
  • State List
  • Concurrent List
Residuary powers (not in any list) stay with the Centre.

Sovereignty

Sovereignty (Latin: superanus = “supreme”) = the State’s final, supreme, and independent legal authority over its territory + citizens.
Don’t confuse:
  • Federalism = sharing power inside a country (between levels of government).
  • Sovereignty = ultimate legal authority of the state (supreme + independent).

India’s Living Democracy

Scale, Diversity and Inclusion

  • Scale: 96.8 crore+ registered voters in 2024 (bigger than the population of many countries).
  • Representation size: In 2019, 1 MP ≈ 25 lakh people (average).
  • Linguistic inclusion: Elections run in 22 scheduled languages + others.
  • Accessibility: Symbols help non-literate citizens vote independently.
  • Administrative reach: 10 lakh+ polling stations across mountains/forests/deserts/islands; sometimes one booth = one voter.
  • Political diversity: 2,800+ registered political parties → reflects social/regional diversity + peaceful competition.
  • Institutional strength: Regular nationwide elections for a massive electorate = high organisational capacity.
Meaning: India is the largest democracy not just in numbers, but in sustained citizen involvement.
Living democracy = people from different linguistic, religious, and socio-economic backgrounds included in one system. Ballots carry regional/community voices into one national exercise.

Participatory Democracy and Three-Tier Government

  • Participation beyond elections: People join policy + governance via local councils, public consultations, community initiatives.
  • Three-tier structure in India: Union + State + Local governments → better administration + participation.
  • Autonomous District Councils: In specified NE areas, they have legislative + judicial powers to protect tribal customs.
  • PESA Act, 1996: For Scheduled/tribal areas in other states; makes Gram Sabha the main decision-making body.
  • Constitutional balance: National unity + traditions of diverse communities together.
Exam phrasing: “Participatory democracy” ≠ only voting. It means continuous involvement in governance (local bodies, consultations, initiatives).

Grassroots Democracy: Case Studies

Jethipura Gram Panchayat (Idar, Sabarkantha, Gujarat)

  • Won Nanaji Deshmukh Rashtriya Gaurav Gram Sabha Puraskar in 2019.
  • Ran strong Gram Sabhas, kept records, and held Special Gram Sabhas.
  • Acted on time for education, sanitation, and welfare of women & children.
  • Held Mahila Sabhas.
  • Ensured quorum + active participation by SCs, STs, and women.
Quorum = minimum attendance required for valid proceedings.

South Manubankul Gram Panchayat (Tripura)

  • In Rupaichari Block, under Tripura Tribal Areas Autonomous District Council.
  • Recognised nationally by Ministry of Panchayati Raj as women-friendly.
  • Boosts women’s role in decisions + improves health, sanitation, livelihood facilities.
  • Runs community programmes, supports self-help groups, implements schemes, improves cleanliness, education, social awareness.
Meaning of grassroots democracy: These cases prove democracy is more than voting. Grassroots participation makes democracy:
  • responsive
  • fair
  • inclusive
  • effective
It also:
  • limits concentrated power
  • helps resolve disagreement peacefully
Democratic methods also work inside civil-society bodies, socio-religious/community bodies, cooperatives, and trade unions.
Cooperatives = groups formed for common benefit, with shared profits + responsibilities.

Women and Democratic Representation

  • Historical expansion (West): Full voting rights came late—Britain (1928), USA (1920)—after long protest movements → democracy expanded gradually there.
  • India: Universal Adult Franchise given equally to men + women when Constitution began in 1950; no prolonged legal struggle needed for voting rights.
  • Limits of voting rights: Voting alone ≠ equal participation; prejudice still limits women’s political representation.

Constitutional Provisions for Women’s Reservation in Local Bodies

  • Article 243(d): Minimum 1/3 of directly elected Panchayat seats reserved for women.
    • Extension: 21 States + 2 UTs give 50% reservation via Panchayati Raj laws.
  • Article 243(t): Minimum 1/3 of directly elected municipal seats reserved for women.
    • As of 2023: 17 States + 1 UT give 50% reservation in Urban Local Bodies.
Impact: More representation → stronger women’s empowerment + inclusion in decision-making.
Exam trap: Don’t mix the Articles:
  • 243(d) = Panchayats (rural local bodies)
  • 243(t) = Municipalities (urban local bodies)

RTI, Civil Society and Responsive Democracy

Legislation

Legislation = laws made by the government to maintain order + regulate society.

Why RTI was demanded: transparency concerns

  • Citizens, journalists, civil-society groups, and representatives raised concerns about transparency.
  • These concerns pushed committees and institutions to think about access to official information (i.e., should people have a right to know what government records contain?).

How the RTI Act was made (process and safeguards)

  • Experts reviewed the RTI proposal.
  • Parliament debated it.
  • Stakeholders were consulted (affected groups/interested parties gave views).
  • Representatives added safeguards to balance transparency with:
    • National security (some sensitive info can’t be freely disclosed).
    • Administrative efficiency (rules shouldn’t paralyse routine govt work).

RTI Act, 2005: impact on accountability

Passed by Parliament, the RTI Act came into effect in 2005. It empowered citizens to get government information and strengthened accountability (govt can be questioned using official records).

Civil society and community institutions: bridge between citizen and State

Civil society = voluntary groups, NGOs, community associations working independently of the government.
  • Civil society + community institutions act as a bridge between citizen and State.
  • They do this through:
    • PILs (Public Interest Litigations)
    • Campaigns
    • Organisations
  • Result: more inclusion + a more responsive democracy (govt listens and reacts to people’s needs).

Grassroots leadership and representation of marginalised groups

  • Grassroots leaders can rise from ordinary/rural backgrounds and represent people often kept out of power.
  • Examples mentioned:
    • Chaudhary Charan Singh — from a rural background; spoke for the rural poor.
    • Babu Jagjivan Ram — from a rural background; spoke for marginalised groups.
  • Core idea: democracy gets stronger when leadership includes and speaks for the rural poor and marginalised communities.

Challenges to Democratic Practice

Democracy requires continuous care

Democracy needs constant care by institutions + citizens. It shows not only in elections, but also in conduct, decisions, and civic responsibility.
  • Rights must be used responsibly (misuse harms others + weakens democratic culture).

Actions that weaken democracy

  • Damaging public property
  • Spreading misinformation
  • Violating rules
  • Indifference to public issues
Exam focus: don’t write “democracy = voting only.” Connect democracy to everyday civic behaviour + responsible use of rights.

Major challenges (list and effect)

Major challenges in the text that block equal participation: illiteracy, misinformation/fake news, inequality, poverty, regionalism, gender inequality, and social discrimination.
  • These reduce equal participation by limiting awareness, opportunity, resources, dignity, and voice.

Fake news and weak implementation: how they harm democracy

  • Fake news via social/digital media can:
    • Distort public opinion
    • Create confusion
    • Provoke conflict
  • Gaps in implementing laws/policies reduce public trust (people doubt fair/effective application of rules).

The Emergency (1975–77)

Background causes

  • Early 1970s problems in India:
    • Unemployment
    • Inflation
    • Allegations of misgovernance
    • Public dissatisfaction
  • This led to widespread protests against Indira Gandhi’s government.

Declaration and effects on rights and institutions

National Emergency declared in June 1975 citing internal disturbance. During it, most Fundamental Rights were suspended, the press was censored, and leaders/activists were arrested—liberty restricted + institutions strained.

Role of Jayaprakash Narayan

Jayaprakash Narayan (Lok Nayak) = political leader + socialist thinker who mobilised students and citizens, especially in Bihar and Gujarat.

End of Emergency and democratic significance

  • Emergency ended in 1977.
  • General elections gave people a chance to show their will.
  • Ruling government’s defeat showed democracy’s strength.
  • It showed both:
    • Vulnerability (rights/institutions can be curtailed)
    • Resilience (people + constitutional processes can restore democracy)
  • It highlighted the importance of:
    • Constitutional safeguards
    • Civil liberties
    • Active citizenship

Emergency-related Constitutional Articles

Article 352 → National Emergency Article 356 → President’s Rule Article 360 → Financial Emergency
Common exam slip: mixing article numbers. Fix it as 352–356–360 in order: National, President’s Rule, Financial.

Civic responsibility in daily life

Civic responsibility = responsible behaviour that supports rights, laws, diversity + unity.
  • Includes:
    • Respecting others’ rights
    • Obeying laws and rules
    • Appreciating diversity
    • Using social media responsibly
    • Joining activities that strengthen unity

Think–Discuss–Act (applying democracy)

  • Think: reflect—how does your behaviour support democracy in school + society?
  • Discuss: talk about one important social/community issue + how students can help.

Conclusion and Chapter Recap

Meaning of democracy beyond elections

Democracy = continuous self-governance through institutions, rules + practices at local, state, national levels—not just elections.

Core democratic processes and values

Democratic processes need: transparency, participation, inclusion, fairness, equality, accountability, + respect for different opinions.

Historical roots shaping modern democracy

  • Indian traditions like consultation in ancient Sabhā and Samiti contributed ideas of deliberation/consultation.
  • These mixed with global developments to shape modern democracy.

India’s constitutional foundations of democracy

  • India’s Constitution establishes:
    • Popular sovereignty
    • Universal adult franchise
    • Rights and duties
    • Rule of law
    • Separation of powers

India and world democracies: scale, variety, and common principles

  • India is the largest democracy by scale of voter participation.
  • But deeper inclusion + representation are still challenges.
  • Democracies worldwide have different structures, but share core principles.

What makes democracy endure

Democracy lasts because of strong institutions + informed, responsible citizens: civic awareness, responsible media use, community participation, and daily respect for democratic values.
