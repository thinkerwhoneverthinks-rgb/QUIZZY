Chapter 3: Atmosphere and Climate — Complete Short Notes

The Atmosphere

Atmosphere = blanket of air around Earth. Stays because of gravity. It’s a mixture of gases (different proportions) + essential for life.

Functions of the atmosphere

  • Protects Earth from harmful solar radiation (incl. ultraviolet/UV).
  • Regulates temperature by trapping some of the Sun’s energy.
  • Stops all heat escaping into space (reduces heat loss).
  • Creates weather + climate.
  • Affects temperature, humidity + air pressure.

Gravity

Gravity = force of attraction between objects with mass/energy. Earth’s gravity = force pulling objects on/near Earth’s surface towards Earth.
Without gravity, the atmosphere would not stay around Earth.

Importance of the atmosphere (what happens without it)

  • No air to breathe for living things.
  • Extreme temperatures on Earth.
  • Harmful solar radiation would reach the surface.
  • No clouds, rainfall, winds (no weather phenomena).
  • Most life would not survive.

Composition of the Atmosphere

Major gases and approximate proportions

  • Nitrogen (N₂): 78%
  • Oxygen (O₂): 21%
  • Argon (Ar): 0.93%
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂): 0.04%
  • Other gases: 0.03%

Trace gases (present in very small quantities)

  • Helium
  • Neon
  • Krypton
  • Xenon
  • Ozone
  • Hydrogen

Other components in the atmosphere

  • Water vapour
  • Tiny dust particles

Water vapour

  • Usually about 0.1% to 0.4% of the atmosphere.
  • Key for:
    • Cloud formation
    • Precipitation
    • Humidity
    • Weather conditions
The atmosphere’s composition changes with altitude.

Importance of nitrogen

  • Nitrogen is essential for plant growth.
  • Plants can’t use most atmospheric nitrogen directly.
  • Nitrogen-fixing microorganisms change atmospheric nitrogen into usable compounds → helps form proteins + plant tissues.

Structure of the Atmosphere

How atmospheric layers are identified

  • Atmosphere is made of several layers.
  • Layers are named/identified by changes in:
    • Temperature
    • Air density
    • Altitude
  • Air density is highest near Earth’s surface and decreases with height.

Altitude

Altitude = height of a place above mean sea level. Measured in metres or feet. Mean sea level = 0.

Major atmospheric layers (from Earth’s surface upwards)

  1. Troposphere
  2. Stratosphere
  3. Mesosphere
  4. Thermosphere
  5. Exosphere

Troposphere

  • Lowest layer + most important.
  • Average height ≈ 12 km.
  • Temperature drops as altitude increases.
  • Contains:
    • Air humans breathe
    • Most water vapour
    • Most clouds
  • Almost all weather happens here:
    • Rainfall
    • Fog
    • Hail
    • Clouds
    • Storms
  • Boundary (troposphere ↔ stratosphere) = tropopause.

Stratosphere

  • Above the troposphere.
  • Up to about 50 km.
  • Aeroplanes often fly here because it’s mostly free of clouds + weather disturbances.
  • Has the ozone layer.
  • Ozone filters harmful solar radiation (incl. UV rays).
  • Boundary (stratosphere ↔ mesosphere) = stratopause.

Mesosphere

  • 3rd layer.
  • Up to about 80 km.
  • Temperature decreases as altitude increases.
  • Most meteors entering the atmosphere burn up here.
  • Upper boundary = mesopause.
Temperature decreases with altitude only in the troposphere and mesosphere.

Thermosphere

  • Roughly from 80 km to 700 km.
  • Temperature rises fast with altitude.
  • Gas molecules absorb X-rays + short-wave UV from the Sun.
  • Helps radio communication by reflecting radio waves back to Earth.
  • Ionosphere = part of the thermosphere.
  • Auroras happen here.

Auroras

  • Aurora is Latin for “dawn” / “morning light”.
  • Named after Aurora (Roman goddess of dawn).
  • Colourful lights seen near the polar regions.
  • Process:
    1. Sun releases charged particles = solar wind.
    2. Earth’s magnetic field guides them to the poles.
    3. Particles collide/interact with atmospheric gases.
    4. Different gases glow in different colours.
  • Naming:
    • Aurora Borealis = Northern Hemisphere
    • Aurora Australis = Southern Hemisphere

Exosphere

  • Highest/uppermost layer.
  • Air is extremely thin.
  • Gravity is weak → light gases like helium + hydrogen slowly escape into space.
  • Satellites orbit in the upper atmosphere and nearby space.
  • All layers affect atmospheric processes, weather, or climate.

Atmospheric Pressure on the Human Body

  • Air puts a lot of pressure on the human body.
  • We don’t usually feel it because:
    • Air presses from all directions.
    • Pressure inside the body = outside atmospheric pressure.
    • So internal + external pressures balance/cancel out.

Weather and Climate

Difference between weather and climate

Weather = hour-to-hour + day-to-day atmospheric conditions; can change fast (even within a day). Climate = average pattern of weather over a long time; includes totals + variations over a big area, usually over 30 years or more.
  • Weather:
    • Short-term atmospheric conditions
    • Changes hourly/daily
    • For a specific time
  • Climate:
    • Long-term average of weather
    • Studied over about 30 years or more
    • Shows the general pattern of a place/region

Major elements of weather and climate

  • Temperature
  • Humidity
  • Precipitation
  • Atmospheric pressure
  • Wind
These elements change depending on location and atmospheric conditions.

Temperature

  • Atmospheric temperature can change:
    • between day and night
    • across seasons
    • from one latitude to another
    • with altitude
  • Summers are usually warmer than winters.
  • Insolation is a major control on temperature.

Insolation

Insolation = incoming solar energy from the Sun intercepted by Earth.
  • Insolation is greatest near the equator because rays hit more directly.
  • It decreases from equator to poles because rays become more slanting.
  • So, temperature generally decreases from equator → poles.

Temperature zones

  • Torrid Zone: around the equator; gets the most direct sunlight.
  • North & South Temperate Zones: get moderately slanting sunlight.
  • North & South Frigid Zones: near the poles; get highly slanting sunlight → stay very cold.

Humidity

  • Water evaporates from land + water bodies and enters air as water vapour.
  • Humidity = presence/amount of water vapour in the air.
  • Warm air holds more water vapour than cold air.
  • High humidity leads to:
    • clothes dry slowly
    • sweat evaporates slowly
    • more physical discomfort

Precipitation

  • Precipitation happens when air becomes saturated with water vapour.
  • Water vapour condenses and falls under gravity.
  • Forms of precipitation:
    • Drizzle
    • Rain
    • Snow
    • Sleet
    • Hail
  • Precipitation is affected by:
    • Prevailing winds
    • Mountains
    • Seasons

Rain

Rain = liquid precipitation.
Don’t mix up layer boundaries: tropopause (troposphere–stratosphere), stratopause (stratosphere–mesosphere), mesopause (top of mesosphere). Also: temperature decreases with altitude only in the troposphere and mesosphere (in this classification).

Rainfall

Rainfall = the most common type of precipitation.
  • Effects / importance of rainfall:
    • lowers temperature of a place
    • supports agriculture
    • recharges rivers, reservoirs, and groundwater
  • Prolonged lack of rainfall → dry conditions and may cause drought.
  • Much of the groundwater supply is replenished by rainwater.

Atmospheric Pressure

Atmospheric (air) pressure = pressure from the weight of air on Earth’s surface.
  • Air pressure is highest at sea level.
  • Air pressure drops rapidly with altitude.
  • Air pressure is influenced horizontally by air temperature (temp differences → pressure differences between places).

Formation of Low Pressure

  • Warm air expands → becomes lighter → rises.
  • This leaves a low-pressure area at the surface.
Low pressure is usually linked with:
  • cloudy skies
  • unsettled conditions
  • wet weather

Formation of High Pressure

  • Cold air becomes heavy → sinks.
  • This creates a high-pressure area at the surface.
High pressure is usually linked with:
  • clear skies
  • sunny weather
Basic rule of air movement: air moves from high pressure → low pressure.

Wind

Wind = air moving from a high-pressure area to a low-pressure area.
  • Wind direction is also affected by Earth’s rotation.
  • Wind strength ranges from gentle to extremely strong.
  • Wind can:
    • move dust and dry leaves
    • sway branches
    • make walking difficult
    • uproot trees
    • damage buildings + infrastructure

Naming of Winds

Winds are named by the direction they come from.
  • Example: wind coming from the west = a westerly.

Wind Speeds and Effects

Wind categories, speeds & common effects:
  • Calm (0–1 km/h): smoke rises vertically.
  • Light breeze (6–11 km/h): felt on the face; leaves rustle; normal wind vane moves.
  • Strong breeze (39–49 km/h): large branches sway; umbrellas hard to use.
  • Storm (103–117 km/h): rare; causes widespread damage.
Don’t confuse wind direction naming: it’s named for where it comes from (westerly = from west, not towards west).

Local Winds

Sea Breeze

Sea breeze = local wind blowing sea → land during the day (esp. afternoon).
Sea-breeze mechanism (day sequence):
  1. Land heats faster than water.
  2. Warm air over land rises.
  3. Low pressure forms over land.
  4. Cooler air moves sea → land.

Land Breeze

Land breeze = local wind blowing land → sea during the night.
Land-breeze mechanism (night sequence):
  1. Land cools faster than the sea.
  2. Water stays relatively warmer.
  3. Air moves from cooler land → sea.
  • Pressure + temperature difference is usually small → land breeze is usually slow.
  • Land + sea breezes help moderate the climate of coastal regions.

Seasons in India

Formation of Seasons

  • Weather can change within a day.
  • But when similar atmospheric conditions last for weeks/months, we divide the year into seasons.
Seasonal patterns can be:
  • Cool / hot
  • Windy / calm
  • Cloudy / bright
  • Wet / dry

India’s Climate

India’s climate is broadly a tropical monsoon climate.
The India Meteorological Department (IMD) recognises four principal seasons.

Winter

  • Duration: December to early April
  • Coldest months: December & January
  • Average temperatures:
    • ~ 10–15°C in north-western India
    • ~ 20–25°C in south-eastern mainland India
  • Temperature generally increases towards the equator.

Summer or Pre-Monsoon Season

  • Duration: April to June
  • In north-western India, may extend into July.
  • Hottest month (generally):
    • April in western & southern India
    • May in northern India
  • Average temperatures in most inland India: 32°C to 40°C

Monsoon or Rainy Season

  • Duration: June to September
  • Dominated by the humid south-west monsoon.
  • Monsoon starts entering India in late May / early June.
  • It advances gradually across the country.
  • Rain begins withdrawing from northern India at the start of October.
  • Southern India gets significant rainfall during the withdrawal period.

Post-Monsoon or Retreating-Monsoon Season

  • Duration: October to December
  • October & November are generally cloudless in north-western India.
  • South-eastern India gets important rainfall from retreating / north-east monsoon winds.

Additional Seasons in Himalayan States

Because the climate is more temperate, Himalayan states also have:
  • Spring
  • Autumn

Traditional Indian Seasons

Six-Season System

Traditional Indian knowledge splits the year into six seasons (each ~ 2 months) called Ṛtu.
Six Ṛtus + Indian-calendar months + matching Gregorian months:
  • Vasanta: Chaitra–Vaiśhākha (March–April)
  • Grīṣhma: Jyeṣhṭha–Āṣhāḍha (May–June)
  • Varṣhā: Śhrāvaṇa–Bhādrapada (July–August)
  • Śharad: Āśhvina–Kārtika (September–October)
  • Hemanta: Mārgaśhīrṣha–Pauṣha (November–December)
  • Śhiśhira: Māgha–Phālguna (January–February)
  • Exact timings can vary by region in India.
  • Based on astronomical division of the 12 months into 6 parts.
  • The traditional Indian calendar month order reflects these seasons.
  • Hindustani Classical music links specific rāgas to seasons.

Ancient Rainfall Measurement

  • Kauṭilya’s Arthaśhāstra notes scientific rainfall measurements.
  • Used for:
    • Revenue administration
    • Agricultural planning
    • Relief measures

Monsoon

Meaning and Origin

Monsoon = seasonal reversal of wind direction over the year.
  • Ancient sailors sailing to India observed this reversal and used it to navigate.
  • Arab traders named it monsoon from Arabic mausim = “season”.

Traditional Monsoon Forecasting

Since farming depended heavily on rains, Indians developed rainfall prediction methods.
Kṛiṣhiparāśhara
  • Explained seasonal techniques to predict rainfall.
  • Main method: studied the positions of the Moon and Sun.
Varāhamihira’s Bṛihatsaṁhitā
  • Used lunar mansions (nakṣhatras) to forecast seasonal rainfall.
  • Many farming practices still draw on these traditional ideas.

Lunar Mansions or Nakṣhatras

  • Nakṣhatras split the Moon’s path into 27 equal parts.
  • Each part links to a star or group of stars.
  • Moon takes ~ 27 days to complete this path (relative to stars).
  • So it passes through about 1 nakṣhatra per day (like a new “house” each night).

Types and Mechanism of Monsoon

South-West Monsoon

  • South-west monsoon = also called summer monsoon.
  • Mainly active from June to September.
  • Winds blow sea → land across the:
    • Indian Ocean
    • Arabian Sea
    • Bay of Bengal

Mechanism

  1. In summer, Indian land heats up faster than nearby oceans.
  2. A low-pressure area forms over the subcontinent.
  3. The cooler Indian Ocean stays at high pressure.
  4. Moist winds blow from high (ocean) to low (land) pressure.
  5. These moisture-laden winds bring widespread rainfall.
The south-west monsoon gives most of India’s annual rainfall.

Advance of the South-West Monsoon

  • Usually reaches the southern seas + Andaman region around 22–26 May.
  • Reaches parts of Kerala around 1 June.
  • Spreads northwards & westwards through June.
  • Covers much of northern & north-western India by late June / early July.

North-East Monsoon (Winter Monsoon)

North-east monsoon (= winter monsoon) runs roughly from October to February.

Mechanism (How the North-East Monsoon Works)

  1. In winter, India’s land cools faster than nearby seas.
  2. High pressure builds over the cold land.
  3. Lower pressure stays over the relatively warm seas.
  4. So cold, dry winds blow land → sea (high → low pressure).
Since the winds start over land, they are mostly dry → little rain for most of India.
  • But after crossing the Bay of Bengal, they pick up moisture.
  • Then they cause rain along India’s eastern coast, esp.:
    • Tamil Nadu
    • Andhra Pradesh
    • Parts of Karnataka
So the north-east monsoon is crucial for south-eastern India (esp. the Tamil Nadu coast) as it gives major seasonal rainfall there.

Retreat (Withdrawal) of the Monsoon

  • Withdrawal usually starts in north-west India around mid-September.
  • It shifts gradually southward and eastward.
  • Most of northern India sees withdrawal by late September / early October.
  • It withdraws from central + peninsular India during October.
Don’t mix seasons: retreat begins mid-Sep (NW) → moves S/E; then NE monsoon operates Oct–Feb.

Importance of the Monsoon

Indian agriculture mostly depends on monsoon rain. Timing + strength + distribution directly affect livelihoods + the economy.

Benefits of a Good Monsoon

  • Helps sowing + crop growth
  • Ensures enough food production
  • Recharges rivers, reservoirs, wells
  • Boosts rural employment
  • Improves overall water supply

Wider Impacts of the Monsoon (Beyond Farming)

  • Transport
  • Daily routines
  • Festivals
  • Employment
  • Rural livelihoods
  • National and regional economies

Problems Caused by Monsoon Variation

  • Too much rain → floods
  • Weak/failed monsoon → droughts
Monsoon strength + distribution shape India’s economy + lifestyle by deciding who gets floods, droughts, or adequate rain.

Kālidāsa and the Monsoon (Historical Reference)

  • In Meghadūtam (c. 5th century CE), Kālidāsa:
    • Noted the approximate monsoon onset over central India
    • Traced the route of monsoon clouds
Use as evidence: Indians have observed/valued monsoon patterns since ancient/classical times (ex: Kālidāsa).

Modern Monsoon and Weather Forecasting

National Monsoon Mission

  • Started by the Ministry of Earth Sciences.
  • Built advanced weather + climate prediction models.
  • Goal: better Indian monsoon prediction across different time scales.
Exam angle: remember the ministry + the main aim (better monsoon prediction via improved models).

Mission Mausam

Mission Mausam: strengthens India’s weather/climate science + services to cut risk and improve preparedness.
  • Targets India as a global leader in weather + climate science.
  • Aims for “Weather Ready” + “Climate Smart” India.
  • Upgrades weather and climate services.
  • Ensures timely + accurate:
    • Observations
    • Modelling
    • Forecasts
  • Supports sectors like:
    • Agriculture
    • Disaster management
    • Rural development
Differentiate: National Monsoon Mission = monsoon prediction models; Mission Mausam = broader weather/climate leadership + services (observations, modelling, forecasts) for key sectors.

Climate Change

Meaning of Climate Change

Climate change = long-term changes in patterns of:
  • Temperature
  • Rainfall
  • Wind
  • Extreme weather
  • Today’s climate change is mainly due to human activities, e.g.:
    • Burning fossil fuels
    • Deforestation
    • Industrial pollution

Greenhouse Gases

Greenhouse gases = gases in the atmosphere that trap heat → warming.
  • Human activities raise greenhouse gases, incl.:
    • Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
    • Methane (CH₄)
    • Nitrous oxide (N₂O)
    • Water vapour
  1. Gases accumulate in the atmosphere.
  2. They trap heat.
  3. They increase global temperatures.
  4. They intensify global warming and climate change.
Don’t write “only CO₂ causes global warming.” You must mention multiple gases (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, water vapour) + the chain: accumulate → trap heat → warming → climate change.

Effects of Climate Change

  • More frequent floods
  • More severe droughts
  • Melting glaciers
  • Rising sea levels
  • Loss of biodiversity
  • Damage to ecosystems
  • Health problems
  • Agricultural losses
  • Livelihood insecurity
  • Effects are felt across society, incl. women and children.
Impacts are environmental + economic + social (health, farming, livelihoods) and they hit vulnerable groups too (women & children are explicitly named).

Responding to Climate Change

Response needs collective action + individual action; small steps together = big change.
  • Reducing carbon footprints
  • Using renewable energy
  • Protecting and planting forests
  • Conserving electricity
  • Conserving water
  • Reducing waste
  • Recycling materials
  • Avoiding single-use plastic
  • Using public transport or cycling
  • Adopting sustainable lifestyles
Exam wording to use: “Every individual action contributes to a healthier and greener future.” (key concluding idea in the text)

Carbon Footprint

Carbon footprint = the total greenhouse gases released due to human activities like:
  • Energy use
  • Transportation
  • Production of goods
  • Provision of services

Evaluating Daily Habits (Carbon-Footprint Activity Criteria)

Daily behaviours are grouped by impact level (low/medium/high/very high) and scored using a points system for climate-friendliness.

Transport Habits (Impact Levels)

  • Walking or cycling: low impact
  • Public transport or carpooling: medium impact
  • Private cars for short distances: high impact
  • More than two flights annually: very high impact

Electricity Use (Impact Levels)

  • Always switching off unused appliances: low impact
  • Sometimes forgetting: medium impact
  • Frequently leaving appliances on: high impact

Water Use (Impact Levels)

  • Careful water use, e.g. one bucket for bathing: low impact
  • Sometimes leaving taps running / long showers: medium impact
  • Rarely thinking about water conservation: high impact

Waste and Plastic Use (Impact Levels)

  • Reusing, recycling + avoiding single-use plastic: low impact
  • Sometimes using disposable bottles, bags or straws: medium impact
  • Frequently throwing plastic away without recycling: high impact

Scoring System (Points)

  • Low impact: 1 point
  • Medium impact: 2 points
  • High impact: 3 points
  • Very high impact: 4 points
Interpretation: Lower total score = more climate-friendly behaviour.
  • Students should pick two practical changes + write a personal climate-action pledge.
If asked to “evaluate habits,” do BOTH: (1) sort behaviours into impact levels using the examples, and (2) apply the points system where lower is better.

Punjab Floods, 2025: Case Study

Overview

  • 2025: Punjab saw major flooding after very heavy monsoon rain + rivers Satluj, Beas, Ravi swelled.
  • Flood damage hit:
    • Villages
    • Agricultural fields
    • Houses
    • Roads
    • Bridges
    • Other infrastructure
  • Relief operations happened; water receded from some areas.
  • But the floods still caused serious:
    • Economic losses
    • Social disruption
    • Environmental damage
Core lesson: showed the need for stronger flood preparedness + management.

Natural Causes

  • Extremely heavy monsoon rainfall over Punjab.
  • Western disturbances added extra moisture + rain.
  • Heavy rain also in Himachal Pradesh + Jammu and Kashmir.
  • Satluj, Beas, Ravi, Ghaggar already carrying high volumes.
  • More rainfall + hill runoff → rivers overflowed.

Human-Made Causes (Factors that Intensified Flooding and Damage)

  • Weak/old river embankments called dhūsī bāndh
  • Houses + farms built too close to rivers
  • Natural floodplains reduced
  • Silt + mud build-up in rivers and dams
  • Lower capacity of rivers/reservoirs to carry/store water
  • Late/unclear flood warnings
  • Lack of public preparedness
Punjab floods = natural + human-made factors interacting (not just one cause).

Effects of the Punjab Floods

  • Loss of lives.
  • Thousands displaced into relief camps.
  • Large areas of farmland submerged.
  • Paddy + other crops badly damaged.
  • Poultry + dairy farms faced heavy losses.
  • Cows, buffaloes, chickens fell ill or died.
  • Roads, bridges, border fences, public buildings damaged.
  • Stagnant muddy water led to:
    • Waterborne diseases
    • Sanitation problems
    • Public-health risks

Flood-Management Discussion (What the Case Study Prompts You to Examine)

  • How much was due to natural vs human causes
  • Could better planning have reduced losses?
  • NDMA flood-management guidelines
  • Role of students/young people in disaster preparedness

Role of Students and Young People in Disaster Preparedness

  • Awareness campaigns
  • Joining emergency drills
  • Sharing verified warnings
  • Helping prepare emergency kits
  • Supporting relief work (with responsible supervision)
  • Promoting environmental protection
In answers: split (1) causes (natural vs human-made) and (2) effects (people, agriculture, infrastructure, health). End with preparedness/management + youth roles.

Climatic Data of Selected Indian Stations

Purpose of Climatic Graphs

Climatic (climate) graphs turn monthly temperature + rainfall data into visuals → easier comparisons.
  • Graphs help compare:
    • Temperature range

Seasonal Rainfall and Monsoon-Related Climatic Features (India)

Seasonal rainfall = rain concentrated in certain seasons (India: mainly tied to monsoon circulation). Wet vs dry months = high-rain months (wet) vs little/none (dry) → clear yearly rainfall rhythm.
Coastal (maritime) climate = sea influence → smaller temperature extremes + often higher humidity. Continental (inland) climate = away from sea → larger seasonal temperature range (hotter summers, colder winters).
Effects of altitude and relief
  • Altitude: higher altitude usually lowers temperature; can affect precipitation type/amount.
  • Relief: hills/mountains force moist winds to rise → cool → condense → more rain on windward side.
  • Rain shadow: leeward side gets little rain because moisture is lost on the windward side.
Monsoon patterns = seasonal wind reversal/shift causing clear wet + dry seasons; includes advancing (south-west) monsoon + retreating (north-east) monsoon influences.

Important Climatic Comparisons (Using the Representative Stations)

Extreme Climate

  • Delhi & Jodhpur → large seasonal temperature range due to inland/continental location.
  • Leh → very cold + very dry because of:
    • High altitude
    • Rain-shadow location

Equable Climate

Equable climate = small temperature range (not extremely hot or cold).
Thiruvananthapuram has an equable climate because:
  • Near the equator
  • Near the sea
  • Maritime influence cuts down temperature extremes

Retreating-Monsoon Rainfall

Retreating (north-east) monsoon = phase after the south-west monsoon withdraws; brings significant rain to parts of India, esp. the south-eastern coast.
  • Chennai is strongly influenced by the retreating / north-east monsoon.
  • Thiruvananthapuram also gets substantial post-monsoon rain.
  • Chennai’s peak rainfall is in October–November → i.e., after the south-west monsoon has withdrawn from most of India.
Common mistake: thinking India’s peak rainfall is always during the south-west monsoon. The south-eastern coast (e.g., Chennai) is the key exception: peak rain in Oct–Nov due to the retreating monsoon.

Hottest Stations (By Month, from the Representative Table)

  • February → hottest: Thiruvananthapuram, then Chennai (close).
  • June → hottest: Jodhpur, then Delhi.

Shillong and Kolkata: Why Rainfall Differs

Orographic rainfall = rain when moisture-bearing winds are forced to rise over hills/mountains → air cools, condenses → heavy rain.
  • Shillong gets more rainfall than Kolkata because its elevated relief forces monsoon winds to rise → cool → condense.
  • Result = heavy orographic rainfall.

Delhi and Jodhpur: Why Rainfall Differs

  • Delhi gets more rain than Jodhpur because it is better placed to receive moisture-bearing monsoon winds.
  • Jodhpur lies in the dry western region where:
    • Monsoon winds carry less moisture.
    • Aravalli range does not effectively block these winds to cause heavy rainfall.

Leh’s Precipitation (Very Low Rain/Snowfall)

  • Leh gets only light precipitation all year.
  • Located in the rain shadow of the Himalayas.
  • Winter precipitation is partly due to western disturbances.
  • Annual rainfall is extremely low.
Exam tip: For Leh, always write both controls: altitude (cold) + rain shadow (dry), and add winter western disturbances for limited precipitation.

Climatic Unity of India

Climatic unity = one overall climate pattern across a country, even with regional differences.
Even with big regional variations, the monsoon gives climatic unity because it:
  • sets the rhythm of seasons
  • gives most annual rainfall
  • shapes agriculture
  • fixes sowing & harvesting cycles
  • recharges water sources
  • influences livelihoods & festivals
  • affects almost every region directly/indirectly

Climate, Houses and Clothing (Human Adaptations)

Core idea: Houses + clothing change with climate and relief → humans adapt to local air + land conditions.
Examples (climate/relief-based):
  • Sloping roofs in heavy rain/snow areas → quick runoff; no accumulation.
  • Thick walls + small openings in hot, dry regions → less heat entry + blocks hot winds.
  • Raised houses in flood-prone areas → safer from floodwater.
  • Warm woollen clothes in mountains → insulation from cold.
  • Light cotton clothes in hot & humid regions → comfort + ventilation/sweat absorption.
These show the link between human life, relief and climatic conditions.
