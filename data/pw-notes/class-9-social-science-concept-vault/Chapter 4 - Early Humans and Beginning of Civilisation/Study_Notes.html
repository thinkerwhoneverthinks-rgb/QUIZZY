Chapter 4: Early Humans and Beginning of Civilisation

The Big Questions

  • How did humans live before civilisation?
  • How did humans communicate before writing?
  • How does archaeology help us understand the past?
  • How did early civilisations interact?

Prehistory, Writing and Historical Evidence

Prehistory = the long stretch of human past before writing. Rebuilt mainly using archaeological evidence (because writing began at different times in different regions).
  • Human history goes way beyond kingdoms/empires: to study earliest ancestors we look at evolution, where they lived, what they ate, how they lived, and how they became modern humans.
  • Writing/sources: before writing → mainly artefacts + material remains; after writing → we also get documents and recorded info.

Early scripts mentioned (and what we know)

  • Sindhu–Sarasvatī / Indus Valley / Harappan civilisation used pictographic engravings on seals and pottery.
  • Harappan script is undeciphered (called Sindhu lipi) → so the meaning of its writing is unknown.
  • Sumerian cuneiform (Mesopotamia) + Egyptian hieroglyphics were contemporary with Harappans, but these have been deciphered.
  • Deciphered scripts = start of the historical period roughly 5,000 years ago (written records become available).
  • Brahmi: used from about 400 BCE in southern India + the Ganga Valley; later formalised by Emperor Aśhoka (3rd century BCE); also used by contemporary Iron Age communities across southern India.

Before and After the Invention of Writing (exam-ready comparison)

  • Share of human history: before writing = over 99% (about 3 million–5,000 years ago); after writing = under 1% (last ~5,000 years).
  • Main sources: before writing = artefacts (tools, implements, material remains); after writing = material remains + documents.
  • Understanding people: before writing = thoughts/ideas hard to recover; after writing = literature can give names, events, plus social/political/cultural life.
  • Dating: before writing = mostly approximate dates for cultures/events; after writing = more accurate dates from records (e.g., coronations, wars).
Exam tip: Don’t equate “history” with “oldest times.” Here, history begins with writing; earlier = prehistory, even though humans existed millions of years before.

Why Study Early Humans?

  • Shows humanity’s long biological + cultural evolution linked to climate and environmental change.

Biological evolution

Biological evolution = slow physical/genetic change from early ancestors (like australopithecines) to Homo sapiens. Australopithecines: australis = southern, pithecus = primates.

Cultural evolution (Quaternary Period)

Cultural evolution = adapting through tools, techniques, technology to use resources, especially in the Quaternary Period. Quaternary Period = last 26 lakh years (includes today).

From foraging to farming: foundation of civilisation

Big shift: hunting-gathering → agriculture → food/material surplus → laid the foundation of civilisation.

Out-of-Africa movements

  • Earliest ancestors evolved in Africa.
  • Homo erectus (“upright”, bipedal) = first hominin to leave Africa, around 2 million years ago.
  • Homo erectus carried handaxes + cleavers into Asia and Europe between 2 and 0.5 million years ago.
  • Homo sapiens evolved in Africa around 300,000 years ago.
  • Another major out-of-Africa move ≈ 125,000 years ago → modern humans eventually spread worldwide.
  • Suggested migration reasons: climatic pressure, resource availability, mobility.
Hominins = modern humans + early human-like ancestors.

Archaeology and Experimental Archaeology

Archaeology = study of the human past mainly through material remains found by excavation.
  • Archaeologists excavate tools, bones, objects → reconstruct past life.
Experimental archaeology = making + using similar tools/objects to figure out how they were made, how they were used, and how people depended on them daily.
Exam distinction: excavation = finds evidence; experimental archaeology = tests explanations using replicas.

Human Ancestors and Tool-Making

Old World and early settlements

Old World = Africa, Asia, Europe → region of earliest Palaeolithic settlements.
  • Several human species coexisted in the Old World.
  • Oldest stone tools date to about 3.3 million years ago.
  • Tool-making = key “human behaviour” → hominins became tool-makers.

Tools as “extra-corporal limbs”

Tools = “extra-corporal limbs”: body-extensions that let humans do tasks beyond bare hands.
  • Discarded tools later got buried → became evidence for archaeologists.

Fossils

Fossils = preserved remains/traces/impressions of ancient plants/animals/humans that become mineralised after burial over thousands or millions of years.

What tools and fossils reveal about major human species

  • Tools + fossils help rebuild hominin lives + tech stages:
  • Homo habilis (“handy man”) lived esp. near Olduvai Gorge (Tanzania/Kenya) and made choppers.
  • Homo erectus invented handaxes/cleavers and spread beyond Africa.
  • Homo sapiens made complex technologies.
For image-based questions: look for skull changes + gradual straightening of the face across evolution.

Key ancestors: time/place and technology/status

  • Homo habilis: Africa; Olduvai Gorge; about 2–6 million years ago; chopper tools.
  • Homo erectus: Eastern African Rift Valley; about 2 million years ago; handaxes, cleavers; early migrant.
  • Homo neanderthalensis: Europe/Southwest Asia till about 40,000 years ago; Middle Palaeolithic flake tools.
  • Homo sapiens: only living human species today; complex technology; global spread.
Don’t mix tool types: choppers → habilis; handaxes/cleavers → erectus; flake tools (Middle Palaeolithic) → Neanderthals; complex technology → sapiens.

Periods in Early Human History

Early human history is split into periods mainly by changes in technology, agriculture, lifestyle & settlement.
  • Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age): hunting-gathering; simple stone tools.
  • Mesolithic: microliths; in-between stage (shift from foraging → farming).
  • Neolithic (New Stone Age): agriculture, settled life, animal domestication, polished stone tools — the Neolithic Revolution.
  • Chalcolithic: copper used with stone; early metallurgy.
  • Bronze Age: bronze (copper + tin); growing trade, towns & early civilisations.
  • Iron Age: widespread iron smelting/working; stronger tools/weapons; more advanced societies.
Agricultural shift = “revolution” because it transformed subsistence, settlement, production & society — not because it was small/isolated.

Palaeolithic Hunter-Gatherers in the Indian Subcontinent

Stone Age stages and word meaning

Stone Age has 3 stages: Palaeolithic, Mesolithic, Neolithic. Palaeolithic: palaeo = old; lithic = stone.

Earliest settlement evidence and key sites

  • Oldest settlement evidence in the subcontinent: about 2 million years ago.
  • Attirampakkam (Tamil Nadu): about 1.5–1.7 million years old.
  • Isampur (Karnataka): about 1.2 million years old.

Tools, raw materials, and what they reveal about activities

  • Finds: quartzite/limestone tools like handaxes, cleavers, scrapers, choppers + animal fossils.
  • These point to activities like:
    • chopping meat
    • digging tubers
    • scraping hides
    • splitting bones to get protein-rich marrow
  • Later: smaller scrapers, borers, points → better hunting via sharp-tipped projectiles.
  • Bow and arrow, parallel-sided blades & microblades made from glassy, sharp rocks → helped hunt small animals.

Symbolic communication and advanced tool use

Symbolic communication grew through material/visual culture: cave/rock art, body pigments, and beads of stone, bone, shell.
  • Burins/engravers: used to incise symbols on bone & shell, incl. ostrich eggs.
  • Microblades often mounted in handles (composite tools).
  • These innovations linked to Homo sapiens, who reached Australia and the Americas around 50,000–12,000 years ago.

Map-based knowledge: key sites to recognise

Chapter map shows many sites across the subcontinent; named examples include Attirampakkam, Isampur, Bhimbetka, Hathnora, Mehrgarh and others.

Mesolithic Hunter-Gatherers

  • Around 12,000 years ago, warming spread forests and grasslands into areas earlier affected by glaciation.
  • These environments offered small game, fish, and edible wild grains → history’s first population explosion.
  • Microliths helped marine & freshwater food collection; fishing became a major subsistence activity.
  • Art flourished; caves/rock shelters were used again and again.
  • Bhimbetka (Madhya Pradesh): World Heritage Site; hundreds of painted shelters showing Mesolithic + earlier occupation.
Mesolithic = microliths + warming after glaciation + fishing + rock shelter art (key named example: Bhimbetka).

The Neolithic Revolution

  • Growing knowledge of seasons & foods slowly moved humans from gathering → food production.
Domestication (hallmark of the Neolithic Revolution) = plants/animals brought under human control, creating new breeds via cultivation & husbandry.

Neolithic Transition: Tools, Villages, and Uneven Regional Pathways

Neolithic shift in material culture
  • Foragers’ tools: mainly for procurement (getting food/resources).
  • Farmers’ tools: more about production + processing (grow, harvest, store, grind, etc.).
  • Farmers also made varied earthenware pottery (big change from earlier mobile life).
  • Diverse raw materials + first villages laid the base for the later urban revolution (towns/cities + complex life).
Non-simultaneous transition (different regional sequences) Farming + village life did not begin everywhere at the same time or in the same order. Regions followed different sequences involving:
  • Cultivation (crop domestication + farming)
  • Livestock (animal domestication + herding)
  • Pottery (earthenware making)
  • Sedentism (permanent settlement)
  • Agricultural landscapes (fields, irrigation/farming environments)
Examples of regional crops mentioned:
  • West Africa: pearl millet
  • West Asia: wheat and barley
  • India: millets
  • Ganga Plains: rice
  • North China: millets
  • Yangtze / South China: rice
Exam trap: Don’t write that the Neolithic “package” (farming + pottery + villages + animals) appeared everywhere together. The text says the transition was not simultaneous and the sequence differed by region.

Neolithic Indian Subcontinent

  • Timing wasn’t uniform across the subcontinent → no single “Neolithic start date” for all of India.

Mehrgarh: Earliest Neolithic Village in the Subcontinent

  • Site: Mehrgarh
  • Location: on the Bolan River (present-day Pakistan)
  • Date: ~7000 BCE
  • Why it matters: oldest Neolithic site + earliest agricultural village in the Indian subcontinent
Mehrgarh: material life + economy
  • Houses/granaries: handmade sun-dried brick houses + granaries → permanent settlement + storage.
  • Burial practice: grave burials (major cultural marker).
  • Ornaments/materials: lapis lazuli, carnelian, shell ornaments → craft skill + exchange networks.
  • Crops: wheat, barley.
  • Animals: sheep, goats, Indian zebu cattle.

From Neolithic to Chalcolithic and Toward the Bronze Age

  • Mehrgarh people were among the earliest copper-object makers.
  • This signals the Chalcolithic (Copper Age) by ~4000 BCE.
  • Mehrgarh developments helped form the base for the Bronze Age Sindhu–Sarasvatī civilisation by ~3500 BCE.
Keep the dates separate: Mehrgarh Neolithic (~7000 BCE) → copper/Chalcolithic (~4000 BCE) → base for Bronze Age Sindhu–Sarasvatī (~3500 BCE).

Other Neolithic Evidence and Wider Spread

  • Sanganakallu: hilltop Neolithic site, dated ~2000 BCE.
  • By ~2500 BCE, Neolithic farming communities spread across much of the subcontinent.
  • Typical combo:
    • Herding: cattle, sheep, goats
    • Crops: cereals, millets, pulses
  • Sometimes in contact with Chalcolithic cultures → overlap/interaction, not isolation.

Rise of the Sindhu–Sarasvatī Civilisation

From Mehrgarh Expansion to Chalcolithic Settlements and Bronze Age Beginnings

  • Mehrgarh’s Neolithic lifestyle spread across the middle + upper Indus and eastward.
  • Copper extraction around 4000 BCE → early Chalcolithic settlements + the push toward the Bronze Age.
  • Copper tools in the fertile alluvium of the Indus and Ghaggar–Sarasvatī boosted productivity + prosperity.
  • Large-scale pottery production grew, with clear regional styles (key clue for cultural patterns).

Early Dates and the Pre-Harappan Phases

  • Radiocarbon dates from Bhirrana and Kunal (dried Sarasvatī belt) point to Pre-Harappan phases between ~7000–5500 BCE.
Exam tip: Quote it exactly → 7000–5500 BCE (radiocarbon evidence: Bhirrana + Kunal).

Early Harappan Stage: Continuity and Emerging Urban Features

  • From ~4000 BCE, regional Chalcolithic styles kept evolving.
  • By ~2500 BCE, many traits became standard Sindhu–Sarasvatī features.
  • Early Harappan is defined by cultural continuity.
Continuities (carried into Harappan culture):
  • Pottery
  • Semi-precious beads
  • Shell bangles
  • Terracotta
  • Copper working
Emerging features (new):
  • Settlement perimeter walls
  • Seals
  • Maybe script (early writing)

Crafts, Pottery Variation, and Early Writing Signals

  • Harappans did farming + major crafts.
  • Regional pottery differences showed up in:
    • Vessel shape
    • Design
  • Copper, shell, bead crafts developed strongly.
  • Early bead production found at Harappa, Kunal, Datrana.
  • Pottery graffiti + seals with geometric / simple animal motifs at Harappa, Rehman Dheri, Kunal → seen as early signals of Harappan writing + later inscribed seals.
  • Tech growth + economic integration → mature urban centres by ~2600 BCE.

Harappan Timeline and Enduring Practices

Broad Chronological Sequence

  • Neolithic: ~7000 BCE
  • Chalcolithic: ~4500 BCE
  • Early Harappan / Bronze Age: ~3300 BCE
  • Mature Harappan: ~2600–1900 BCE
  • Late Harappan: after 1900 to ~1300/1000 BCE
Common mistake: Don’t start Mature Harappan at 2500 BCE here—this set gives ~2600–1900 BCE.

Kalibangan Field Evidence: Double Cropping

  • A nearly 5,000-year-old field at Kalibangan has crossing horizontal + vertical furrows.
  • This suggests double cropping, like Rabi + Kharif seasons.

Standard Weights: Binary and Decimal Multiples

  • Harappans used standard cubical stone weights.
  • Small units followed binary multiples: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, …
  • Bigger values followed decimal multiples.
  • Why standardisation mattered:
    • Planning
    • Long-distance trade

Bronze Age River-Valley Civilisations

Major Bronze Age river-valley civilisations (river regions + key traits/contacts)
  • Sindhu–Sarasvatī (Sindhu + Sarasvatī):
    • Planned cities
    • Drainage/water systems
    • Standards
    • Beads, seals, script
  • Mesopotamian (Tigris–Euphrates, West Asia):
    • Closest to Harappans
    • Strong contact/trade
  • Egyptian (Nile):
    • Little tangible direct Harappan contact
  • Chinese (Huang He, northern China):
    • Little tangible direct Harappan contact
  • River plains → agricultural surplus + rise of cities, but also needed flood/water management.
  • Geography made Mesopotamia + the Indus–Ghaggar-Sarasvatī basins especially connected → helps explain contact/trade.

Mesopotamian Civilisation

  • Meaning: Mesopotamia = “land in between” (between rivers).
  • Region: Tigris–Euphrates basin.
  • Modern map: mainly Iraq + Kuwait, plus parts of Turkey and southwestern Iran.

The Fertile Crescent (Zagros–Taurus Foothill Arc)

  • Zagros–Taurus foothill arc runs from the Mediterranean to the Persian Gulf.
  • This arc is the fertile crescent → key early zone for agriculture + settlement.

Beginnings of Farming and Early Mesopotamian City-States

  • Farming starts about 12,000 years before present.
  • Copper arrives c. 4500 BCE → boosts agriculture, crafts + trade → helps farming communities expand.
  • Next two millennia: big settlements grow into sovereign city-states.
  • Main Mesopotamian powers from c. 3500 BCE: Sumerian, Akkadian, Assyrian, Babylonian.

Sumerians

Origins, Cities, and Engineering

  • Earliest city civilisation develops at Ur + other cities in southern Mesopotamia (southern Iraq).
  • Sumerians pioneered:
    • Dams + canal irrigation (control water → farming + settlement growth).
    • Building with mud-brick + burnt-brick for houses, walls, public buildings.

Religion, Ziggurats, and Political Economy

  • Religion = polytheistic → many gods seen as controlling natural forces like floods + winds.
  • Each city centred on a chief-god ziggurat.
Ziggurat = stepped, many-level temple with a sacred summit. Included palaces/storehouses, usually one guarded entrance, and acted as a treasury.
  • Temple authority ran key areas: agriculture, trade, transport.
  • Restricted sacred access (only high priests/priestesses) = strong social hierarchy → religious power tied to social + economic control.

Social Life, Occupations, and Material Culture

  • Kings lived in palaces; ordinary people in small brick houses.
  • Economy mainly farming, plus:
    • metalwork, pottery, textiles
    • merchants + traders
  • Visual evidence (images) shows:
    • wheeled carts
    • elaborate clothing + ornaments

Comparative Note: Early Harappans (Indus Region) Water and Trade Infrastructure

  • Early Harappans also built big water-management + trade structures:
    • Gabarband check dams = small dams to slow water + manage runoff.
    • Dholavira: diverted two streams using dams/canals into interconnected tanks made of stone, mud-brick + bedrock.
    • Lothal: a huge burnt-brick dockyard → organised maritime trade infrastructure.

Cuneiform and Sumerian Innovations

Cuneiform Writing: Features, Users, Spread, and Decline

  • Sumerians start writing c. 3300 BCE.
  • Cuneiform = “wedge” (Latin cuneus).
  • How: hundreds of marks pressed into damp clay with wedge-shaped reeds.
  • Used by high-status scribes.
  • By 3000 BCE, used across Mesopotamian city-states + across different languages.
  • Cuneiform disappears by end of the 1st millennium BCE.

What Tablets Preserve (Why Writing Matters)

  • Clay tablets preserve:
    • myths, epics, hymns
    • law codes
    • teaching
    • records of farming + crafts, incl. work of potters, seal cutters, shipbuilders, carpenters, farm workers

Technological and Mathematical Innovations (Including Base 60)

  • Key Sumerian innovations:
    • wheeled carts
    • sailboats
    • calculations for buildings + fields (early applied maths for planning/measurement)
    • base 60 (sexagesimal system)
  • Base 60 legacy:
    • 60-minute hour
    • 60-second minute
    • 360-degree circle
Exam focus: connect cuneiform decipherment to what historians can know—because it’s readable, it gives detailed political, economic + cultural info that isn’t available the same way for undeciphered scripts (e.g., Harappan writing).

Akkadians, Assyrians and Babylonians

Akkadians: Empire, Language, Literature, and Trade

  • Akkad rises in 2334 BCE in central Mesopotamia → overshadows Sumer.
  • Akkadian ≠ Sumerian, but both use cuneiform.
  • Akkad forms the first dynastic empire + drives creative literature.
  • Epic of Gilgamesh = early written story tied to this tradition.
  • King Sargon’s tablets record eastern trade with:
    • Dilmun (Bahrain)
    • Magan (Oman peninsula)
    • Meluhha (Sindhu–Sarasvatī / Indus region)
  • Trade goods: semi-precious beads, ivory, timber, gold dust, probably copper.

Assyrians: Rise and Duration

  • Assur replaces Akkadian supremacy around 2154 BCE.
  • Assyrian rule starts in northern Mesopotamia, spreads west + south, lasts until the early 1700s BCE.

Babylonia: Hammurabi, Law Code, and Decline Factors

  • Babylonia dominates central Mesopotamia from about 1900 BCE.
  • Hammurabi (from 1792 BCE):
    • conquers neighbouring states
    • compiles civil + social rules → Code of Hammurabi
    • code becomes a model for later legal systems
  • By the late 1400 BCE, Babylon weakens due to:
    • Hittite attacks + other rivals
    • rivals’ superior technologies + resource use
    • environmental degradation
    • stressed farmland
    • internal political + economic problems

Hittites: Identity and Region

  • Hittites = an Indo-European people.
  • They build an empire in Anatolia (modern Turkey) in the 2nd millennium BCE.

Egyptian Civilisation

Why Egypt Is “Richly Recorded”

  • One of the earliest + most richly recorded civilisations.
  • Known to the Greeks and Romans.
  • Herodotus visited in the 5th century BCE.
  • 19th-century writings (called de l’Égypte) added more historical detail.

Papyrus: Material, Records, Libraries, and Literature

Papyrus records kept both stories + practical know-how.
How papyrus sheets were made:
  1. Cut inner-stem strips.
  2. Lay strips in cross-layers.
  3. Press.
  4. Dry.
  5. Polish to finish.
  • Papyrus Ebers: 700+ medical cures + spells → medicine mixed with belief/ritual.
  • Libraries (from ~2000 BCE): stored labelled papyrus jars.
  • Egyptian literature included:
    • early Sindbad-type story
    • animal fables linked to Aesop
    • ghosts, miracles, romance
    • early Cinderella-style tale

Egyptian Timeline (Key Periods)

  • Neolithic: c. 5500 BCE
  • Chalcolithic: c. 4000 BCE
  • Bronze Age: c. 3100 BCE
  • Old Kingdom: 2686–2181 BCE
  • Middle Kingdom: 2030–1650 BCE
  • New Kingdom: 1570–1069 BCE

Hieroglyphs, Calendar and Nile

Rosetta Stone and Decipherment of Egyptian

  • Rosetta Stone:
    • found by French engineer Pierre Bouchard in 1799
    • written in three scripts, incl. readable Greek
  • Jean-François Champollion cracked Egyptian in 1822.

Egyptian Calendar: Seasons, Sirius Basis, and Year Length

Calendar = 3 seasons, each 4 months, based on Sirius (its cycle helped mark the year).
  • Inundation (autumn): flood season
  • Peret / growing (winter): crops grow
  • Shemu / harvest (summer): harvesting
Length of the year: 12 × 30 + 5 = 365 days
  • 12 = months
  • 30 = days per month
  • 5 = extra days added after 360
  • 365 = calendar year, but it missed ~¼ day (≈ 0.25 day) each year

Beliefs About the Afterlife: Papyrus of Ani

  • Papyrus of Ani: shows heart-and-feather scales → belief in judgement after death.

Nile Floods, Agriculture, Administration, and “Kemet”

  • Cities appear around 3000 BCE.
  • Yearly Nile floods left fertile black mud called kemet.
  • Land called Kemet = “the black” (fertile black soil).
  • About 5,000 years ago, farmers:
    • dug ditches
    • diverted + stored Nile water
    • counted flood intervals to make/keep a calendar
  • Cooperation for dams + ditches pushed development of:
    • local government
    • administrative class (officials organising + keeping records)

Geography of Egypt: Upper vs Lower Egypt (Map Skill)

  • Find the Nile on a map + remember flow direction (exam map skill).
  • Lower Egypt = north, downstream, near the Nile delta.
  • Upper Egypt = south, upstream.
Common exam mistake: “Upper” ≠ north. In Egypt: Upper = upstream (south), Lower = downstream (north/delta) because the Nile flows north.

Pharaohs, Pyramids, Society and Life

Pharaohs as rulers and the evolution of pyramids

Pharaoh = ruler of ancient Egypt; top of politics + society.
  • Early elite tomb: underground chambers with rectangular mastabas on top.
  • Architecture change over time: stacked mastabas → gradually became pyramids.
  • Example to name: Step Pyramid at Saqqara (shows the “stacked steps”).

Belief in ka and the practice of mummification

Ka = spiritual double; could survive only if the body was preserved.
Mummification = ritual preservation of the corpse so the ka can continue.
  1. Remove internal organs (heart was not removed).
  2. Dry the body using natron (natural drying agent).
  3. Oil the body + wrap in linen.
  4. Coffin + burial with rituals.
Exam trap: don’t say “all organs removed.” It’s all except the heart.

Egyptian social hierarchy

Memorise the exact order (top → bottom) + be ready to compare with other civilisations.
  1. Pharaoh
  2. Government officials / nobles / priests
  3. Free landholders / artisans / merchants
  4. Serfs / slaves
  • Occupation + daily routine evidence: infer jobs + routines from evidence (material culture + depictions help reconstruct social life).

Daily objects as evidence of life and class

  • Everyday objects show: fashion, materials, design, accessories, + how classes used objects differently.

Leisure, religion in public life, and the Sed festival

  • Leisure: swimming, canoeing, board games, music, dancing.
  • Religious processions: statues of gods paraded, with instruments like the sistrum.
Sed festival = celebration of a pharaoh’s 30th regnal year (30 years ruling).

Status of women and Cleopatra

Comparison point: Egyptian women usually had more rights than Greek/Roman women.
  • Rights mentioned: could own property + do business.
Cleopatra (69–30 BCE): trained to rule; became queen at 18.

Chinese Civilisation

Geographic setting and early timeline

  • Grew up along the Huang He (Yellow River) + the Yangtze.
  • Neolithic cultures → about 7000 BCE.

Bronze Age emergence and early empire formation

  • Copper/bronze use (~2000 BCE) pushed Yellow River settlements into the Bronze Age.
  • Urban centres show up ~1600 BCE because of growth in:
    • agriculture
    • metallurgy
    • craft production
  • Result: the region’s first territorial Bronze Age empire.

Dynasties and major political milestones

Memorise the dynasty order + dates — standard exam facts.
  • Shang: 1600–1046 BCE
  • Zhou: 1046–256 BCE
  • Iron becomes widespread by 600 BCE.
  • Qin/Ch’in: 221–206 BCE — unified China + probably gave China its name.
  • Han: 206 BCE–220 CE

Zhou rule: heaven’s appointment and official examinations

Zhou rulers = priest-kings: seen as appointed by heaven, but could be removed if the people didn’t prosper.
  • Selection/testing of officials included exams in:
    • archery
    • horsemanship
    • calculation
    • writing
    • music

Evidence, Script, Crafts and Great Wall

Records and oracle bones (early evidence)

  • Official historiographers left abundant records → key evidence base for Chinese history.
Oracle bones: animal bones / tortoise shells with oracle symbols; heated, then the heat-crack patterns were read to foretell the future.
  • They reveal people’s hopes, desires, fears (the questions/concerns were written down).

Chinese writing system (logographic)

Logographic writing: characters represent words/morphemes, not sounds.
Common confusion: “logographic” ≠ one character = one spoken sound; it mainly links to meaning units (words/morphemes).

Crafts and materials: jade, marble, bronze, and music

  • Jade objects (ritual/prestige): likely helped social transactions; imported jade = outside contact.
  • Marble: used for bird/animal ornaments + wooden-pillar foundations.
  • Bronze: made weapons, tools, + elaborate ritual vessels.
  • Music + craftsmanship evidence: a struck cord-tied jade fish made clear, lasting musical notes → shows:
    • importance of music
    • high skill in craftsmanship

The Great Wall: origin, purpose, and long construction history

Great Wall (built over time): started as separate walls by the Zhou + others (~680 BCE) to stop nomadic raids; later joined, extended, and repaired again and again up to the 17th century CE.
Exam framing: not a one-time build — a long, multi-phase project over centuries.

Silk, Society, Money and Administration

Silk: origins and Silk Route export

  • Silk known in Neolithic China (4000–3000 BCE).
  • Later becomes a Bronze Age craft.
  • Under the Han (in the 2nd century BCE), silk becomes a major export on the Silk Route.
  • Cross-link for revision: Silk Route connections with India + Buddhist connections (as noted in the text).

Chinese Bronze Age social stratification (by 1500 BCE)

By 1500 BCE, society is highly stratified: rulers/nobles/aristocrats over farmers/labourers.

Money and administration innovations

  • Metal exchange begins under the Zhou.
  • Money economy develops by the 5th century BCE.
  • China first introduced paper currency.
  • Also developed civil services based on public examinations.

Shared Pattern of Bronze Age Civilisations

Shared pattern: big civilisations arose independently in fertile river plains.
  • Cause: strong agriculture + access to new resources → surplus.
  • Surplus effects: economic growth + rise of cities.
  • Political/social development: growth of administration + social hierarchy.
  • Role of writing:
    • first for economic/social records
    • later supports literature
  • Continuity: many modern cultural traditions trace back to the Bronze Age.

Chapter Recap (Complete Short Notes)

High-yield checklist: treat each dot point like a short-answer prompt.
  • Prehistory evidence: mainly tools, fossils, cave art; humans evolved in Africa then migrated worldwide.
  • Palaeolithic: foragers using handaxes, cleavers, scrapers; lived in caves + open camps.
  • Mesolithic: used microliths; temporary settlements near rivers/lakes.
  • Neolithic: agriculture + domestication → permanent villages, plus pottery + weaving.
  • Chalcolithic: used copper + stone in growing farming communities.
  • Bronze Age: urban centres + trade, writing, administration, social organisation.
  • Independent civilisations + contributions: Sindhu–Sarasvatī, Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Chinese — all independently contributed to riverside agriculture, writing, architecture, administration, culture.

Key Terms

Homo erectus: upright/bipedal early ancestor. Hominin: modern humans + human-like ancestors. Old World: Africa, Asia, Europe — oldest settlement zone. Fossil: preserved ancient remains/traces/impressions. Metallurgy: extracting + shaping metals. City-state: sovereign city ruling surrounding land. Ziggurat: stepped Sumerian temple; treasury + civic/economic centre. Cuneiform: wedge-mark writing on clay tablets. Papyrus: Egyptian writing sheet from papyrus stems. Mummification: ritual preservation of a corpse. Kemet: Egyptian “the black” (Nile soil). Oracle bones: inscribed + heated bones/shells used for divination in China.
