Chapter 9: The Price Puzzle—What Drives the Market

Introduction: Why Do Prices Change?

Prices change in real life: mangoes get cheaper later in the season; vegetables cost more in the morning and less in the evening; onion prices can jump over months; the same flight seat may be ₹3,000 one day and ₹9,000 another; seasonal/festival discounts happen. These changes are not random.
Prices respond to many influences:
  • Consumer demand
  • Availability or supply
  • Seasons
  • Festivals
  • Trends
  • Expectations
  • Rumours
  • Production conditions
But the main market forces that decide prices are demand and supply.

Demand

1. Meaning of Demand

Demand = the quantity of a product consumers are willing and able to buy at a given price.
Demand depends on:
  • Need
  • Preference
  • Price
  • Income
  • Season
  • Trends
  • Purchasing power
Desire alone ≠ demand. For demand, the buyer must have:
  • Willingness to buy
  • Ability to pay

Purchasing Power

Purchasing power = how much one unit of currency can buy at a particular time.

2. Law of Demand

Law of demand:
  • If price rises → quantity demanded falls (generally).
  • If price falls → quantity demanded rises (generally).
So, price and quantity demanded have an inverse relationship, with other factors unchanged.
Price rises ⇒ Quantity demanded falls Price falls ⇒ Quantity demanded rises Price = money cost per unit of the good Quantity demanded = amount consumers plan to buy at that price Other factors unchanged = income, taste, and other conditions stay constant
In exams, always write the condition “other factors remaining unchanged” (ceteris paribus). Without it, the law of demand is incomplete.

3. Individual Demand

Individual demand = quantity of a good/service that one consumer wants to buy at different prices, with other factors constant.
Worked example: Srivalli’s demand for mangoes (inverse price–quantity link):
  • ₹150 per kg → Srivalli buys 1 kg
  • ₹100 per kg → Srivalli buys 2 kg
  • ₹50 per kg → Srivalli buys 3 kg
This shows the law of demand: as price falls ₹150 → ₹50, quantity demanded rises 1 kg → 3 kg.

4. Demand Schedule and Demand Curve

Demand schedule = quantities demanded at different prices (shown in a table). Plot the schedule on a graph → you get the demand curve.
Demand graph rules:
  • Price on vertical (y) axis
  • Quantity demanded on horizontal (x) axis
  • Join the plotted points → downward-sloping demand curve
  • Downward slope shows the inverse relationship (assuming income, taste and other conditions stay unchanged)
Don’t swap axes in demand diagram: P on Y, Q on X.

Market Demand

5. Meaning of Market Demand

Market demand = total quantity demanded by all consumers at different prices. Found by adding individual demands.
QD = Q1 + Q2 + Q3 + … QD = market quantity demanded at a given price Q1, Q2, Q3, … = quantities demanded by individual consumers at that same price
Worked example: Market demand for mangoes (sum of individuals):
  • ₹150: Srivalli 1 kg + Alex 2 kg + Israt 3 kg = Market demand 6 kg
  • ₹100: Srivalli 2 kg + Alex 4 kg + Israt 6 kg = Market demand 12 kg
  • ₹50: Srivalli 3 kg + Alex 6 kg + Israt 9 kg = Market demand 18 kg
Market demand = horizontal addition of individual demands (add quantities at the same price).

6. Market Demand Curve

Plot total market demand at different prices → market demand curve.
  • It slopes downward, like an individual demand curve.
  • It is usually flatter because it combines many consumers’ reactions.
From the example:
  • Price ₹150 → ₹50: Srivalli rises by 2 kg (1 → 3).
  • Same price fall: market demand rises by 12 kg (6 → 18).
Since many consumers respond to one price change, the market shows a bigger total quantity change than any single buyer → market curve often looks flatter.

Other Determinants of Demand (Demand can change even if price is unchanged)

Demand can change even when the product’s own price stays the same — because other factors shift consumers’ willingness/ability to buy.

7. Prices of Related Goods

Related goods = goods with linked demand: change in price/availability of one affects demand for the other.

A. Substitute Goods

Substitutes = goods that can replace each other.
Examples of substitutes:
  • Tea and coffee
  • Mangoes and bananas
  • Apples and bananas
  • Air conditioners and coolers
  • Laptops and desktop computers
If coffee becomes costlier while tea’s price is unchanged, many buyers shift to tea.
Price of one substitute rises ⇒ Demand for the other rises Substitute = replacement good Demand for the other = quantity consumers want of the alternative good (at its current price)

B. Complementary Goods

Complementary goods = goods used together.
Examples of complements:
  • Smartphone and earphones
  • Car and petrol
  • Printer and cartridge
  • Cinema ticket and popcorn
  • Pencil and eraser
  • Notebook and pen
Demand link examples:
  • If demand for printers rises, demand for cartridges may also rise.
  • If cinema tickets become expensive and fewer people go, demand for cinema-hall popcorn may fall.
Exam trap: don’t mix them up. Substitutes replace each other; complements are consumed together.

8. Consumer Income

  • If household income rises, consumers can generally:
    • Buy more goods
    • Buy better-quality goods
    • Spend with more confidence
  • So, demand for many goods can rise even if prices don’t change.
  • But income does not affect demand for every product in the same way.
Always add the qualifier: income rise generally raises demand for many goods, but the effect varies by product type.

9. Tastes and Preferences

  • Consumers differ in likes, habits, and preferences.
  • If someone strongly prefers mangoes, they may not switch to cheaper oranges.
  • Ads, fashion, and lifestyle changes shape preferences → demand changes.

10. Population Size and Composition

  • Bigger population → higher total market demand (generally).
  • India’s large domestic market supports economic growth.
  • Population composition (age structure) changes what is demanded:
    • More children → more demand for sports shoes + school-related goods
    • More working adults → more demand for formal shoes + professional services
    • More elderly → more demand for comfortable/orthopaedic footwear + healthcare

11. Diminishing Marginal Utility

Principle of diminishing marginal utility: the 1st unit gives high satisfaction; each extra unit usually gives less extra satisfaction than the previous one.
  • When marginal utility falls, willingness to pay falls.
  • So consumers usually buy extra units only at lower prices.
This supports the downward-sloping demand curve: less extra satisfaction from extra units → buyers need lower prices to buy more.

12. Seasonality

Seasonality = demand changes with time due to changing conditions, even if price stays the same.
Demand may vary due to:
  • Weather
  • Festivals
  • Academic sessions
  • Cultural habits
  • Seasonal requirements
Examples:
  • Books at the start of an academic session
  • Sweets during festivals
  • Sweaters and jackets in winter
Seasonal demand shifts can happen without any change in the good’s price.

13. Expectations of Future Prices

  • If consumers expect prices to fall, they may postpone buying → current demand falls.
  • If consumers expect prices to rise, they may buy now → current demand rises.
  • Example: People may delay buying durable goods before Diwali/New Year expecting discounts.
Expectations change current demand: expected fall → wait; expected rise → buy now.

Supply

14. Meaning of Supply

Supply = quantity sellers are willing and able to offer at a given price.
Supply is not just production; it’s what is offered for sale.

15. Law of Supply

Law of supply:
  • Price rises → quantity supplied generally rises.
  • Price falls → quantity supplied generally falls.
So, price and quantity supplied have a direct relationship.
Price rises ⇒ Quantity supplied rises Price falls ⇒ Quantity supplied falls Quantity supplied = amount sellers plan to offer for sale at that price

Reasons (Higher prices)

The text begins the section “Reasons: Higher prices:” here; the detailed reasons continue beyond this portion.

Individual Supply

Individual supply = quantity of a good that one seller is willing and able to offer for sale at different prices (over a given time period, other factors constant).

Seller A’s Supply Schedule (Example)

  • At price ₹50 per kg → quantity supplied = 1 kg
  • At price ₹100 per kg → quantity supplied = 2 kg
  • At price ₹150 per kg → quantity supplied = 3 kg
A supply schedule = a list of price–quantity pairs for a seller; used to draw the supply curve.

Individual Supply Curve

Individual supply curve = graph showing relationship between price and quantity supplied by one seller.
  • Axes: Price on y-axis; Quantity supplied on x-axis.
  • Slope: Since price and supply move together, supply curve usually slopes upward (higher price → higher quantity supplied).
Diagram tip: don’t swap axes — here, price is always on y-axis and quantity on x-axis.

Seasonal Mango Supply

  • Beginning of mango season: supply low → prices high.
  • Middle of the season: supply rises → prices fall.
  • If supply < demand, prices tend to rise.
  • If supply > demand, prices tend to fall.
Seasonality shifts market conditions over time: harvest/availability changes supply and therefore the market price.

Market Supply

Meaning of Market Supply

Market supply = the total quantity of a good offered by all sellers together at different prices.
Market supply: QS = SA + SB + SC + … QS = total quantity supplied in the market (at a given price) SA, SB, SC = quantities supplied by individual sellers A, B, C (at the same price)

Mango Market-Supply Schedule (Worked Example)

  • At ₹50: A = 1 kg, B = 3 kg, C = 2 kg → Market supply = 6 kg
  • At ₹100: A = 2 kg, B = 4 kg, C = 6 kg → Market supply = 12 kg
  • At ₹150: A = 3 kg, B = 7 kg, C = 8 kg → Market supply = 18 kg
Add all individual supplies at each price = market supply. Plot these totals → upward-sloping market supply curve.

Other Determinants of Supply

Determinants of supply (other than the good’s own price) = factors that change how much producers are willing/able to supply at each price.

Prices of Related Goods

  • Producers compare profitability of other goods they could make.
  • Example: if wheat prices are low + chickpea prices are high, farmers may shift to chickpeas next season.
  • Consequences:
    • Chickpea supply ↑
    • Wheat supply may ↓
“Related goods” here = alternative products for producers (what to produce), not consumer substitutes/complements.

Number of Sellers

  • More sellers + more production → market supply ↑
  • If supply > demand → prices tend to fall
  • Fewer sellers → supply ↓
  • If supply < demand → prices tend to rise

Technology

  • Better technology → lower costs + higher productivity.
  • So sellers can supply more at each price (i.e., supply increases).
  • Examples:
    • Drip irrigation
    • Weather sensors
    • Improved seeds
    • Mechanised production
    • Cold-storage facilities
  • Cold storage example: mangoes reach distant markets with less spoilage → market supply ↑
Technology changes supply mainly via costs + effective output (incl. less spoilage/wastage).

Future Expectations of Producers

  • Expect higher future demand → may increase current production
  • Expect lower future demand → may reduce production
  • If wholesalers expect future prices ↑ → may withhold current stock and sell later
  • Example: potato wholesalers may cut present supply expecting peak-season prices
Expectations can change current supply even if current costs/technology don’t change (e.g., withholding stock reduces current market supply).

Other Factors Affecting Supply

Supply may also change due to:
  • Input costs
  • Availability of alternative inputs
  • Resource depletion
  • Weather
  • Natural disasters
  • Taxes
  • Transport conditions
  • Labour costs
  • Government rules
In exam answers, explain supply changes using: costs (inputs, labour, taxes), conditions (weather, disasters, transport), resources (depletion, input availability), policy (government rules), and producer expectations.

Market Equilibrium

Price Determination

  • Buyers show what they’re willing to pay.
  • Sellers show what they’re willing to accept.
  • Market price comes from interaction of demand + supply.

Meaning of Market Equilibrium

Market equilibrium = where quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
Equilibrium condition: QD = QS QD = quantity demanded QS = quantity supplied
At equilibrium:
  • No excess demand
  • No excess supply
  • Market is “cleared”
  • Price stays fairly stable unless outside conditions change

Mango-Market Equilibrium (Worked Example)

  • At ₹40: QD = 38 kg, QS = 6 kg → Excess demand
  • At ₹100: QD = 12 kg, QS = 12 kg → Equilibrium
  • At ₹150: QD = 8 kg, QS = 43 kg → Excess supply
Equilibrium price = ₹100 and equilibrium quantity = 12 kg (because QD = QS).

Excess Demand (Shortage)

Excess demand / shortage = when quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied.
Shortage condition: QD > QS QD = quantity demanded QS = quantity supplied
  • Example (₹40): demand = 38 kg, supply = 6 kg → shortage.
  • Shortage creates upward pressure on price.
  • As price rises, it may:
    • Reduce demand
    • Increase supply
    • Push market towards equilibrium

Excess Supply (Surplus)

Excess supply / surplus = when quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded.
Surplus condition: QS > QD QS = quantity supplied QD = quantity demanded
  • Example (₹150): supply = 43 kg, demand = 8 kg → surplus (unsold stock).
  • Surplus creates downward pressure on price.
  • As price falls, it may:
    • Increase demand
    • Reduce supply
    • Push market towards equilibrium

Equilibrium Graph

  • Demand curve slopes downward
  • Supply curve slopes upward
  • Intersection = equilibrium point E
  • In this chapter’s example, E shows:
    • Price = ₹100
    • Quantity = 12 kg
When drawing the graph, use prices ₹40, ₹100, ₹150 and label each point clearly as shortage, equilibrium, or surplus.

Does Equilibrium Exist in the Real World?

Dynamic Nature of Markets

  • In theory, equilibrium = the exact intersection of demand + supply.
  • Real markets keep changing because of:
    • Technology
    • Wages
    • Interest rates
    • Political events
    • Wars
    • Pandemics
    • Weather
    • Natural disasters
    • Income
    • Consumer trends
  • So equilibrium is rarely permanent.
  • Markets keep adjusting towards new equilibrium points.
Exam idea: treat equilibrium like a moving target — shifts in demand/supply move the intersection, and the market adjusts via price changes.

Face Masks During COVID-19 (Illustration of Adjustment)

  • In 2020, demand for masks jumped suddenly.
  • Supply couldn’t rise immediately.
  • This caused a shortage + sharp price rise.
  • Later, producers expanded supply → prices fell.
  • After the pandemic, demand fell more and prices moved back towards earlier levels.

Hotel Tariffs: Dynamic Pricing

Meaning of Tariff

A hotel room’s price is also called its tariff.
  • Hotels change tariffs based on demand and availability.

Goa Hotel Example (Dynamic Pricing in Practice)

  • A 100-room hotel might charge:
    • Off-season weekday in July: ₹1,500 per night
    • Tourist-season weekend in December: ₹8,000 per night
    • New Year’s Eve: ₹25,000 per night
  • If a big tour group cancels, the hotel may cut prices by 40% overnight to fill rooms.
  • Tariffs can change several times a day.
Applied example: prices adjust frequently as demand/supply conditions change = dynamic pricing.

Factors Affecting Hotel Tariffs

  • Speed of bookings
  • Prices charged by nearby hotels
  • Festivals
  • Conferences
  • Local events
  • Weather forecasts
  • Number of days before arrival
  • Past booking patterns

Revenue

Revenue = total income from selling goods/services before expenses are subtracted.
Other dynamic price examples:
  • Air tickets
  • Taxi fares
  • Event tickets
  • Vegetables
  • Electricity tariffs
  • Online shopping products

Markets and Sustainability

Present Demand and Future Supply

  • Today’s consumer choices can harm future resources.
  • Examples:
    • Fast fashion
    • Overfishing
    • Excessive groundwater extraction
  • Markets shouldn’t focus only on short-term gain.
  • Sustainable consumption protects future supply.
  • If resources run out, future supply falls and equilibrium prices may rise a lot.
Link sustainability to supply: resource depletion shifts supply left (lower supply) → tends to raise equilibrium price in the long run.

Role of Government in the Economy

Nature of the Indian Economy

  • India = a large, market-based and regulated economy.
  • Market prices usually respond to demand and supply.
  • But markets don’t always give fair outcomes.
  • Markets distribute goods by willingness + ability to pay.
  • This can make essentials like medicines unreachable for low-income groups.
  • Government steps in for fairness, equity, and social welfare.

Regulation of Unfair Practices

Protection of Economic Participants

  • Government regulates markets to protect:
    • Consumers
    • Workers
    • Producers
    • Vulnerable and low-income groups

Price Ceiling

Price ceiling = the highest legal price a seller can charge.
  • Used for essential goods (e.g., medicines) to stop overcharging.
  • If set below equilibrium, it can:
    • Increase demand
    • Reduce supply
    • Cause a shortage
Key exam linkage: a binding price ceiling (below equilibrium price) gives QD > QS → shortage.

Price Floor

Price floor = the lowest legal price for a good, service, or labour.
  • Effectiveness condition: A price floor is effective (binding) only if set above equilibrium price. If below equilibrium, it won’t change the market outcome.
  • Example: Minimum wage = a price floor to protect workers, so wages don’t fall below a legal minimum.
  • Possible market outcome: Can create excess supply when QS > QD at the floor price.
Exam tip: Always write “above equilibrium” for an effective price floor + link it to excess supply (surplus).

Monopoly

Monopoly = a market run by one seller/producer with no close substitutes.
  • Market power: monopolist can strongly influence price + output.
  • How monopoly can harm consumers:
    • Higher prices
    • Restricted supply
    • Lower quality
    • Less consumer choice
  • Government response: may regulate monopolies by monitoring prices, competition, and quantity supplied.
Exam must include: (1) definition (one seller, no close substitutes) + (2) at least two consumer harms + idea of regulation.

Market Regulators

Market regulators = bodies that supervise markets to ensure transparency, accountability, and fairness.

Reserve Bank of India (RBI)

  • Regulates banking and monetary institutions.

Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA)

  • Protects consumer rights.
  • Takes action against unfair trade practices.

Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI)

  • Regulates the telecommunications sector.

Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)

  • Regulates the securities market.
Common mistake: mixing up regulators. Fix it as: RBI–banks, CCPA–consumers, TRAI–telecom, SEBI–securities.

Sanitiser Prices During COVID-19

Market Problem

  • Demand for sanitisers shot up.
  • Supply ran out → shortage.
  • Prices increased.
  • Some sellers reacted by:
    • Hoarding
    • Black marketing

Hoarding

Hoarding = storing/accumulating too much goods or money, usually because people expect shortages or price rises.

Black Marketing

Black marketing = illegal buying/selling of prohibited, scarce, or regulated goods/services.

Government Response

  • Sanitisers declared essential commodities under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955.
  • MRP fixed at ₹100 for a 200 ml bottle → a price ceiling/price control.
  • New companies started making sanitisers.
  • Supply expanded → sanitisers became widely available at fairer prices.

Effects of Temporary Price Control

  • For consumers:
    • Stops extreme overcharging
    • Improves affordability
  • For suppliers:
    • Caps the price they can charge
    • Can reduce profit incentive if continued too long
Conclusion to write in exams: Emergency price controls help if used temporarily, but permanent controls can discourage production and create shortages.

Provision of Public Goods

Meaning of Public Goods

Public goods/services = goods/services provided or funded by the government for the benefit of society.
  • Examples:
    • Roads
    • Bridges
    • Public parks
    • Streetlights
    • National defence
    • Sanitation
    • Drainage
    • Police services

Why Private Markets May Underprovide Them

  • Public goods often don’t give direct private profit.
  • People may benefit even if they don’t pay.
  • This creates the free-rider problem (benefit without paying) → under-provision.

Neighbourhood-Park Example (Free-Rider Problem)

  • A park benefits lots of families.
  • Each family must contribute ₹5,000.
  • Some families may wait for others to pay, expecting to use it free later.
  • So total contributions become insufficient → park not built.
  • Government funding fixes this and supports:
    • Social welfare
    • Equal access
    • Economic development

Public-Goods Activity (What to be able to do)

  • Identify two publicly provided goods/services and explain:
    1. Who benefits
    2. Why private provision is difficult
    3. What problems would arise if government stopped providing them
Exam structure tip: For “why govt provides public goods,” always write: no direct profit → free-rider problem → under-provision → govt provision/funding.

Limitations of Government Intervention

Govt regulation is sometimes needed, but too much intervention can cause adverse effects.

Price Distortions and Reduced Incentives

  • If govt fixes prices below market price, producers get lower returns.
  • Example (wheat):
    • Market price of wheat = ₹30/kg
    • Govt maximum price = ₹20/kg
  • Likely producer responses:
    • Reduce production
    • Shift to other crops
    • Cut investment
    • Supply less
  • Result: may create shortages.

Compliance Burdens

  • Intervention may require:
    • Licences
    • Permits
    • Inspections
    • Forms
    • Regulatory clearances
  • Problem: small businesses may find the time + cost too heavy.
  • Example (restaurant approvals):
    • Food safety
    • Fire safety
    • Pollution control
    • Local government rules
  • Consequence: too much compliance can stop entrepreneurs from starting or expanding businesses.

Ease of Doing Business

Ease of doing business = how easy it is to start, run, and close a business.
  • Depends on regulation, bureaucracy, and legal systems.

Reduced Innovation and Entrepreneurship

  • Heavy price controls/regulation can reduce incentives to:
    • Invest
    • Experiment
    • Adopt technology
    • Improve production
  • Agriculture example: if farmers can’t earn enough, they may avoid spending on better seeds/irrigation.
  • Long-run result: productivity + output may fall.

Democratic Decision-Making

A democracy should choose the type + extent of intervention by thinking about all stakeholders and long-term public welfare (not just short-run price changes).
  • Stakeholders to consider:
    • Consumers
    • Producers
    • Workers
    • Small businesses
    • Vulnerable communities
    • Long-term public welfare
  • Intervention should balance:
    • Fairness
    • Affordability
    • Producer incentives
    • Competition
    • Innovation
    • Sustainability

Applying Demand and Supply

Income and Demand

  • Income ↑ does not mean demand ↑ for all goods.
  • Income ↑ → demand for normal / higher-quality goods tends to ↑.
  • Income ↑ → demand for some lower-quality / less-preferred alternatives can ↓ (people switch to better options).
Exam phrasing: “As income rises, demand rises for normal goods, but may fall for inferior/lower-quality substitutes.”

Doubling of Petrol Prices

  • If petrol prices double, likely demand-side effects:
    • Diesel cars: demand may ↑ if diesel stays relatively cheaper.
    • Electric cars: demand may ↑.
    • Car accessories: demand may ↓ if overall car use/purchases fall.
    • Public transport: demand may ↑.
  • Dependence factors: depends on prices of related fuels, vehicle costs, and consumer preferences.

Drip-Irrigation Technology

  • Given: drip irrigation cuts water use by 40% and increases yield by 30%.
  • Cost + supply effect: cost per unit may ↓, so farmers are willing to supply more at each price.
  • If many farmers adopt it → market supply ↑.
  • Graph: supply curve shifts right (more quantity supplied).
  • New equilibrium: price may fall and quantity may rise.

Online Festival Sales

  • Why prices can be low:
    • Bulk sales
    • Clearance of older inventory
    • Competition among sellers
    • Lower digital selling costs
    • Promotional strategies
    • Expectations of sharply increased demand
  • Demand effect: price ↓ → quantity demanded ↑.
  • How sellers may still benefit:
    • Higher sales volume
    • Inventory clearance
    • Customer acquisition
    • Increased market share

Vaccine Price Ceiling

  • A maximum vaccine price set below equilibrium will likely cause a shortage.
  • Reason (demand): price ↓ → quantity demanded ↑.
  • Reason (supply): producers get less → quantity supplied may ↓.
  • Policy implication: government procurement, subsidies, or production support may be needed to keep supply going.
For any price ceiling below equilibrium, write the full chain: price ↓ → Qd ↑ and Qs ↓ → shortage, then add possible government steps to stop supply collapse.

Taxes on Harmful Goods

  • Governments impose high taxes on tobacco and alcohol to:
    • Raise prices
    • Reduce consumption
    • Promote healthier choices
    • Recover some social + healthcare costs
  • Similar controls may be used for other harmful, scarce, or essential products.

Interpreting an Equilibrium Graph

Point E (Market Equilibrium)

Point E = intersection of demand and supply.
  • It shows market equilibrium (market-clearing point).
  • At equilibrium: Qd = Qs.
In Fig. 9.8, equilibrium is approx:
  • Equilibrium price ≈ ₹200
  • Equilibrium quantity ≈ 30 kg

Upper Price Line (High Price Above Equilibrium)

  • Point A (on demand curve) = Qd at the higher price.
  • Point B (on supply curve) = Qs at the same higher price.
  • Price > equilibrium → Qs > Qd.
  • Gap A to B = excess supply = surplus.
Exam tip: Price above equilibrium ⇒ surplus / excess supply (S > D). Don’t mix it up with shortage.

Lower Price Line (Low Price Below Equilibrium)

  • Point C (on supply curve) = Qs at the lower price.
  • Point F (on demand curve) = Qd at the same lower price.
  • Price < equilibrium → Qd > Qs.
  • Gap C to F = excess demand = shortage.
If a low price continues in a free market, buyer competition can push price up toward equilibrium.
Exam tip: Price below equilibrium ⇒ shortage / excess demand (D > S). This creates upward pressure on price in a free market.

Numerical Equilibrium Exercise (Using a Schedule)

Given Schedule: Identifying Equilibrium

Equilibrium (from a schedule) = where Qd = Qs at the same price.
Given data (Price; Quantity demanded; Quantity supplied):
  • ₹10; 5 kg; 25 kg
  • ₹20; 10 kg; 20 kg
  • ₹30; 15 kg; 15 kg
  • ₹40; 20 kg; 10 kg
  • ₹50; 25 kg; 5 kg
  • Equilibrium price = ₹30
  • Equilibrium quantity = 15 kg

Calculating Excess Supply and Excess Demand from the Schedule

  • Excess supply (surplus): when Qs > Qd
  • Excess demand (shortage): when Qd > Qs
At ₹20:
  • Demand = 10 kg
  • Supply = 20 kg
  • Excess supply = 10 kg (20 − 10)
At ₹40:
  • Demand = 20 kg
  • Supply = 10 kg
  • Excess demand = 10 kg (20 − 10)
Common mistake: For “excess”, always find the difference at that price + label it correctly: surplus (S − D) or shortage (D − S).

Note on Unusual Shapes in the Plotted Data

In this exercise, the figures give an unusual pattern:
  • Demand seems to move upward with price.
  • Supply seems to move downward with price.
Even if it looks odd, plot exactly what’s given and mark equilibrium where both quantities = 15 kg.
Exam tip: Don’t “fix” numbers to match theory. In schedule/numerical Qs, equilibrium is strictly where Qd = Qs in the given data.

Chapter Summary (Demand, Supply, Equilibrium, and Policy)

Demand and the Law of Demand

  • Demand = quantity consumers are willing + able to buy.
  • Law of Demand: inverse relation between price and Qd (P↑ → Qd↓, P↓ → Qd↑).
Determinants of demand (demand depends on):
  • Income
  • Population
  • Preferences
  • Related goods
  • Seasons
  • Utility
  • Future expectations

Supply and the Law of Supply

  • Supply = quantity sellers are willing + able to offer.
  • Law of Supply: direct relation between price and Qs (P↑ → Qs↑, P↓ → Qs↓).
Determinants of supply (supply is influenced by):
  • Related-goods prices
  • Sellers
  • Technology
  • Inputs
  • Expectations
  • Weather and disasters

Market Equilibrium, Shortages, and Surpluses

  • Market equilibrium = where Qd = Qs.
  • In shortage (excess demand), prices tend to rise.
  • In surplus (excess supply), prices tend to fall.
  • Real markets are dynamic → keep moving towards new equilibrium.

Why Government Intervention May Be Required

Government intervention may be needed when markets:
  • Make essential goods unaffordable
  • Encourage exploitation
  • Permit monopoly abuse
  • Underprovide public goods
  • Produce seriously unequal outcomes

Government Measures and Risks of Excessive Intervention

Government measures include:
  • Price ceilings
  • Price floors
  • Regulation
  • Taxation
  • Public provision
Too much intervention may:
  • Distort prices
  • Reduce supply
  • Impose compliance costs
  • Discourage innovation

Balancing Goals in Sound Economic Policy

Sound economic policy = balance:
  • Consumer welfare
  • Producer incentives
  • Equality
  • Competition
  • Long-term sustainability
