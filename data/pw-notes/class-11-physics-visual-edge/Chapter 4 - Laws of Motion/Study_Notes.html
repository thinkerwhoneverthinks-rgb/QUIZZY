TOPIC1

FORCE AND MOTION

1Definition & Introduction

  • Force is an external agency that can change a body’s state of rest or motion.
  • Force can start motion, stop motion, or retard (reduce speed) a moving body.
  • Force can change speed, direction, or both; motion changes show force is acting.
  • The external agency may be in contact with the object or act from a distance.
  • In everyday life, forces often appear needed to keep motion due to friction/drag opposing motion.

2Diagram

Contact forces act via touch; non-contact forces (gravity, magnetism) act across distance.

3Key Concepts

  • 🦵Kicking a football: applied force can take a body from rest to motion.
  • ✋Stopping a rolling ball: force opposite motion causes retardation and can stop it.
  • 🌍Dropping a stone: gravitational pull accelerates it downward without contact.
  • 🧲Magnet attracts nail: magnetic attraction acts across a gap (non-contact).
  • ⛸️Uniform straight motion question: is force needed to keep it going? Think net force and friction.

4WHAT FORCE CAN DO (MOTION CHANGES)

  • Start motion: a kick makes a resting ball move.
  • Stop motion: opposing force brings a moving object to rest.
  • Retard: force opposite velocity reduces speed (e.g. brakes, friction).
  • Change direction: turning needs force even if speed stays same.
  • Key exam idea: force relates to change in motion, not necessarily motion itself.

5UNIFORM MOTION QUESTION (WHY IT FEELS CONFUSING)

  • On Earth, friction/air drag oppose motion, so you keep applying force to maintain speed.
  • If friction were absent, no continuous push is needed for uniform straight motion.
  • Uniform motion can happen with net external force = 0; forces may still be present but cancel.
  • Example: a book at rest has weight down and normal up; net external force is zero.

6CONTACT FORCE vs NON-CONTACT FORCE

Basis Contact force Non-contact force
Needs physical touch? Yes, bodies interact by contact No, acts across distance

Everyday examples kick/push, friction while sliding, air resistance falling stone (gravity), magnet attracts nail

7Solved Q&A

1
A skater moves straight on smooth ice with nearly constant speed. Is an external force required to keep the skater moving? Explain.
Answer:
If speed and direction are constant, there is no acceleration.
In ideal frictionless motion, net external force must be zero, so no continuous force is required to keep moving.
On real ice, small friction/air drag exist; a small push is needed only to balance these opposing forces.
✓  No continuous external force is required in the ideal case; in reality a small force may be needed to overcome friction/drag.
2
Classify the force in each situation as contact or non-contact: (i) wind pushing a sailing boat, (ii) stone falling after release, (iii) magnet attracting a nail, (iv) braking a bicycle.
Answer:
(i) Wind exerts force through air molecules hitting the sail: contact force.
(ii) Fall is due to Earth’s gravitational pull: non-contact force.
(iii) Attraction by a magnet across a gap: non-contact force.
(iv) Brakes use friction between surfaces: contact force.
✓  (i) Contact (ii) Non-contact (iii) Non-contact (iv) Contact.

8⭐Important Points

  • Force is an external agency: internal forces within a body cannot change its overall motion alone.
  • Force is required to change motion (start/stop/retard/turn), not to sustain uniform straight motion ideally.
  • In real life, the need of continuous push usually indicates friction/drag opposing motion.
  • Non-contact forces act without touch: gravity and magnetism are standard examples.
  • Always distinguish: forces may exist, yet net external force can be zero (balanced forces).

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Saying: force is needed to keep any body moving (confusing due to friction).
  • Mixing up contact vs non-contact (e.g. thinking wind is non-contact).
  • Assuming: if two forces are equal and opposite, the object must be at rest (it can move uniformly).
  • Ignoring friction/air resistance when explaining why objects stop on their own.

10💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: "SSTT" for what force can change”
Start motion, Stop motion, Turn (direction), Throttle down (retard). If none of SSTT happens, suspect balanced forces (net external force zero).

TOPIC2

ARISTOTLE’S FALLACY

1Definition & Introduction

  • Aristotle’s fallacy: the incorrect idea that an external force is required to keep a body in motion.
  • Everyday observation (toy car stops unless pulled) seems to support it, but the stopping is due to friction/drag.
  • For uniform motion on a horizontal surface, applied force often just balances friction, so net external force is zero.
  • If friction were absent, no force would be needed to maintain uniform straight-line motion (constant velocity).
  • Galileo’s idealised frictionless experiments led to the correct concept: rest and uniform linear motion are equivalent states (both have zero acceleration).

2Diagram

In uniform motion on a rough surface, applied force balances friction; net external force is zero.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧊Frictionless idealisation reveals the true law; real-world friction masks it.
  • ⚖️Uniform motion can occur with net external force = 0 (forces may exist but cancel).
  • 🧱Friction (solids) and viscous drag (fluids) oppose motion; force is applied to overcome them.
  • 🎯Galileo’s insight is the foundation of Newton’s first law (law of inertia).

4WHAT ARISTOTLE CLAIMED (AND WHY IT SEEMS TRUE)

  • Claim: motion needs a continuous external cause (force) to sustain it.
  • Common experience: a toy car stops when you stop pulling.
  • Misinterpretation: stopping is blamed on “absence of force” rather than presence of opposing forces.

5GALILEO’S CORRECTION (CORE IDEA)

  • Real reason for stopping: friction/air resistance provide a force opposite to velocity.
  • On a frictionless horizontal surface: no acceleration and no retardation → constant velocity.
  • Rest and uniform straight-line motion are equivalent: both correspond to zero acceleration.
  • To find the true law, imagine an ideal world with friction removed (conceptual experiment).

6ARISTOTELIAN VIEW vs GALILEO/NEWTON VIEW

Basis Aristotle Galileo/Newton
Uniform motion Needs continuous external force Needs zero net external force
Why objects stop Because force is not applied Because friction/drag opposes motion
Role of friction Not recognised as the key cause of stopping Central: masks inertia in daily life
Correct general statement Force maintains motion Force changes motion (causes acceleration)

7Solved Q&A

1
A puck slides on a very smooth horizontal ice surface with nearly constant speed. Is a net external force required to keep it moving?
Answer:
If speed and direction are constant, acceleration is zero.
By Newton’s first law, zero acceleration implies net external force is zero.
Any small force needed in practice is only to compensate small friction/air drag.
✓  No. For uniform straight-line motion, net external force is zero; only friction/drag (if present) needs to be balanced.
2
A toy car on the floor stops after you stop pulling it. Does this prove Aristotle’s claim?
Answer:
When you stop pulling, friction from the floor still acts opposite motion.
That unbalanced friction gives a nonzero net force opposite velocity, producing retardation.
If friction were absent, the car would keep moving uniformly after release (idealised case).
✓  No. The car stops due to friction (an opposing external force), not because motion inherently needs a sustaining force.

8⭐Important Points

  • Aristotle’s fallacy = confusing “force needed to overcome friction” with “force needed to maintain motion”.
  • Uniform velocity (straight line) means acceleration = 0, so net external force must be 0.
  • Forces can be present yet cancel: net force matters for change of motion.
  • Galileo used idealised frictionless reasoning to uncover the law of inertia.
  • In practice, friction can never be fully eliminated; hence continuous push is often needed on Earth.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Thinking that any moving body must have a net force in the direction of motion.
  • Forgetting that friction/drag is an external force opposing motion, causing the observed slowing.
  • Saying “forces cancel, therefore it is at rest” instead of “it is unaccelerated, therefore net force is zero”.
  • Assuming that if applied force is removed, the body must immediately come to rest (ignoring inertia).

10💡Memory Technique

“FORCE DOESN’T KEEP, IT CHANGES”
Keep: uniform motion needs no net force. Change: acceleration/retardation needs net force. On Earth, you keep pushing mainly to fight friction.

TOPIC3

LAW OF INERTIA (GALILEO)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Law of inertia: A body stays at rest or in uniform straight-line motion unless a net external force acts.
  • Rest and uniform linear motion are equivalent states: both have zero acceleration.
  • Galileo inferred this by imagining frictionless motion, unlike everyday motion with friction.
  • On a frictionless horizontal plane, a moving object has neither retardation nor acceleration.
  • Inertia means resistance to change of the state of motion (speed or direction).

2 Diagram

Galileo’s double-inclined-plane idea leads to uniform motion on a frictionless horizontal plane.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧠Aristotle’s error: mistook friction’s effect as a need for force to maintain uniform motion.
  • 🧪Inclined plane: down the slope speeds up; up the slope slows down; horizontal is the limiting case.
  • ⚖️If forces cancel (net external force zero), motion cannot change: no acceleration.
  • 🎢Double-inclined plane: ball tends to regain the same height; reducing the second slope increases travel distance.
  • ∞Second slope → 0 (horizontal): to reach the same height needs infinite distance ⇒ motion never ceases (ideal).

4GALILEO’S REASONING (WHAT THE EXPERIMENT SHOWS)

  • Down an incline: gravity component along the plane increases speed (accelerates).
  • Up an incline: the same component opposes motion, so speed decreases (retards).
  • Horizontal is the limiting case where the along-plane component becomes zero.
  • With no friction, no cause remains to change the speed on a horizontal plane.

5DOUBLE-INCLINED-PLANE LIMITING ARGUMENT

  • Ball released from rest rolls down plane 1 and climbs plane 2.
  • With smoother planes, final height approaches the initial height; never exceeds it.
  • Decreasing plane 2 slope: the same height is reached, but over a longer distance.
  • Plane 2 becomes horizontal: to reach the same height needs infinite distance ⇒ motion never ceases (ideal).

Role of friction Not identified as the real cause of stopping Key reason why moving bodies come to rest on Earth
Horizontal motion (ideal) Would stop without sustaining force Would remain at constant velocity indefinitely
Rest vs uniform motion Different in nature Equivalent: both imply a = 0

7Solved Q&A

1
A coin on a smooth (nearly frictionless) table is given a push and then left alone. What happens to its motion ideally, and why does it stop in real life?
Answer:
Ideally, after the push ends, no net external horizontal force acts, so its velocity does not change.
So it continues in uniform straight-line motion (same speed and direction).
In real life, friction and air resistance provide an opposing external force, causing it to slow down and stop.
✓  Ideally it keeps moving with constant velocity; in reality it stops due to friction/drag.
2
A book is at rest on a table. Does this mean no forces act on it? Use the law of inertia to justify your answer.
Answer:
Forces do act: weight acts downward and the normal reaction acts upward.
The book is observed to be at rest (zero acceleration).
Therefore, by the law of inertia, the net external force must be zero, meaning the forces balance.
✓  Forces are present but cancel; net external force is zero, so the book remains at rest.

8⭐Important Points

  • Inertia is not a force; it is a property: resistance to change of motion.
  • Zero net external force means no acceleration, not necessarily zero velocity.
  • Rest and uniform straight-line motion are both non-accelerated states.
  • On Earth, applied force is often needed only to balance friction/drag so that net force becomes zero.
  • Galileo’s key step was idealising frictionless motion to infer the true law.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Saying: force is required to keep motion even when friction is absent.
  • Confusing ‘no net force’ with ‘no forces present’; balanced forces can exist.
  • Thinking inertia means ‘tendency to stop’; it resists any change (start/stop/turn).
  • Concluding: net force zero ⇒ velocity must be zero (wrong); correct is velocity remains constant.

10💡Memory Technique

“GALI: Glide Always, Less Interference”
Galileo imagined frictionless glide. Absent net force ⇒ no change in velocity. Linear uniform motion persists. Inertia resists change, not motion itself.

TOPIC4

NEWTON’S FIRST LAW (LAW OF INERTIA)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Newton’s First Law: A body remains at rest or moves with uniform velocity in a straight line unless a net external force acts on it.
  • Equivalent statement: if the net external force on a body is zero, then its acceleration is zero.
  • Rest and uniform linear motion are equivalent states: both have a = 0.
  • Inertia is the tendency of a body to resist change in its state of rest or uniform straight-line motion.
  • On Earth, forces often act but may cancel vectorially, giving zero net external force.

2 Diagram

Two classic First-Law cases: balanced forces (book) and negligible external force (spaceship) both give a=0.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧮Force-balance idea: ΣFext = 0 ⇔ a = 0.
  • 📚Book on table: upward normal R balances weight W ⇒ ΣFext = 0.
  • 🚀Spaceship with engines off (far from stars): ΣFext ≈ 0 ⇒ velocity remains constant.
  • 🚌Bus start/stop jerks: parts of your body resist sudden change of velocity (inertia).
  • 🧲If motion is unaccelerated, First Law lets you infer ΣFext = 0 even when individual forces are unknown.

4FORCE-BALANCE REASONING (WHAT TO WRITE IN EXAMS)

  • If a body is at rest or in uniform straight-line motion, then a = 0.
  • From First Law: a = 0 ⇒ ΣFext = 0 (external forces cancel vectorially).
  • For a book on a table: forces are W downward and R upward; hence magnitudes are equal and directions opposite.
  • From ΣFext = 0 you can infer a = 0, but rest needs the additional fact that initial v = 0.

5INERTIA EXAMPLES (DAILY LIFE)

  • Bus starts suddenly: feet move with bus (friction), upper body tends to keep earlier state ⇒ backward jerk.
  • Bus stops suddenly: feet stop first, upper body tends to keep moving ⇒ forward jerk.
  • Inertia is not a force; it is a property of matter (resistance to change in motion).
  • In deep space with engines off, velocity does not “die out” because opposing forces are negligible.

Acceleration a = 0 a = 0
Net external force ΣFext = 0 ΣFext = 0
What changes this state? Only a nonzero net external force Only a nonzero net external force

7Solved Q&A

1
A book is resting on a horizontal table. What can you conclude about the net external force on the book? Are there forces acting on it?
Answer:
Since the book is at rest, its acceleration is zero.
By Newton’s First Law, zero acceleration implies ΣFext = 0.
Forces do act: weight W acts downward and normal reaction R acts upward; they cancel vectorially.
✓  Net external force is zero, but forces are present (typically W downward and R upward).
2
A spaceship far from stars switches off its engines. If the net external force on it is (approximately) zero, what happens to its motion?
Answer:
Newton’s First Law states: ΣFext = 0 ⇒ a = 0.
If acceleration is zero, the velocity does not change.
So it continues with the same velocity (same speed and direction) it already had.
✓  Acceleration becomes zero; the spaceship continues in uniform straight-line motion with constant velocity.

8⭐Important Points

  • First Law deals with the special case ΣFext = 0; it expresses inertial behaviour.
  • If ΣFext = 0, then a = 0; velocity may be zero or nonzero.
  • On Earth, an object can be at rest even with forces present because forces can balance.
  • Use First Law in both directions: known ΣFext = 0 ⇒ infer a = 0; known a = 0 ⇒ infer ΣFext = 0.
  • Jerk sensations in buses occur because our body tends to maintain its previous state of motion (inertia).

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Saying “ΣF=0 means no forces act”; actually forces may act but cancel vectorially.
  • Saying “ΣF=0 means the body must be at rest”; it could be moving with constant velocity.
  • Thinking a moving body needs a force to keep moving; force is needed to change velocity, not to maintain it.
  • Using ΣFext but accidentally including internal forces of the system.

10💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: "Z-A-C"”
Zero net force ⇒ Acceleration zero ⇒ velocity Constant. In a jerk (accelerating bus), your body tends to keep its previous velocity (inertia).

TOPIC5

MOMENTUM AND IMPULSE

1Definition & Introduction

  • Momentum of a body: p = mv (vector; direction same as v).
  • Impulse J of a net force over a time interval equals the change in momentum: J = Δp.
  • If force is constant: J = FΔt = m(v−u).
  • For variable force: J = ∫F dt and equals area under F–t graph.
  • SI unit: momentum kg m s−1; impulse N s (numerically the same unit).
  • Large force for short time giving finite Δp is an impulsive force.

2Diagram

Same Δp achieved with larger Δt needs smaller average force.

3Key Concepts

  • •p is vector: magnitude mv; direction along velocity.
  • •Mass effect: larger m ⇒ larger momentum for same v ⇒ harder to stop.
  • •Speed effect: larger v ⇒ larger momentum for same m ⇒ larger stopping impulse.
  • •Same Δp in smaller Δt needs larger average force Favg.
  • •Cricketer pulls hands back: increases Δt ⇒ reduces average force on hands for the same stopping impulse.

4FROM MOMENTUM TO FORCE (CORE IDEA)

  • Newton’s second law: net force is proportional to the rate of change of momentum.
  • For constant mass: Δp = mΔv; direction of Δp equals direction of net impulse.
  • Average force: Favg = ΔpΔt.
  • If you stop an object in half the time, required Favg doubles (same Δp).

5IMPULSE IN COLLISIONS (WHAT EXAMS LOVE)

  • During a hit/catch, contact time is small; force may be huge but impulse stays finite.
  • Direction: impulse is along change in momentum, not always along initial momentum.
  • If a ball rebounds, |Δp| is larger than if it just comes to rest.
  • Use J to avoid unknown force-time details: J = Δp.

9Solved Examples

1
A 0.15 kg cricket ball moving at 20 m s−1 is caught and brought to rest in 0.10 s. Find (i) impulse on the ball, (ii) average force on the ball (magnitude).
Solution:
Given m=0.15 kg, u=20 m s−1, v=0, Δt=0.10 s.
Impulse J = Δp = m(v−u) = 0.15(0−20) = −3.0 kg m s−1.
Magnitude |J| = 3.0 N s; direction opposite to initial motion.
Average force magnitude: |Favg| = |Δp|Δt = 3.00.10 = 30 N.
✓  (i) J = −3.0 N s (opposite to motion), magnitude 3.0 N s
(ii) |Favg| = 30 N
2
A 1200 kg car moving at 15 m s−1 is brought to rest in 6.0 s by brakes. Find (i) impulse, (ii) average retarding force.
Solution:
Given m=1200 kg, u=15 m s−1, v=0, Δt=6.0 s.
Impulse J = Δp = m(v−u) = 1200(0−15) = −18000 kg m s−1.
So |J| = 1.8×104 N s; direction opposite to motion.
Average force: Favg = ΔpΔt = −180006.0 = −3000 N.
✓  (i) J = −1.8×104 N s
(ii) Favg = −3.0×103 N (retarding)

10⭐Important Points

  • Momentum depends on both mass and velocity; doubling either doubles |p|.
  • Impulse is not force; it is change in momentum (equals FΔt only when F is constant).
  • F = dp/dt is general; F=ma only when m is constant.
  • Stopping safely: increase stopping time (airbags, bending knees) to reduce average force.
  • Rebound cases have larger |Δp| than just stopping, hence larger impulse needed.

11❌Common Mistakes

  • Treating momentum as scalar and ignoring direction/sign of velocity.
  • Using F=ma blindly even when mass changes (e.g., rocket fuel loss).
  • Confusing impulse (J) with impulsive force; impulse can be finite even if time is tiny.
  • Forgetting that N s and kg m s−1 are equivalent units.

12💡Memory Technique

NEWTON’S SECOND LAW

1Definition & Introduction

  • Newton’s Second Law: net external force is proportional to rate of change of momentum and along it.
  • Mathematically: F ∝ Δp/Δt ⇒ F = dpdt (choose proportionality constant k=1 in SI).
  • For constant mass m: p=mv ⇒ F=ma.
  • SI unit: 1 newton (N) is the force giving 1 kg mass an acceleration 1 m s−2.
  • Vector law: applies component-wise: Fx=max, Fy=may, Fz=maz (for constant m).
  • Local law: acceleration depends on net external force at that instant and point; no “memory” of past motion.

2Diagram

x F (vertical)If F has no x-component, vx stays constant
Vector & component nature: if net force has zero x-component, x-component of velocity stays constant.

3Key Concepts

  • 📈More force → faster change of momentum: same Δp in smaller Δt needs larger F.
  • 🧭Direction: F is along dpdt; it can change speed, direction, or both.
  • 🧩Component idea (fixed axes): if net Fx=0 then ax=0, so vx remains constant.
  • 🎯Projectile (no air): net force has no x-component ⇒ ax=0 ⇒ horizontal velocity stays constant.
  • 🧪Consistency: if F=0 ⇒ a=0 (matches Newton’s First Law).

4DERIVATION (EXAM-SAFE, SHORT)

  • Define momentum: p=mv (vector).
  • Newton’s statement: F ∝ Δp/Δt and along Δp.
  • In the limit: F=kdpdt; choose k=1 by defining SI unit of force.
  • For constant m: dpdt=d(mv)dt=mdvdt=ma ⇒ F=ma.

5LOCAL & VECTOR CONSEQUENCES

  • Local: a(t) is determined by net external F(t) at that instant, at that location.
  • If net F is perpendicular to v at an instant, speed does not change at that instant; only direction changes (e.g., uniform circular motion).
  • For fixed axes: if net external force has zero component along an axis, the acceleration component along that axis is zero.
  • Projectile (no air): net force only in y (gravity) ⇒ ax=0 ⇒ vx=constant.

9Solved Examples

1
A 2 kg block starts from rest on a frictionless floor. A constant horizontal force of 10 N acts for 3 s. Find (i) acceleration, (ii) final speed, (iii) change in momentum.
Solution:
Given m=2 kg, F=10 N ⇒ a=Fm=102=5 m s−2.
From rest: v=u+at=0+5×3=15 m s−1.
Δp=mΔv=2×15=30 kg m s−1 (same direction as F).
Check via momentum form: Δp=FΔt=10×3=30 N s = 30 kg m s−1.
✓  a=5 m s−2, v=15 m s−1, Δp=30 kg m s−1.
2
A 0.50 kg ball moving east at 8 m s−1 is acted upon by a constant northward force of 2 N for 4 s (ignore other forces). Find the final velocity vector and speed.
Solution:
Force only along north ⇒ ay=Fym=20.50=4 m s−2.
Initial components: vx=8 m s−1, vy=0.
Since Fx=0 ⇒ ax=0 ⇒ vx unchanged = 8 m s−1.
vy=0+ayt=4×4=16 m s−1 (north).
Speed = √(vx2+vy2)=√(82+162)=√320≈17.9 m s−1.
✓  Final velocity = 8 m s−1 east + 16 m s−1 north; speed ≈ 17.9 m s−1.

10⭐Important Points

  • Always use net external force in F=dpdt.
  • F=ma is valid when mass is constant (typical mechanics).
  • Second law is vector: resolve forces and accelerations into components and keep signs.
  • 1 N = 1 kg m s−2; dimensional check: [F] = M L T−2.
  • Local nature: acceleration at an instant depends on force at that instant, not on previous acceleration.

11❌Common Mistakes

  • Using F=ma blindly when mass changes; start from F=dpdt with a correct choice of system and mass flow.
  • Taking F as a single applied force, ignoring other external forces (weight, normal, friction).
  • Treating the law as scalar: forgetting directions/signs in components.
  • Assuming "force changes only one velocity component" without checking whether the force has a component along that axis.

NEWTON'S THIRD LAW

1Definition & Introduction

  • For two interacting bodies A and B, the force on A by B is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force on B by A.
  • Action and reaction are the two forces of the same interaction.
  • The two forces act simultaneously during the interaction.
  • They act on different bodies, so they do not cancel each other for the motion of a single chosen body.
  • In an isolated system, internal forces cancel in pairs, which supports conservation of momentum.

2Diagram

Interaction between two bodies produces an equal-and-opposite force pair on different bodies.

3Key Concepts

  • ⚖️Pair property: interaction forces occur in equal-magnitude, opposite-direction pairs
  • 🧍Different bodies: FAB acts on A, FBA acts on B
  • ⏱️Simultaneous: both forces exist at the same instant during interaction
  • 🧩Internal-force cancellation: within a system, action–reaction pairs sum to zero
  • 🌍Applies to contact (normal, friction, tension) and non-contact (gravity, electric) interactions

4ACTION–REACTION: WHAT IT MEANS (AND DOES NOT)

  • Correct statement: force on A by B equals the negative of force on B by A.
  • No time gap: both forces appear together during the interaction.
  • No cancellation on one body: only one force of the pair acts on any chosen body.
  • Pairs can be contact (push, normal, friction, tension) or non-contact (gravity).

5EXAM APPLICATIONS

  • Book on table: table on book (upward normal); book on table (downward normal).
  • Recoil: gun on bullet (forward); bullet on gun (backward).
  • Walking: foot on ground (backward); ground on person (forward static friction).
  • Ball hits wall: wall on ball changes the ball’s momentum; ball on wall is equal and opposite.

6Solved Q&A

1
A ball hits a smooth vertical wall and rebounds along the same line with the same speed. What is the direction of force on (i) the ball by the wall, (ii) the wall by the ball?
Answer:
Smooth wall ⇒ only normal interaction; no tangential (friction) force from the wall on the ball.
The wall’s force on the ball is perpendicular to the wall and pushes the ball away during contact.
By the third law, the ball exerts an equal-magnitude force on the wall in the opposite direction.
✓  (i) On ball by wall: perpendicular to wall, away from the wall. (ii) On wall by ball: perpendicular to wall, towards the wall.
2
A student says: "When you push a wall, your force and the wall’s reaction cancel, so net force is zero and you cannot accelerate." Identify the mistake and correct it.
Answer:
Mistake: adding action and reaction as if they act on the same body.
Third-law forces act on different bodies: your push acts on the wall; the wall’s force acts on you.
Whether you accelerate depends on the net force on you (wall on you, ground friction, weight, normal, etc.).
✓  Action–reaction do not cancel on one body. Draw the free-body diagram of a single chosen body to find its net force.

7⭐Important Points

  • Third law is about interaction forces, not about equilibrium.
  • Action–reaction forces are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and simultaneous.
  • They act on different bodies: never cancel in a single-body free-body diagram.
  • For a whole system, internal action–reaction forces cancel in the net internal sum; external forces decide system motion.
  • A force exists only due to interaction between two bodies.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Adding action and reaction to claim net force on one body is zero.
  • Thinking "action happens first, reaction later".
  • Pairing wrong forces (e.g., weight of book with normal on the same book; correct pair is Earth-on-book with book-on-Earth).
  • Assuming equal forces imply equal accelerations; accelerations can differ because masses of the two bodies can differ.

CONSERVATION OF MOMENTUM

1Definition & Introduction

  • In an isolated system (net external force zero), the total linear momentum remains constant (vector conservation).
  • For two interacting bodies A and B: pA + pB = constant throughout interaction (vector sum).
  • Internal (mutual) forces occur in pairs and cancel in the net force on the system.
  • Works for all interactions: contact forces, tension, explosions, recoil, collisions.
  • If external impulse is nonzero (e.g. friction, push), total momentum changes by that impulse.

2Diagram

In an isolated system, internal forces cancel in pairs, so total momentum stays constant.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩System approach: apply Newton’s laws to A+B together, not separately
  • 🤝Mutual forces: FAB = −FBA (act on different bodies, same time)
  • 🧲Isolated system: ΣFext = 0 ⇒ Σp conserved
  • 🔫Recoil: gun gains momentum opposite to bullet so total stays constant
  • 💥Collisions: elastic/inelastic both conserve momentum; kinetic energy may not

4DERIVATION (NEWTON’S 2nd + 3rd ⇒ MOMENTUM CONSERVATION)

  • For two bodies: FAB on A by B, and FBA on B by A.
  • Newton 2nd: FAB = dpA/dt, FBA = dpB/dt.
  • Newton 3rd: FAB = −FBA ⇒ dpA/dt = −dpB/dt.
  • So d/dt(pA+pB) = 0 ⇒ pA+pB = constant.
  • With external forces: dP/dt = ΣFext; total momentum changes only due to external impulse.

5APPLICATIONS: RECOIL & COLLISIONS

  • Gun–bullet (initially at rest): pgun + pbullet = 0 after firing (taking initial total momentum as zero).
  • Recoil speed: vgun = −(mb/mg)vb (opposite direction).
  • 1D collision: m1u1+m2u2=m1v1+m2v2.
  • Perfectly inelastic (stick together): final common speed v = (m1u1+m2u2)/(m1+m2).
  • Elastic/inelastic label depends on kinetic energy, not on momentum (momentum conserved if isolated).

6ELASTIC vs INELASTIC COLLISION (ISOLATED SYSTEM)

✓  vgun = −3.0 m s−1 (3.0 m s−1 backward).

Solution:

u2=0, so v = (0.50×6.0)/(0.50+1.0) = 3.0/1.5 = 2.0 m s−1.
Initial KE: Ki = 12 m1u12 = 0.5×0.50×6.02 = 0.25×36 = 9.0 J.
Final KE: Kf = 12(1.50)×(2.0)2 = 0.75×4 = 3.0 J.
Loss = Ki − Kf = 9.0 − 3.0 = 6.0 J.
✓  v = 2.0 m s−1; kinetic energy lost = 6.0 J.

9⭐Important Points

  • Momentum conservation needs ΣFext=0; internal forces cannot change total momentum of the system.
  • Third-law forces cancel only when considering the whole system, not for an individual body.
  • External impulse equals change in total momentum: ΔP = ∫ΣFext dt.
  • Recoil is a direct consequence of momentum conservation during interaction (simultaneous action–reaction).
  • In collisions, momentum is conserved if isolated; kinetic energy is conserved only if elastic.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Adding action–reaction forces on one body and concluding its net force is zero.
  • Calling a system isolated even when friction/normal from ground gives external impulse.
  • Using kinetic energy conservation in an inelastic collision.
  • Sign errors in 1D problems (choosing directions inconsistently).

EQUILIBRIUM OF A PARTICLE

1Definition & Introduction

  • A particle is in equilibrium when the net external force on it is zero: ΣF = 0.
  • Equilibrium implies a = 0; the particle is either at rest or moves with constant velocity.
  • For concurrent forces, equilibrium can be tested by vector sum = 0 (closed polygon) or by component sums = 0.
  • In 2D equilibrium: ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 simultaneously.
  • Always start with a correct free body diagram (FBD) of the particle only.

2Diagram

Three concurrent forces in equilibrium: they can be arranged head-to-tail to form a closed triangle (vector sum zero).

3Key Concepts

  • 🎯Equilibrium condition: ΣF = 0 (not “no forces”, but forces cancel).
  • 🧩Two-force equilibrium: the two forces must be equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and collinear.
  • 📐Three concurrent forces in equilibrium form a closed triangle when placed head-to-tail.
  • 🧭Component method: resolve each force; apply ΣFx=0, ΣFy=0 (and ΣFz=0 in 3D).
  • 🪢Tension in a light string acts along the string away from the particle; direction is set from the FBD.

4WORKING: HOW TO SOLVE EQUILIBRIUM (CONCURRENT FORCES)

  • Step 1: Isolate the particle; draw FBD with all external forces.
  • Step 2: Choose axes; mark angles from a clearly defined reference line.
  • Step 3: Resolve each force into components along the chosen axes.
  • Step 4: Apply equilibrium: ΣFx=0 and ΣFy=0 (and ΣFz=0 if 3D).
  • Step 5: Solve for unknowns; verify directions and physical sense (e.g. tension cannot be negative).

5SPECIAL CASES: 2-FORCE AND 3-FORCE EQUILIBRIUM

  • Two forces only: they must be equal, opposite and collinear.
  • Three concurrent forces: triangle law holds; each side represents a force (magnitude and direction).
  • If one force is weight W, the other forces’ vertical components must balance W and horizontal components must cancel.
  • In smooth contact: normal reaction is perpendicular to the surface; friction (if present) is tangential.

7Solved Q&A

1
Concept check: A particle is acted upon by several forces but is observed to move with constant velocity in a straight line. What can you conclude about the net external force?
Answer:
Constant velocity implies acceleration is zero.
By Newton’s first/second law, zero acceleration implies ΣF = 0.
Individual forces may be nonzero; they must cancel vectorially.
✓  Net external force on the particle is zero (ΣF = 0).
2
Concept check: In two-force equilibrium, why must the two forces be collinear and opposite, not just equal in magnitude?
Answer:
If two equal forces are not collinear, their vector sum is not zero; it produces a nonzero resultant.
To make ΣF = 0 with only two forces present, they must be equal and opposite along the same line so that they cancel exactly.
✓  With only two forces, zero resultant is possible only when they are equal, opposite, and collinear.

8⭐Important Points

  • Equilibrium means net external force on the particle is zero; forces can be nonzero individually.
  • Do not include Newton’s 3rd law pair on different bodies in the same particle’s FBD.
  • For 2-force equilibrium, forces must be equal, opposite, and collinear.
  • For concurrent forces, polygon closure is equivalent to ΣF=0.
  • Choose axes to simplify component equations (often along convenient directions).

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up sin/cos when the angle is measured from the horizontal vs vertical.
  • Assuming equilibrium means “no forces act”.
  • Adding action–reaction forces (acting on different bodies) in one FBD.
  • Omitting weight W in the FBD of a hanging particle.

COMMON FORCES IN MECHANICS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Force is an interaction that can change the state of rest/uniform motion or cause acceleration; net external force determines acceleration (Fnet = ma).
  • In mechanics, frequently used forces are contact forces (normal reaction, friction, tension, spring force, fluid drag) and the non-contact force of gravity (weight).
  • Forces are vectors; draw all forces acting on the chosen body only (free-body diagram).
  • Normal reaction and static friction are self-adjusting as demanded by constraints, up to limiting values set by contact conditions.
  • Forces occur in pairs between two interacting bodies (Newton’s 3rd law), but action and reaction act on different bodies.

2Diagram

Free-body diagram of a block on a rough horizontal surface (forces on the block).

3Key Concepts

  • 🧱Normal reaction N: perpendicular contact force exerted by a surface
  • 🧤Friction f: tangential contact force opposing relative motion/impending motion
  • 🧵Tension T: pull transmitted along an ideal string/rope
  • 🌀Spring force: restoring force proportional to extension/compression (Hooke’s law)
  • 🌍Weight W = mg: gravitational force by Earth, acts at centre of mass (near Earth)
  • 💧Drag: resistive force by fluid, opposite relative velocity; depends on speed and shape

4NORMAL REACTION (CONTACT PERPENDICULAR FORCE)

  • N acts perpendicular to the contact surface; it is not always equal to mg.
  • On a horizontal surface with no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration: N = mg.
  • On an incline (only weight contributes perpendicular component): N = mg cosθ.
  • N is a self-adjusting force fixed by the constraint (no interpenetration).

5FRICTION (CONTACT TANGENTIAL FORCE)

  • Static friction: 0 ≤ fs ≤ fs,max; direction opposes impending relative motion.
  • Limiting static friction: fs,max = μsN.
  • Kinetic friction (sliding): fk = μkN, opposes relative sliding velocity.
  • Typically μk < μs for the same pair of surfaces.

FRICTION LAWS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Friction is the contact force that opposes relative motion (or tendency of motion) between surfaces.
  • Static friction fs acts when there is no slipping; it adjusts as needed to prevent motion.
  • Limiting static friction fs,max is the maximum static friction just before slipping starts.
  • Kinetic friction fk acts during slipping; its magnitude is approximately constant for given surfaces.
  • Coefficients: μs and μk are dimensionless; typically μk < μs.

2Diagram

Direction: friction opposes relative motion (or its tendency); N is normal reaction.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧲fs is self-adjusting: 0 ≤ fs ≤ fs,max.
  • 🚦Impending slip: fs reaches fs,max = μsN.
  • 🛼During slip: fk = μkN (opposite relative velocity).
  • 📉As motion begins, friction usually drops: fk < fs,max.
  • ⛰️Angle of repose: tanθr = μs (block just starts sliding).
  • 🚆Train/vehicle acceleration is provided by static friction at the wheel–ground contact.

4STATIC FRICTION (SELF-ADJUSTING)

  • Starts from 0 and increases to match the applied tangential force until a maximum.
  • At rest: choose fs from equilibrium, but ensure fs ≤ μsN.
  • If required friction exceeds μsN, static friction fails and slipping begins.
  • Direction: opposite the impending relative motion (not always opposite applied force).

5KINETIC FRICTION + KEY APPLICATIONS

  • During sliding: fk = μkN, nearly constant for given surfaces and load.
  • Typically μk < μs ⇒ easier to keep moving than to start moving.
  • Incline: at rest needs fs = mgsinθ; check against μsmgcosθ.
  • Angle of repose θr: impending slip gives tanθr = μs.
  • Train/box acceleration: forward static friction from ground provides net external force for acceleration.

  • Impending slip condition is an equality: fs = μsN only at the threshold.
  • Angle of repose uses μs, not μk.
  • For rolling without slipping (wheels), friction is typically static; it can accelerate vehicles.

11❌Common Mistakes

  • Assuming fs = μsN always, even when body is simply at rest.
  • Using μs in sliding problems or using μk for angle of repose.
  • Taking friction always opposite applied force instead of opposite impending/actual relative motion.
  • Forgetting to recompute N when extra vertical forces exist (pull at angle, incline, etc.).
