MECHANICAL PROPERTIES OF SOLIDS

TOPIC1

ELASTICITY AND PLASTICITY

1Definition & Introduction

  • Elasticity: property of a body to regain original shape and size after removal of deforming force.
  • Elastic deformation: temporary change in dimensions; disappears on unloading (within elastic limit).
  • Plasticity: property of a body to undergo permanent deformation; does not fully recover on unloading.
  • Real solids are not perfectly rigid; even steel bars deform under sufficiently large external force.
  • On applying a deforming force, an internal restoring tendency develops, opposing the deformation.

2Key Concepts

  • 🧲Restoring force appears inside the solid and opposes the applied deforming force.
  • 🪀Within the elastic region, unloading brings the body back to its original dimensions.
  • 🧈Beyond elastic limit, unloading leaves a non-zero strain: permanent set.
  • 🏗️Engineering design keeps working stress well below elastic limit to avoid permanent deformation.
  • 🧱Putty and mud are close to ideal plastics: large deformation with little recovery.

3WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS INSIDE A SOLID

  • Deforming force changes interatomic/intermolecular separation and slightly shifts atoms from equilibrium positions.
  • Internal restoring tendency arises due to bonding forces; it acts opposite to the deformation.
  • If deformation is small, the body can return to original configuration when force is removed.
  • If bonding rearrangement/slip occurs, the body cannot fully return: plastic deformation results.

4WHY THIS MATTERS IN REAL LIFE (ENGINEERING MOTIVATION)

  • Steel bars in structures elongate/compress under load; design aims to keep deformation in elastic range.
  • Bridges, buildings, automobiles, ropeways require materials that avoid permanent set under working loads.
  • Choice of material depends on desired behaviour: elastic return (springs) vs shape retention (moulding).
  • Rubber-like materials can show large elastic strains but may not obey a simple linear relation.

6⭐Important Points

  • Rigid body is an idealisation; real solids deform under sufficiently large external force.
  • Elasticity is about recovery after unloading, not about being "hard" or "strong".
  • Plasticity implies permanent deformation; unloading leaves non-zero deformation (permanent set).
  • Restoring tendency exists in deformed solids and opposes the applied deforming force.
  • Engineering design typically aims to avoid plastic deformation in structural members under service loads.

7❌Common Mistakes

  • Thinking "more elastic" means "stretches more"; it actually means better recovery for given load range.
  • Assuming steel never deforms; it does deform—often just too small to notice without measurement.
  • Confusing plastic (material behaviour) with plastic (polymer); many polymers can be elastic in some range.
  • Believing permanent set happens only after fracture; it can occur well before breaking.

8💡Memory Technique

“E-P: "E comes Back, P stays Put"”
Elastic → returns to original shape: "comes back" after unloading. Plastic → "put" forever: permanent set remains.

TOPIC2

STRESS AND STRAIN

1Definition & Introduction

  • Stress is restoring force per unit area developed inside a deformed body: σ = FA.
  • In the usual static-equilibrium treatment, the internal restoring force equals the applied deforming force in magnitude (opposite in direction).
  • SI unit of stress: N m−2 = pascal (Pa); dimensional formula: [M L−1 T−2].
  • Strain is fractional change in dimension; it is dimensionless (no unit).
  • Main types: longitudinal (tension/compression), shear (shape change), volume (uniform compression/expansion).

2Key Concepts

  • 📏Longitudinal strain: ε = ΔLL for a rod under tension or compression.
  • 🧊Shearing stress: tangential restoring force per area; shearing strain: ΔxL = tanθ ≈ θ (radian, small-angle).
  • 💧Hydraulic stress equals fluid pressure p acting normal everywhere; volume strain: ΔVV.
  • ↔️Tensile stress and compressive stress are both longitudinal stresses (force normal to area).
  • ⚖️In a hanging wire, tension at any cross-section is F (not 2F); stress is F/A.

3TYPES OF STRESS (BY FORCE DIRECTION)

  • Longitudinal stress: force normal to area; causes change in length.
  • Tensile (pull): length increases; compressive (push): length decreases.
  • Shearing stress: force tangential to area; changes shape without significant volume change.
  • Hydraulic stress: normal stress everywhere due to fluid pressure; causes volume change (shape unchanged).

4STRAINS FOR THE THREE LOADINGS

  • Longitudinal strain: ε = ΔL/L (same definition for tension and compression).
  • Shearing strain: γ = Δx/L = tanθ ≈ θ for small angles (use radians).
  • Volume strain: εv = ΔV/V; negative for compression (ΔV < 0).
  • Strain is dimensionless, so units never appear in final strain values.

  • Writing strain with units (e.g., "mm"), forgetting it is dimensionless.
  • Using degrees in γ ≈ θ; approximation requires θ in radians.
  • Confusing compressive stress with hydraulic stress: hydraulic is normal on all sides uniformly.

TOPIC3

HOOKE'S LAW

1Definition & Introduction

  • Hooke’s law: for small deformations, stress is directly proportional to strain.
  • Mathematically: stress = k × strain, where k is an elastic modulus.
  • It is an empirical law; valid only in the linear (Hookean) region of the stress–strain curve.
  • Within the linear region, unloading brings the body back to original dimensions (purely elastic).
  • Materials like rubber/tissues may be elastic but often show non-Hookean (nonlinear) stress–strain relation.

2Key Concepts

  • 📈In linear region: slope of stress–strain graph = modulus (e.g., Y, G, B).
  • 🔍Small deformation means strain is small enough that proportionality holds (region O→A).
  • 🧪Hooke’s law applies separately to tensile/compressive, shear, and bulk (hydraulic) deformations.
  • ⚠️Beyond proportional limit, stress and strain are not proportional; plastic effects may appear.

3WHAT “MODULUS OF ELASTICITY” MEANS

  • k is the proportionality constant in stress = k × strain.
  • Since strain is dimensionless, modulus has same unit as stress: Pa (N m−2).
  • For axial loading: modulus is Young’s modulus Y.
  • For shear deformation: modulus is shear modulus G.
  • For uniform pressure: modulus is bulk modulus B.

4LIMITS: LINEAR, ELASTIC, PLASTIC

  • Proportional limit: end of linear relation; Hooke’s law stops being valid.
  • Elastic limit (yield point): beyond this, permanent set appears on unloading.
  • Non-Hookean materials: may be elastic yet show nonlinear σ–ε over most range (e.g., elastomers).
  • Exam cue: “Hookean” refers to linear σ–ε, not merely “returns to original shape”.

  • Treating modulus as dimensionless; modulus has unit Pa, only strain is dimensionless.

TOPIC4

STRESS–STRAIN CURVE (TENSILE TEST)

1Definition & Introduction

  • A stress–strain curve is the experimental plot of tensile stress σ versus tensile strain ε for a material under gradual loading.
  • Region OA is linear: σ ∝ ε; slope gives Young’s modulus Y.
  • Up to B (yield point / elastic limit), the body returns to original length on unloading (no permanent strain).
  • Beyond B, plastic deformation occurs; unloading leaves a permanent set (non-zero ε at σ=0).
  • D is ultimate tensile strength σu (maximum engineering stress); beyond it necking leads to fracture at E.
  • If D and E are close → brittle; far apart → ductile (large plastic region).

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧷Stress: σ=FA (Pa); Strain: ε=ΔLL (dimensionless)
  • 📏Linear region OA obeys Hooke’s law; Y=σε = slope of OA
  • 🚦Yield point (elastic limit) B: onset of plasticity; yield strength σy is stress at B
  • 🧱Plastic region B→D: large strain for small stress change; unloading gives permanent set
  • 🏋️Ultimate tensile strength at D: σu is maximum stress reached on the curve
  • 💥Fracture at E: material breaks; necking typically occurs after D for ductile metals

4HOW TO READ EACH REGION (O → E)

  • O–A (proportional limit): straight line; σ=Yε; unloading retraces same line.
  • A–B (nonlinear elastic): not proportional, but still elastic; no permanent set on unloading.
  • B (yield point / elastic limit): plasticity begins; σy is stress at B.
  • B–D (plastic deformation): large strain grows; unloading leaves residual strain (permanent set).
  • D (ultimate tensile strength): maximum engineering stress σu on curve.

5UNLOADING FROM PLASTIC REGION (PERMANENT SET IDEA)

  • If unloaded from a point C between B and D, path is roughly parallel to OA (elastic recovery).
  • At σ=0, strain remains non-zero: εp (plastic strain) = permanent set.
  • Total strain at C: ε = εe + εp; only εe recovers.

  • Using wrong area units: 1 mm² = 1×10−6 m² (not 10−3 m²).

YOUNG'S MODULUS (LONGITUDINAL ELASTICITY)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Young’s modulus Y is the ratio of longitudinal (tensile/compressive) stress to longitudinal strain within elastic limit.
  • Longitudinal stress: σ = F/A (force normal to cross-section per unit area).
  • Longitudinal strain: ε = ΔL/L (fractional change in length).
  • Y is a material property: larger Y ⇒ smaller strain for the same stress (stiffer).
  • Unit: Pa (N m−2); strain is dimensionless so Y has same unit as stress.
  • For a given material, tensile and compressive strains are same for equal magnitude stress (within elastic limit).

2Key Concepts

  • 📌Hooke region: valid only where stress ∝ strain (linear stress–strain curve).
  • 🧮Working formula: ΔL = FLAY (small deformations; within elastic limit).
  • 🏗️Metals have large Y; steel (~2.0×1011 Pa) is stiffer than copper (~1.1×1011 Pa).
  • 🧬Elastomers (rubber, aorta tissue) show large strain but often non-linear; effective Y depends on strain range.
  • ⚠️Use Young’s modulus only in the elastic (recoverable) deformation range; beyond yield, stress–strain is non-linear and permanent set may occur.

3DERIVATION (FROM STRESS & STRAIN)

  • Longitudinal stress: σ = F/A (tension or compression).
  • Longitudinal strain: ε = ΔL/L.
  • Within elastic (linear) region: σ = Yε ⇒ Y = σ/ε.

4INTERPRETATION USING MATERIAL DATA

  • Large Y ⇒ hard to stretch: metals usually have higher Y than elastomers.
  • Exam language: “more elastic” often means larger Y (smaller strain for same stress).
  • Elastomers: elastic range can be large but often non-linear, so Y may not be constant.
  • Young’s modulus is defined from the linear (Hooke’s law) part of the stress–strain curve.

TOPIC6

SHEAR MODULUS (MODULUS OF RIGIDITY)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Shear modulus G is the ratio of shearing stress to shearing strain within elastic limit.
  • Shearing stress σs = F/A when tangential force acts parallel to a face of area A.
  • Shearing strain is given by tanθ, and geometrically tanθ = Δx/L. For small angular distortion, tanθ ≈ θ (in rad), so strain ≈ θ ≈ Δx/L.
  • G measures rigidity: larger G ⇒ smaller sideways shift for the same tangential stress.
  • SI unit: Pa (N m−2); dimension: [M L−1 T−2].
  • For isotropic solids, G is generally less than Y and is related by G = Y/[2(1+ν)] where ν is Poisson’s ratio. A rough estimate G ≈ Y/3 may be used only for some materials.

2Key Concepts

  • 🧩Pure shear mainly changes shape (angle), with negligible volume change.
  • 📐Geometry: tanθ = Δx/L; for small θ, tanθ ≈ θ (radians).
  • ⚖️Hooke form in shear: σs = Gθ (linear elastic region).
  • 🧪Only solids sustain static shear; fluids flow under sustained shear stress.

3WORKING: FROM GEOMETRY TO HOOKE FORM

  • Geometry gives tanθ = Δx/L for the distorted block/cylinder.
  • Shearing strain = tanθ; for small θ, strain ≈ θ (radians) and also ≈ Δx/L.
  • Define G = (shearing stress)/(shearing strain) = (F/A) ÷ (Δx/L).
  • Linear elastic relation: σs = G × shearing strain (valid only within elastic limit).

4SHEAR MODULI: QUICK DATA (ORDER OF RIGIDITY)

  • Typical G values (GPa): Lead 5.6, Wood 10, Glass 23, Al 25, Cu 42, Steel 84, W 150.
  • Trend cue: metals (especially steel, tungsten) have high G; lead is very low (soft).
  • Link with Young’s modulus (isotropic solids): G = Y/[2(1+ν)]. The shortcut G ≈ Y/3 is only a rough estimate for some materials.

BULK MODULUS AND COMPRESSIBILITY

1Definition & Introduction

  • Bulk modulus B is the ratio of hydraulic stress (pressure) to volume strain under uniform compression.
  • For an increase in pressure Δp, volume decreases by ΔV; volume strain = ΔV/V.
  • B = − ΔpΔV/V; negative sign because ΔV is negative for positive Δp.
  • Compressibility k measures how easily volume changes: k = 1/B.
  • SI unit: B in Pa (N m−2); k in Pa−1.

2Key Concepts

  • 💧Hydraulic stress acts normal to surface everywhere; shape unchanged, only volume changes.
  • ➖Minus sign ensures B is positive in equilibrium: Δp > 0, ΔV < 0.
  • 📏For small compressions: Δp = B×|ΔV/V| (linear response).
  • 🪨Solids: largest B (least compressible) → liquids → gases (most compressible).
  • 🌊Water has B ≈ 2.2×109 Pa, so even a few km depth gives only ~1% volume decrease.

3SIGN CONVENTION AND PHYSICAL MEANING

  • Take Δp as positive when external pressure increases.
  • Compression means ΔV is negative; hence ΔV/V is negative.
  • B uses a minus sign so that B remains positive for stable materials.
  • Larger B ⇒ smaller |ΔV/V| for the same Δp (stiffer against volume change).

4USING B AND k IN NUMERICALS

  • For small changes: ΔV = − ΔpVB.
  • Compressibility for small changes: k = − 1Δp × (ΔV/V).
  • Unit check: Pa ÷ (dimensionless) → Pa for B; inverse for k.
  • For liquids/solids, treat B approximately constant over moderate pressure ranges.

  • Forgetting unit conversions (cm3 to m3) in volume calculations.

POISSON'S RATIO (ν)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Poisson's ratio ν is defined (signed form) as negative of the ratio of lateral strain to longitudinal strain within elastic limit: ν = −εlatεlong.
  • When a wire is stretched, its length increases but its diameter decreases in a perpendicular direction.
  • Lateral strain: εlat = Δdd (Δd is negative in tension).
  • Longitudinal strain: εlong = ΔLL (positive in tension).
  • ν is a pure number (ratio of two strains): no units, no dimensions.
  • Typical values (engineering solids): steel ≈ 0.28 to 0.30; aluminium alloys ≈ 0.33.

2Key Concepts

  • ↔️Lateral strain is in a direction perpendicular to the applied force.
  • ↕️Longitudinal strain is in the same direction as the applied force.
  • 🧮Standard definition: ν = −εlatεlong. Using magnitudes (common in numericals): ν = |εlat|εlong.
  • ✅If sign is not asked, use magnitudes; in tension Δd is negative and ΔL is positive.

3SIGN CONVENTION (EXAM-SAFE)

  • In tension: ΔL > 0, but Δd < 0 (diameter contracts).
  • So (Δd/d)(ΔL/L) is negative; therefore standard definition uses minus sign: ν = −εlatεlong so that ν is usually positive for ordinary materials.
  • If question asks only the numerical value, use magnitude unless sign is explicitly discussed.

4TYPICAL VALUES (FAST RECALL)

  • Steel: 0.28 to 0.30 (often taken ~0.29 in numericals).
  • Aluminium alloys: ~0.33.
  • For a given material, ν is approximately constant only for small strains (within elastic limit).

ELASTIC POTENTIAL ENERGY (STRAIN ENERGY)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Elastic potential energy (strain energy) is the work stored in a body during elastic deformation.
  • For a wire obeying Hooke’s law, force rises linearly with extension: F ∝ l (within elastic limit).
  • Work done in stretching from 0 to extension l equals area under the F–l graph.
  • Energy per unit volume (energy density) in linear uniaxial elasticity: u = 12(σ×ε).
  • Valid only in the linear (Hooke’s law) region; beyond elastic limit, energy is partly dissipated.

2Key Concepts

  • 📈Linear rise: F = YALl ⇒ triangle area gives work.
  • 🧱Volume link: V = AL, stress σ = FA, strain ε = lL.
  • ⚡For uniaxial linear elasticity, energy density at a point equals area under the σ–ε line up to that strain: u = 12(σ×ε).
  • 🧪Units: u in J m−3; since 1 Pa = 1 N m−2 = 1 J m−3.

3DERIVATION: WORK DONE IN STRETCHING A WIRE

  • Hooke’s law (uniaxial): F = YA(l/L), valid within elastic limit.
  • Infinitesimal work: dW = F dl = YA(l/L) dl.
  • Integrate from 0 to l: W = YAl22L.
  • Also W = 12Ffinall (triangle area in linear case).

4ENERGY DENSITY FORM (MOST USED IN QUESTIONS)

  • Volume of wire: V = AL.
  • Stress: σ = FA; strain: ε = lL.
  • Using F = σA and l = εL: W = 12(σ×ε)(AL) = 12(σ×ε)V.
  • Therefore energy per unit volume: u = WV = 12(σ×ε) = σ22Y = 12Yε2.

  • Using Young’s modulus relations beyond elastic limit (plastic region).

ENGINEERING APPLICATIONS (ELASTICITY)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Engineering applications use elastic limits and deformation relations to keep structures safe and functional.
  • Design criterion: keep working stress below yield strength using a factor of safety.
  • Members in tension/compression: size cross-sectional area so stress stays within allowable limit.
  • Beams: limit deflection (sag) under load using Young’s modulus Y and the second moment of area I of the cross-section.
  • Buckling: slender members can fail by lateral instability before the material yields; end conditions and slenderness matter.
  • I-section beams give high bending stiffness with less material by placing area far from the neutral axis.

2Diagram

Applications: choose allowable stress (ropes), control beam deflection using Y and I, and use efficient sections like I-beams.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧱Safe design: σwork ≤ σyn where n is factor of safety
  • 🪢Rope/wire sizing (axial load): A ≥ Wσallow ensures no yielding
  • 📏Beam deflection depends on Y and I: for simply supported with central load, δmax = Wl348YI
  • 🏛️Column end conditions affect buckling: fixed ends withstand more than hinged (pinned) ends for the same length
  • 🧩I-section: large I about the bending axis → smaller bending stress and deflection for a given load
  • 🧠Depth matters strongly: for a rectangle, I = bd312 so deflection ∝ 1/d3

41) SAFE LOAD USING YIELD STRENGTH (DESIGN RULE)

  • Yield strength σy: stress at yield point (elastic limit); beyond it, permanent set begins.
  • Working/allowable stress: σallow = σyn, where n is factor of safety (often large for ropes/cables).
  • For axial load W: required area A ≥ Wσallow = nWσy.
  • Circular rope: r = √(Aπ), diameter D = 2r.
  • Braided thin wires are used for flexibility; a single very thick wire is less flexible.

52) BEAM BENDING: DEFLECTION (SAG) AND WHY I-SECTIONS HELP

  • For a simply supported beam with a central point load W: δmax = Wl348YI.
  • Rectangular section: I = bd312 ⇒ δmax = Wl34Ybd3.
  • Deflection reduces strongly with depth: δ ∝ 1/d3 but only ∝ 1/b.
  • To reduce deflection: choose higher Y, increase I (shape), increase d, reduce span l, reduce mid-span loads.
  • I-sections increase I efficiently by placing more area far from the neutral axis.

9⭐Important Points

  • Always design with σallow = σy/n, not with σy directly.
  • For ropes/cables: choose larger factor of safety due to wear, shocks, and hidden flaws.
  • Beam deflection depends on Y and I; increasing depth increases I strongly for a rectangle.
  • I-section is efficient because it places more area far from the neutral axis, increasing I with less material.
  • Column buckling depends strongly on end conditions (fixed vs hinged) and slenderness.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Using ultimate tensile strength instead of yield strength for safe-load sizing.
  • Forgetting factor of safety, or applying it inconsistently.
  • Mixing units: taking σ in MPa but W in N without converting.
  • In deflection problems: using the wrong support/loading formula or using d2 instead of d3 for rectangular sections.
