MOTION IN A STRAIGHT LINE

TOPIC1

BASICS OF MOTION

1Definition & Introduction

  • Motion is change in position with time relative to a chosen reference.
  • In rectilinear motion, the object moves along a straight line (1D).
  • Choose an origin O and a positive direction; signs of x, v, a depend on this choice.
  • Position at time t is given by a position coordinate x(t) on the number line.
  • A moving body is often treated as a point object if its size ≪ distance traveled in the time considered.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧭Reference frame matters: the same object can be at rest or moving in different frames.
  • ➕Sign convention: right/along chosen axis is +, left/opposite is −.
  • 📌Position x is an algebraic coordinate; displacement Δx can be +, −, or 0.
  • 🧩Point-object approximation: ignore rotation/shape; track a representative point (often CM).

4REFERENCE ORIGIN & SIGN CONVENTION (EXAM CORE)

  • State your choice first: origin O, + direction, and coordinate axis.
  • Changing origin shifts x values but not physical motion; Δx is origin-independent.
  • Reversing axis flips signs of x, v, a simultaneously; equations still work algebraically.
  • In 1D, displacement is Δx = x2 − x1; it can be negative.

5POINT OBJECT APPROXIMATION (WHEN VALID?)

  • Treat body as point if size ≪ distance moved in the time interval.
  • Good example: train between stations; bad example: tumbling beaker or spinning ball turning sharply.
  • When size matters, different points have different motion; point model fails for rotation/deformation.

9⭐Important Points

  • Motion is always described relative to a chosen origin and direction.
  • For 1D, all quantities are algebraic: assign signs before substituting in formulas.
  • Displacement depends only on endpoints: Δx = x2 − x1.
  • Point-object model is valid only when size is negligible compared to travel distance.
  • In x–t graph, slope gives velocity; steeper slope means higher speed (magnitude).

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting to declare origin and + direction; then mixing signs inconsistently.
  • Taking distance = displacement always; wrong when motion reverses direction.
  • Using |Δx| in vavg instead of Δx, losing direction information.
  • Treating a rotating/tumbling object as a point when its size/rotation affects motion.

11💡Memory Technique

TOPIC2

INSTANTANEOUS VELOCITY AND SPEED

1Definition & Introduction

  • Instantaneous velocity at time t is the limit of average velocity as Δt &rarr; 0.
  • Mathematically: v(t) = dxdt (rate of change of position with time).
  • Graphically: v at an instant equals the slope of tangent to the x-t curve at that instant.
  • Sign of v gives direction of motion along chosen +x; magnitude gives how fast.
  • Instantaneous speed is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity: speed = |v|.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 📌Average velocity: ΔxΔt; instantaneous: limit as Δt &rarr; 0.
  • 📈On x-t graph: chord slope approaches tangent slope as interval shrinks.
  • ➕Uniform motion: x-t is a straight line; slope is constant &rArr; v constant.
  • 🧮Numerical estimate (symmetric): v(t)= x(t+Δt/2) − x(t−Δt/2)Δt.
  • 🔁Speed ignores direction: +24 m s−1 and −24 m s−1 both have speed 24 m s−1.

4LIMIT IDEA (Δt &rarr; 0) IN ONE LINE

  • Average over interval: vavg = ΔxΔt with Δx = x(t+Δt) − x(t).
  • Instantaneous: make the interval extremely small so velocity corresponds to one instant.
  • If x(t) is smooth, the limiting value exists and equals dxdt.

5NUMERICAL METHOD (DATA-BASED VELOCITY AT AN INSTANT)

  • When only a table of x vs t is given, use small Δt around the required t.
  • Prefer central difference: v(t) &asymp; x(t+h) − x(t−h)2h for better accuracy.
  • Smaller h reduces truncation error, but too small h can amplify measurement noise.

  • Instantaneous speed is always equal to |instantaneous velocity| at the same instant.
  • Uniform motion: x ∝ t &rArr; v constant; average velocity equals instantaneous velocity.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Confusing slope of x-t with slope of v-t (they represent different quantities).
  • Using average velocity over a large Δt and calling it instantaneous velocity.
  • Taking speed as signed quantity; speed is magnitude, never negative.
  • In 1D, assuming average speed = |ΔxΔt|. This holds only if there is no reversal (motion is monotonic so distance = |Δx|); in general, average speed = total distance/total time.

TOPIC3

ACCELERATION CONCEPT

1Definition & Introduction

  • Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity with time.
  • Average acceleration over an interval: aavg = ΔvΔt = v2−v1t2−t1.
  • Instantaneous acceleration at time t: a = dvdt = limΔt→0 ΔvΔt.
  • SI unit: m s−2; sign depends on chosen positive direction.
  • Speeding up or slowing down depends on relative directions of v and a, not on sign alone.

2 Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 📈On a v-t graph: slope of secant = aavg, slope of tangent = instantaneous a.
  • 🧭If v and a have same sign ⇒ speed increases; opposite signs ⇒ speed decreases.
  • ⏸️v=0 at an instant does not imply a=0 (e.g., top of vertical throw).
  • ➕➖Negative acceleration can still increase speed if motion is in negative direction.

4SLOPE INTERPRETATION (v–t GRAPH)

  • Average acceleration: aavg equals slope of line joining (t1,v1) and (t2,v2).
  • Instantaneous acceleration at t: slope of tangent to the v–t curve at that point.
  • Steeper slope ⇒ larger |a|; horizontal v–t line ⇒ a=0.
  • Units check: slope = (m s−1)/s = m s−2.

5SIGN OF ACCELERATION VS SPEEDING UP

  • Speed increases when v·a > 0 (same direction).
  • Speed decreases when v·a < 0 (opposite directions): this is deceleration.
  • Turning point: at the instant v=0, sign of a still indicates the direction of upcoming velocity.
  • Always state chosen positive direction first; signs follow that choice.

9⭐Important Points

  • Acceleration concerns change in velocity (magnitude and/or direction); in 1D, direction is via sign.
  • On v–t graph, slope gives acceleration; steeper slope means larger magnitude.
  • Sign of a depends on chosen axis; speeding up depends on whether a aligns with v.
  • Momentarily zero velocity can coexist with non-zero acceleration (turning point).
  • Uniform acceleration ⇒ v–t straight line; then aavg equals instantaneous a everywhere.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Assuming negative acceleration always means slowing down (ignores direction of velocity).
  • Using speed instead of velocity in Δv/Δt; acceleration needs signed velocity.
  • Reading slope from wrong graph (using x–t slope as acceleration).
  • Forgetting units: reporting m s−1 instead of m s−2 for acceleration.

TOPIC4

MOTION GRAPH INTERPRETATIONS (x–t, v–t, a–t)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Motion graphs connect x(t), v(t) and a(t) via derivatives and integrals.
  • Slope of x–t at an instant (tangent) gives instantaneous velocity: v = dx/dt.
  • Slope of v–t at an instant (tangent) gives instantaneous acceleration: a = dv/dt.
  • Area under v–t curve between t1 and t2 gives displacement Δx.
  • Curvature of x–t indicates sign of a: curves upward for a > 0, downward for a < 0.
  • Sharp kinks imply non-differentiability: v or a changes abruptly; this is an idealization (real graphs are smooth).

2 Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧭Sign convention matters: right/up positive; then signs of x, v, a follow.
  • 📐Slope of x–t is v; steeper slope ⇒ larger speed.
  • 🪜Slope of v–t is a; constant a ⇒ straight-line v–t.
  • 🧮Area under v–t gives displacement; area below axis counts negative.
  • 🌀Uniform motion: x–t straight line, v–t horizontal, a–t at zero.
  • 🟣Uniform acceleration: x–t parabola, v–t straight line, a–t horizontal.

4SHAPES YOU MUST RECOGNISE

  • Uniform motion (a=0): x–t straight line; slope constant; v constant.
  • Uniform acceleration: v–t straight line with slope a; a–t horizontal line.
  • x–t for constant a: parabola; opens upward if a>0, downward if a<0.
  • Turning point in x–t: slope zero ⇒ v=0 instant; does not imply a=0.
  • Increasing separation of x points per second ⇒ speed increasing (|v| increasing).

5SMOOTHNESS, CURVATURE & KINKS (PHYSICAL MEANING)

  • Smooth x(t) ⇒ well-defined instantaneous v at every instant.
  • Smooth v(t) ⇒ well-defined instantaneous a; realistic forces change continuously.
  • Kink in x–t (corner) ⇒ slope jumps ⇒ instantaneous change in v (impulse idealization).
  • Kink in v–t ⇒ acceleration undefined at that instant; implies sudden change in net force.
  • a–t step change (piecewise constant) is common in problems; real transitions are rounded.

9⭐Important Points

  • A point with negative slope on x–t means v is negative (motion in negative direction).
  • a=0 does not mean v=0; it means v is constant (could be nonzero).
  • At a maximum/minimum of x(t), v=0 but a can be nonzero (e.g., projectile top).
  • For piecewise motion, compute displacement by signed areas over each interval and add.
  • Slope uses tangent for curves, not the chord between two points (instantaneous vs average).

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Reading height of v–t as displacement instead of area.
  • Using slope of x–t as acceleration (it gives velocity, not acceleration).
  • Ignoring sign: counting area below time axis as positive displacement.
  • Assuming zero velocity implies zero acceleration (false at turning points).

11💡Memory Technique

“SAA Rule: Slope–Slope–Area”
Slope of tangent to x–t gives v. Slope of tangent to v–t gives a. Area under v–t from t1 to t2 gives Δx (signed).

TOPIC5

AREA UNDER V–T CURVE

1Definition & Introduction

  • For 1D motion, displacement from t1 to t2 equals the signed area under the v–t curve.
  • Positive area (above time axis) adds positive displacement; negative area gives negative displacement.
  • For constant velocity u, the area is a rectangle: x = uT.
  • For uniform acceleration, the area splits into rectangle + triangle on the straight-line v–t graph.
  • Dimensions check: (v)×(t) = (m s−1)×(s) = m, so area represents displacement.

2 Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧭Use signed area: direction matters (area below axis is negative).
  • 🟦Constant v: rectangle area = height u × base T.
  • 🔺Uniform a: triangle area = 12×base t×height (change in v).
  • 🧮General rule: x = ∫t1t2 v dt.

4WORKING: RECTANGLE + TRIANGLE (UNIFORM ACCELERATION)

  • For uniform a, v–t is a straight line from v0 to v in time t.
  • Displacement x = area of rectangle (v0×t) + area of triangle (12(v−v0)t).
  • So x = v0t + 12at2 (since v−v0=at).

5SIGNED AREA & DIRECTION (EXAM TRAP ZONE)

  • If v is negative for some interval, that area is negative and reduces net displacement.
  • Net displacement = (area above axis) − (magnitude of area below axis).
  • Total distance travelled ≠ signed area; it equals area under speed–time graph (|v| vs t).

KINEMATIC EQUATIONS (CONSTANT ACCELERATION)

1Definition & Introduction

  • For 1D motion with constant acceleration, three equations connect v, v0, a, t, x (or x − x0).
  • Valid only when acceleration is constant in magnitude and direction during the interval.
  • All quantities are algebraic; choose origin and positive direction first, then use signs consistently.
  • Average velocity for constant a: vavg = (v + v0)/2, hence displacement x − x0 = vavgt.
  • Graph links: slope of v–t is a; area under v–t gives displacement.

2 Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧭Pick axis & origin: right/up positive; then x, v, a can be ±.
  • 📈Constant a ⇒ v–t straight line; x–t parabola.
  • 🧮Use the equation that avoids the unknown variable (e.g. eliminate t using v² relation).
  • ⏱️Area under v–t: rectangle + triangle = v0t + 12at2.
  • 🔁Momentarily v=0 does not imply a=0 (e.g. top point in vertical throw).

4DERIVATION IN 3 LINES (FROM v–t GRAPH)

  • Constant a ⇒ v varies linearly: v = v0 + at.
  • Displacement Δx = area under v–t = v0t + 12at2.
  • Also Δx = (v+v0)2t; eliminate t to get v2 = v02 + 2aΔx.

5WHICH EQUATION TO USE? (NO-OVERTHINK FLOW)

  • Need v after time t? use v = v0 + at.
  • Need position with time? use x = x0 + v0t + 12at2.
  • Need speed/velocity without time? use v2 = v02 + 2a(x − x0).
  • Constant a only: vavg = (v+v0)2; then Δx = vavgt.

CALCULUS DERIVATION METHOD (EQUATIONS OF MOTION)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Calculus method links x(t), v(t), a(t) using derivatives and integrals.
  • From definitions: v=dxdt and a=dvdt (instantaneous rates).
  • Chain rule (1D): a=dvdt=dvdxdxdt=vdvdx.
  • For constant acceleration in 1D, integrating yields the standard kinematic equations.
  • For non-uniform acceleration, keep a as a function: a(t) or a(x) and integrate accordingly.

2 Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩Pick the right independent variable: a(t) integrate in t; a(x) use a=vdvdx.
  • 🧮Core integrals: v(t) = v0 + ∫0t a(t) dt, and x(t) = x0 + ∫0t v(t) dt.
  • 🔁If a=a(x): 12(v2−v02) = ∫x0x a(x) dx.
  • 📏Always apply limits after integrating; constants come from initial conditions at t=0 or x=x0.

4WORKING TEMPLATE (GENERAL)

  • If a is known as a(t): integrate dvdt=a(t) to get v(t).
  • Then integrate dxdt=v(t) to get x(t).
  • If a is known as a(x): use a=vdvdx and integrate in x to get v(x).
  • Finally obtain t using dt = dxv(x) if needed.

5NON-UNIFORM ACCELERATION: WHAT EXAMS LIKE

  • Given a(t): v=v0+∫a(t)dt, then displacement is area under v–t curve: ∫v(t)dt.
  • Given a(x): energy-like form v2−v02=2∫a(x)dx works even when a varies with x.
  • Given v(t): acceleration is slope: a(t)=dvdt; displacement is integral: x−x0=∫v dt.

VERTICAL MOTION AND FREE FALL

1Definition & Introduction

  • Vertical motion near Earth (small heights) is uniformly accelerated with a = −g if upward is +y.
  • For free fall (released from rest), v0 = 0 and motion is only under gravity (air resistance neglected).
  • Same equations as constant-a motion apply: v-t, y-t, and v-y relations.
  • At maximum height of a thrown-up body: v = 0 but a is still −g (not zero).
  • Sign convention is a choice; once chosen, keep signs of y, v, a consistent throughout.

2 Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • ⬆️Thrown upward: v decreases linearly; acceleration stays downward (−g).

  • ⬇️Free fall from rest: v=−gt, y=y0−12gt2 (upward +y).
  • 🧭Use one axis only: +y upward or downward; both work if signs are correct.
  • ⏱️Time of flight (same start/end level): T=2ug where u=initial upward speed.

  • If +y upward: a=−g, upward v positive, downward v negative.
  • If +y downward: a=+g, drop problems become sign-simple; heights become negative upward.
  • Never mix “speed” (magnitude) with signed velocity; use signs in equations.
  • At same physical point, y can be + or − depending on origin; physics unchanged.

GALILEO ODD NUMBER LAW

1Definition & Introduction

  • For motion along a straight line starting from rest with uniform acceleration, displacements covered in successive equal time intervals are in the ratio 1:3:5:7:…
  • Applies to free fall (neglect air resistance) and motion down a smooth incline (approximately constant acceleration).
  • If each time interval is τ, then displacement in the nth interval is proportional to (2n − 1).
  • Total displacement after n equal intervals is proportional to n2.
  • This follows from x ∝ t2 when initial velocity is zero and acceleration is constant.

2 Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • ⏱️Compare displacements in each equal interval, not cumulative displacement.
  • 📈Uniform acceleration: v increases linearly with t, so later intervals cover more displacement.
  • 🔢1+3+5+…+(2n−1) = n2 ⇒ cumulative displacement ∝ n2.
  • 🧠Neat 1:3:5:… from the first interval requires u=0 and constant a, with no direction reversal.

4PROOF IDEA (USING x = 12at2)

  • From rest with constant acceleration a: x(t) = 12at2 (take initial position as 0).
  • After n intervals of length τ: xn = 12a(nτ)2.
  • Displacement in the nth interval: Δxn = xn − xn−1 = 12aτ2(2n − 1).
  • Hence Δx1:Δx2:Δx3:… = 1:3:5:…

5WHAT IT IMPLIES (EXAM USE)

  • If (and only if) the body starts from rest with constant a, then if first-interval displacement is s, next intervals are 3s, 5s, 7s…
  • Cumulative displacement in first n intervals: Sn = s(1+3+…+(2n−1)) = sn2.
  • So displacements from start at times τ, 2τ, 3τ are in ratio 1:4:9 (since x ∝ t2).
  • For free fall near Earth: use a = ±g with a consistent sign convention.

8⭐Important Points

  • Law compares displacements in successive equal time intervals, not velocities.
  • Valid only for constant acceleration; free fall without air drag is the standard case.
  • To get 1:3:5:… starting from the first interval, initial velocity must be zero.
  • Δxn increases linearly with n; cumulative xn increases as n2.
  • Odd numbers sum rule: 1+3+…+(2n−1) = n2.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Using the law when initial velocity is not zero (then the first-interval ratio is not 1:3:5:…).
  • Applying it when direction reverses within the chosen intervals (then distance vs displacement causes confusion).
  • Applying it to non-uniform acceleration (air resistance, varying slope, etc.).
  • Comparing cumulative displacements at τ, 2τ, 3τ as 1:3:5 instead of 1:4:9.

1Definition & Introduction

  • Stopping distance is the distance travelled from brake application to v=0 under (roughly) uniform deceleration.
  • For constant braking acceleration a (<0), stopping distance ds depends on v02, not linearly on v0.
  • Reaction time tr is the delay between seeing a hazard and starting to brake.
  • Total stopping sight distance ≈ reaction distance v0tr + braking distance ds.
  • Ruler-drop experiment estimates tr using free fall: d = 12gtr2 (neglect air resistance).

3Key Concepts

  • ⚠️Even with strong brakes, reaction distance v0tr can dominate at high speeds.
  • 🧭Use signs carefully: choose + along motion; braking gives a<0 and ds>0.
  • 🧪Ruler drop: larger catch distance d implies larger tr as tr ∝ √d.
  • 🛣️Road safety link: speed limits reduce required stopping distance strongly due to v2 scaling.

4DERIVATION: STOPPING DISTANCE UNDER BRAKING

  • Take +x along motion; initial speed v0, final speed v=0.
  • Braking causes (approximately) constant acceleration a<0 (retardation).
  • Use v2=v02+2ax with x=ds.
  • Result: ds=−v022a = v022|a|.

5REACTION TIME FROM RULER-DROP (FREE FALL)

  • Drop ruler vertically; catcher closes fingers when seeing motion; measured fall distance = d.
  • Assume free fall from rest: d=12gtr2 ⇒ tr=√(2dg).
  • Use g≈9.8 m s−2; convert cm to m before substituting.
  • Interpretation: this estimates visual + neural + muscle delay, not brake system delay.

SIGN CONVENTIONS AND PITFALLS (1D KINEMATICS)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Sign convention: fix origin and positive axis first; then write x, Δx, v, a with algebraic signs.
  • In 1D, the sign of v and a depends only on the chosen + direction; the physical motion is unchanged.
  • Speeding up or slowing down depends on whether v and a point in the same or opposite directions.
  • A particle can have v=0 at an instant yet a≠0 (turning point).
  • The standard kinematic equations are valid only when acceleration is constant (both magnitude and direction) over the time interval.

2Diagram

Axis choice sets signs; speed increases when v and a have the same sign.

3Key Concepts

  • 📍Changing + axis reverses signs of x, v, a together; speed and time stay the same.
  • ⚡Speed increases if v and a have same sign; speed decreases if signs opposite.
  • ⏸️v=0 can occur only instantaneously at a turning point; acceleration can still be nonzero.
  • 🧾Use algebraic v, a in equations; do not substitute speed |v| in place of v.
  • 🧪If a varies with time, use calculus/graphs or split motion into segments with constant a.

4POINTS TO PONDER (EXAM TRAPS)

  • Before substituting values, write the chosen origin O and positive direction (+x or +y).
  • Reversing the axis flips signs of x, v, a together; the motion itself does not change.
  • Speeding up: va>0. Slowing down: va<0 (for v≠0).
  • Negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down; check direction of v also.
  • v=0 means rest only at that instant; it need not be rest for a time interval.

5VALIDITY LIMITS OF KINEMATIC EQUATIONS

  • Use constant-a equations only if acceleration stays constant in magnitude and direction over the interval.
  • If a changes with time, use calculus definitions (v=dx/dt, a=dv/dt) or interpret slopes/areas on graphs.
  • If motion has piecewise constant acceleration, apply constant-a equations separately in each segment with correct initial values.

  • Velocity and acceleration are algebraic (can be ±); speed is |v| (always ≥0).
  • To decide speeding up/slowing down in 1D (for v≠0), compare signs of v and a.
  • At a turning point, v=0 can occur while a is still nonzero.
  • Use constant-acceleration equations only when a is constant over the whole interval.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Changing the origin or positive direction mid-solution, causing unnoticed sign flips.
  • Assuming negative a always means slowing down (ignoring v direction).
  • Substituting speed (always positive) into equations meant for velocity (v).
  • Concluding v=0 implies a=0 (false at turning points under gravity).
