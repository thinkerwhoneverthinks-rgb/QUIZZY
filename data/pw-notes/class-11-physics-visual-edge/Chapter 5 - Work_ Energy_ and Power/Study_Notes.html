SCALAR (DOT) PRODUCT

1Definition & Introduction

  • Core: The scalar (dot) product of vectors A and B is A·B = AB cosθ (a scalar).
  • θ is the smaller angle between the vectors (0° to 180°); sign comes from cosθ.
  • Geometric meaning: Bcosθ is the scalar projection (signed component) of B along A.
  • Component form: A·B = AxBx + AyBy + AzBz.
  • Special cases: A·A = A2; if A ⟂ B, then A·B = 0.

3Key Concepts

  • [geoProjection idea: A·B = A(scalar projection of B on A) = B(scalar projection of A on B).
  • [lawAlgebra: commutative A·B = B·A; distributive A·(B+C)=A·B+A·C.
  • [uniUnit vectors: î·î=ĵ·ĵ=k̂·k̂=1 and î·ĵ=ĵ·k̂=k̂·î=0.
  • [appExam link: Work W = F·d and instantaneous power P = F·v use dot product directly.

4GEOMETRIC MEANING (PROJECTION)

  • A·B = A(Bcosθ) = B(Acosθ): symmetry of projections.
  • If 0° θ θ = 90° ⇒ zero; 90° θ < 180° ⇒ negative.
  • Scalar projection of B on A: (B along A) = Bcosθ = A·BA (for A ≠ 0).

5COMPONENT & UNIT-VECTOR METHOD

  • Write A = Axî + Ayĵ + Azk̂, similarly for B.
  • Expand using distributive law and unit-vector dots to get A·B = AxBx + AyBy + AzBz.
  • Angle from components: cosθ = A·BAB (when A, B ≠ 0).

8⭐Important Points

  • Dot product is scalar: it has magnitude and sign but no direction.
  • Negative dot product implies obtuse angle; zero implies perpendicular vectors (for non-zero vectors).
  • Use component method to avoid angle finding when vectors are given in î, ĵ, k̂ form.
  • A·A = A2 is always ≥ 0; helps compute magnitudes quickly.
  • Work and power are dot products: only the component of force along motion contributes.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Using sinθ instead of cosθ in dot product and hence in work.
  • Forgetting dot product can be negative; writing work as always positive.
  • Treating A·B as a vector or giving it a direction arrow.
  • Mixing vector projection with scalar projection; omitting division by A while computing scalar projection of B on A.

WORK–ENERGY THEOREM

1Definition & Introduction

  • Work–Energy Theorem: Net work done on a particle equals change in its kinetic energy.
  • For a particle, K ≡ 12mv2 (scalar), depends only on speed.
  • Work by a constant force over displacement d: W = F·d = Fdcosθ.
  • In straight-line motion with constant a (and collinear net force), v2 − u2 = 2as gives a quick motivation for WE theorem.
  • For any motion, apply to net force: Wnet = Kf − Ki.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • KC1W is scalar: depends on F component along displacement, not perpendicular component.
  • KC2Use net work: sum of works by all forces (gravity, friction, normal, etc.).
  • KC3Negative work reduces kinetic energy: friction/drag often give W < 0.
  • KC4WE theorem is an integral (energy) form of Newton’s 2nd law; direction details are lost.
  • KC5Raindrop: gravity does + work; air resistance does − work; net gives small ΔK.

4DERIVATION (RECTILINEAR, CONSTANT a)

  • Start with kinematics: v2 − u2 = 2as.
  • Multiply by m2: 12mv2 − 12mu2 = mas.
  • Using Newton: Fnet = ma, so RHS = Fnets = Wnet (valid when net force is constant and collinear with displacement).
  • Hence: Kf − Ki = Wnet.

5VECTOR MOTIVATION + SIGN OF WORK

  • Vector relation (constant a): v2 − u2 = 2a·d.
  • Multiply by m2: 12mv2 − 12mu2 = Fnet·d.
  • If θ < 90°, work positive; if θ = 90°, zero; if θ > 90°, negative.
  • Only forces doing nonzero work change K; perpendicular forces can change direction, not speed.

8⭐Important Points

  • Always use net work: Wnet = ΣWi; then apply Wnet = ΔK.

  • Normal reaction often does zero work if displacement is tangential to the surface.
  • Negative work implies loss of kinetic energy; positive work implies gain.
  • If force varies (1D), work is the signed area under F–x curve.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Using work by a single force instead of Wnet when multiple forces act.
  • Taking W = Fd even when F is not along displacement; forgetting cosθ.
  • Sign errors: friction/drag work is negative when opposite to displacement.
  • Mixing up distance s with displacement vector d in vector form.

Think: net work decides how much speed-squared changes. If force fights motion → W negative → speed drops.

TOPIC3

WORK BY CONSTANT FORCE

1Definition & Introduction

  • Work done by a constant force on a body is W = F·d (scalar).
  • Magnitude form: W = Fdcosθ, where θ is angle between F and d.
  • Sign comes from cosθ: along motion → positive, opposite motion → negative.
  • Unit: joule (J) = N·m; dimensions: [M L² T−2].
  • Work depends on displacement of the point of application; large force with zero displacement gives zero work.

2Diagram

Work by constant force: W depends on the component of F along displacement d.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧭Only the component along displacement does work: F∥ = Fcosθ.
  • ➕Positive work: 0° ≤ θ < 90° ⇒ cosθ > 0 ⇒ energy transfer to the body.
  • ➖Negative work: 90° < θ ≤ 180° ⇒ cosθ < 0 (e.g., friction, braking).
  • 0️⃣Zero work if d=0, or F=0, or F ⟂ d (cos 90° = 0).
  • 🤝Newton’s 3rd law forces are equal/opposite, but their works need not be equal/opposite.

4SIGN OF WORK (EXAM FAVOURITE)

  • W > 0 when force has a component along displacement (cosθ positive).
  • W < 0 when force opposes displacement (often friction; θ = 180°).
  • W = 0 when θ = 90°: force perpendicular to motion (e.g., weight on smooth table).
  • For circular motion with constant speed: centripetal force ⟂ instantaneous displacement ⇒ zero work by centripetal force.

5ZERO-WORK CASES + REAL-LIFE TRAPS

  • Brick wall: large applied force, but wall’s displacement is zero ⇒ work on wall is zero.
  • Weightlifter holding load: force upward, displacement of load zero ⇒ zero work on load.
  • Newton’s 3rd law & work: during skidding/braking, road’s friction does negative work on the cycle; cycle’s force on road does zero work if the road doesn’t move.
  • Work is about external displacement of the body, not about internal fatigue/energy use.

9⭐Important Points

  • Work is a scalar; sign is meaningful and comes from dot product.
  • Use θ between F and d, not between F and velocity unless direction same.
  • If several forces act, work by each force adds algebraically: Wnet = ΣWi.
  • Normal reaction often does zero work when displacement is along surface (perpendicular).
  • Newton’s 3rd law does not imply equal-and-opposite works; depends on each body’s displacement.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Using W = Fd without cosθ when force is not parallel to displacement.
  • Taking work as always positive; forgetting negative sign for opposing forces (friction, braking).
  • Claiming “no work” whenever there is force; ignoring the condition d = 0.
  • Saying action-reaction pair do equal and opposite work automatically (false if displacements differ).

11💡Memory Technique

KINETIC ENERGY CONCEPT

1Definition & Introduction

  • Kinetic energy of a body of mass m moving with speed v is K = 12mv2.
  • K is a scalar quantity; it depends on speed, not on direction of velocity.
  • It measures the capacity to do work due to motion; more K → more work possible before stopping.
  • Work done by net force changes kinetic energy: ΔK = Wnet.
  • Same units and dimensions as work: SI unit joule (J), dimensions [ML2T−2].

2 Diagram

Motion → kinetic energy (scalar) → ability to do work; net work changes speed via ΔK.

3Key Concepts

  • 🔢K ∝ v2: doubling speed makes kinetic energy four times.
  • ⚖️For a given K, speed is v = (2K/m)1/2; lighter body moves faster.
  • 🧱Stopping work equals initial kinetic energy if final speed becomes zero (net work brings K to 0).
  • 🧭Direction information is not in K; different directions can have same K.
  • 🎯If kinetic energy becomes a fraction α of initial, speed becomes α1/2 times.

4MINI-DERIVATION (FROM NEWTON + KINEMATICS)

  • For constant acceleration along displacement: v2 − u2 = 2as.
  • Multiply by 12m: 12mv2 − 12mu2 = mas.
  • Using F = ma and (1D) W = Fs when force is along displacement: ΔK = Wnet.

5PERCENT ENERGY LOSS vs SPEED LOSS (EXAM TRAP)

  • If Kf = αKi, then vf = α1/2vi (speed reduces less than energy).
  • Example: α = 0.10 ⇒ vf = (0.10)1/2vi ≈ 0.316vi.

9⭐Important Points

  • K depends on speed squared; a small % change in speed causes a large % change in K.
  • K is always ≥ 0 in classical mechanics; it becomes zero only when speed is zero.
  • Units: 1 J = 1 N m = 1 kg m2 s−2; dimensions [ML2T−2].
  • Work-energy theorem uses net work; individual forces can do positive/negative work.
  • Energy method: ΔK = Wnet works even when acceleration is not constant (need correct net work).

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Using velocity direction in K; kinetic energy depends on speed only.
  • Forgetting unit conversion: grams to kg, km h−1 to m s−1 (×518).
  • Assuming 10% kinetic energy means 10% speed; actually v scales as K1/2.
  • Equating work done by one force to ΔK when multiple forces act (must use net work).

WORK BY VARIABLE FORCE

1Definition & Introduction

  • Work by a position-dependent force in 1-D equals the signed area under the F–x curve from xi to xf.
  • For a very small displacement Δx, treat force approximately constant: ΔW ≈ F(x)Δx.
  • Total work is the sum of small works: W ≈ Σ F(xk)Δx (Riemann sum).
  • Taking limit Δx→0 gives exact work: W = ∫xixf F(x) dx.
  • Where F(x) is positive, work contribution is positive; where F(x) is negative, contribution is negative (for motion along +x).
  • For piecewise forces, split the integral interval-wise and add signed contributions.

2Key Concepts

  • 🧩Approximation idea: many thin rectangles under the curve; thinner ⇒ more accurate.
  • 🧮Riemann sum: Σ F(sample point)Δx becomes ∫ F(x)dx in the limit.
  • ➕Signed area rule: negative F gives negative work for displacement along +x.
  • ✂️Piecewise F(x): integrate each segment separately; watch jumps and sign changes.
  • 🧠Units check: F×x ⇒ N·m = J.

3RIEMANN-SUM IDEA (HOW INTEGRAL ARISES)

  • Divide displacement into n intervals: Δx = xf−xin.
  • On each interval, choose a sample point xk and take ΔWk ≈ F(xk)Δx.
  • Sum: W ≈ Σk=1n F(xk)Δx.
  • As Δx→0 (i.e., n→∞), the sum tends to the definite integral.

4SIGN, PIECEWISE FORCES, AND TRUNK PUSHING GRAPH

  • If motion is along +x and Fx is negative, the work contribution is negative.
  • Signed-area method: compute areas and use the sign of F over each part of x.
  • Piecewise: W = ∫xiaF1dx + ∫axfF2dx + …
  • Typical exam plot: applied force varies with x; friction can be a constant negative line.

  • Using average force × displacement without verifying linear/piecewise behaviour.
  • Wrong limits: forgetting to split at points where the expression for F(x) changes.
  • Treating F(x) as constant over a large Δx when it varies significantly.

WORK–ENERGY THEOREM (VARIABLE FORCE, 1D)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Work–Energy theorem (1D, variable force): change in kinetic energy equals work by net force: Kf−Ki=∫xixfF(x)dx.
  • Starts from Newton’s second law and uses v=dxdt to convert time-derivative to space-derivative.
  • Valid for net force; any number of forces may act, use their resultant along the line of motion.
  • The integral gives signed area under F–x curve: above axis positive work, below axis negative.
  • Gives energy change between two positions but generally does not give direction-by-direction or time details.

2Key Concepts

  • 🧩dK/dt=Fv ⇒ multiply force by speed to get power into kinetic energy.
  • 🧮dK=F dx converts dynamics into an area problem in the F–x plane.
  • 📏Definite integral: Kf−Ki=∫xixfF(x)dx (Eq. 5.8a) and equals net work (Eq. 5.8b).
  • 🧠Integral form of Newton’s second law: time info is integrated out; only energy change remains.
  • 🎯Scalar vs vector: WE theorem is scalar; directional information present in F=ma is lost.

3DERIVATION IN 1D (CORE STEPS)

  • Start with K=12mv2 ⇒ dKdt=mvdvdt.
  • Use Newton: F=mdvdt ⇒ dKdt=Fv.
  • With v=dxdt ⇒ dK=F dx.
  • Integrate from xi to xf: Kf−Ki=∫xixfF(x)dx (Eq. 5.8a).
  • Since W=∫Fdx, we get Kf−Ki=W (Eq. 5.8b).

4SCOPE vs NEWTON’S LAWS (WHAT YOU GAIN / LOSE)

  • Integral form: WE theorem follows from Newton; it is not more fundamental.
  • Scalar result: only energy change; direction data in vector equation F=ma is lost.
  • No explicit time: cannot directly find how long motion took without extra information.
  • Still powerful: avoids solving a(t) or v(t); uses only endpoints.
  • Use net force along motion; include friction, tension component, etc., via their work.

POTENTIAL ENERGY & CONSERVATIVE FORCE

1Definition & Introduction

  • Potential energy V(x) is defined for a conservative force so that work depends only on endpoints.
  • In 1D, a force is conservative if F(x) can be written as F(x) = −dV/dx.
  • Change in potential energy: ΔV = Vf − Vi = −Wcons (work done by the conservative force).
  • Path independence: Wcons depends only on xi, xf, not on the route or speed.
  • Closed-loop criterion: ∮ F·dr = 0 (in 1D, returning to same x gives net work zero).
  • For gravity near Earth (constant g): V(h) = mgh. For a frictionless drop/slide through vertical drop h, energy conservation gives v = √(2gh).

2Diagram

F(x) = −dV/dx: force points opposite to the slope of the potential curve.

3Key Concepts

  • 📉In 1D, F is the negative slope of the V–x graph.
  • 🧭Conservative ⇒ path independent work; non-conservative (friction) ⇒ path dependent.
  • 🔁Conservative ⇔ closed path work zero: ∮ F·dr = 0.
  • 🏔️Gravity near Earth: ΔV = mgΔh; only height matters, not path.
  • 🎯Energy view: work done against conservative force is stored as ΔV.

4FROM WORK TO POTENTIAL (1D CORE LINK)

  • Small displacement: dW = F(x) dx.
  • Define ΔV so that dV = −F(x) dx.
  • Integrate: Vf − Vi = − ∫xixf F(x) dx.
  • If xf = xi, then ΔV = 0 ⇒ closed-loop work is zero.

5GRAVITY NEAR EARTH: PATH INDEPENDENCE + INCLINED PLANE

  • Take upward positive: gravitational force F = −mg (constant near Earth).
  • Potential: V(h) = mgh (choose zero at h=0 reference).
  • Dropping through height h: ΔV = −mgh ⇒ gain in kinetic energy = mgh (if no non-conservative work).
  • Smooth incline: final speed depends only on vertical drop h, not angle: v = √(2gh).

CONSERVATION OF MECHANICAL ENERGY

1Definition & Introduction

  • Mechanical energy of a system: E=K+V; conserved when only conservative forces do work
  • In 1D for a conservative force field: F(x)=−dV/dx, so work depends only on end points
  • Work-energy theorem: ΔK=Wnet; for conservative force Wcons=−ΔV
  • Hence ΔK+ΔV=0 ⇒ Δ(K+V)=0 along the motion
  • If non-conservative forces act, then Δ(K+V)=Wnc (work done by non-conservative forces)

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🔁Conservative force: W depends only on initial/final positions; closed-loop work = 0
  • ⬇️Gravity near Earth: V=mgh (taking V=0 at reference level)
  • 🎯Energy method avoids time; connect speeds to heights directly
  • 🧵For a fixed-length string, tension is radial while instantaneous displacement is tangential ⇒ work by tension = 0
  • 🟣In a vertical circle, using energy + radial force condition at the top gives minimum speed needed to keep the string just taut/slack

4DERIVATION: WHY Δ(K+V)=0 FOR CONSERVATIVE FORCE

  • Work-energy theorem: ΔK=Wnet
  • For conservative force: Wcons=−ΔV (definition of potential energy)
  • If only conservative forces do work: ΔK=−ΔV
  • Therefore ΔK+ΔV=0 ⇒ Δ(K+V)=0
  • So E=K+V=constant between any two points

SPRING POTENTIAL ENERGY

1Definition & Introduction

  • Elastic potential energy stored in an ideal spring at displacement x is V(x) = 12kx2 (taking V(0)=0).
  • Hooke’s law: spring force Fs = −kx; always opposite to displacement from equilibrium.
  • Work done by spring from xi to xf: Ws = 12k(xi2 − xf2) = −ΔV.
  • Spring force is conservative: work depends only on end points; cyclic work is zero.
  • Without non-conservative forces: K + V = constant (mechanical energy conservation).
  • With friction etc.: Δ(K+V) = Wnc; for compression on a rough horizontal surface, Wf=−μmgx.

2Diagram

Ideal spring: Fs=−kx; displacement x from equilibrium sets both restoring force and stored elastic energy.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧲k (N m−1) measures stiffness; larger k ⇒ smaller deformation for the same stored energy.
  • 🧮Ws from 0→x: Ws = −12kx2; external agent does +12kx2 in quasi-static compression/extension.
  • 🔁Cycle: xi→xf→xi gives net spring work 0 (conservative).
  • 🧱Maximum compression occurs when v=0 momentarily; then Kfinal=0 and energy balance is simplest.
  • 🧊In an ideal mass–spring system: as |x| increases, V increases and K decreases, with K+V constant.

4WORK DONE BY A SPRING (ENDPOINTS ONLY)

  • From W = ∫F·dx, with Fs=−kx: Ws= −∫kx dx.
  • 0→x: Ws = −12kx2 (negative for stretching/compressing).
  • xi→xf: Ws = 12k(xi2 − xf2).
  • Cyclic: return to same x ⇒ Ws=0 (conservative test).

5ENERGY METHOD FOR MAX COMPRESSION (WITH/WITHOUT FRICTION)

  • No friction: at max compression v=0 ⇒ 12mv02 = 12kxm2 ⇒ xm = v0√(mk).
  • With kinetic friction on horizontal: Wf = −fkxm = −μmgxm.
  • Energy: 12mv02 = 12kxm2 + μmgxm ⇒ quadratic in xm.
  • Choose the positive root only (compression magnitude).

POWER

1Definition & Introduction

  • Power is the time rate of doing work or energy transfer.
  • Average power over an interval: Pav = ΔWΔt.
  • Instantaneous power: P = dWdt at a particular instant.
  • Using dW = F·dr, instantaneous power becomes P = F·v (dot product).
  • SI unit: watt (W) where 1 W = 1 J s−1; dimensions [P] = ML2T−3.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • ⏱️Pav uses total work in the interval: ΔWΔt; P uses an instant: dWdt.
  • 🧲Only the force component along velocity contributes: P = Fvcosθ.
  • ⬆️Lifting at constant speed: P = Fupv ≈ mgv.
  • 🔌kWh is energy: 1 kWh = (1000 W)(3600 s) = 3.6×106 J.
  • 🐎Horsepower: 1 hp = 746 W (mechanical).

4DERIVATION: P = F·v

  • Work in small displacement: dW = F·dr (dot product).
  • Divide by dt: dWdt = F·drdt = F·v.
  • If F makes angle θ with v: P = Fvcosθ; negative if cosθ < 0 (force component opposes motion).
  • Constant speed vertical lift: F = mg ⇒ P = mgv.

5UNITS & PRACTICAL ENERGY BILLING

  • 1 W = 1 J s−1; 1 kW = 103 W; 1 MW = 106 W.
  • 1 hp = 746 W ⇒ 1 kW ≈ 1.34 hp.
  • Electrical energy used: E = Pt; in homes billed in kWh.
  • 1 kWh = 3.6×106 J; it is energy, not power.

COLLISIONS AND ENERGY LOSS

1Definition & Introduction

  • A collision is a short-time interaction where large internal forces act and total linear momentum is conserved.
  • Impulse J = ∫F dt = Δp; during collision, external impulse is usually negligible.
  • Newton’s third law gives equal and opposite interaction forces, so impulses on the two bodies are equal and opposite.
  • Elastic collision: momentum and kinetic energy both conserved; coefficient of restitution e = 1.
  • Inelastic collision: momentum conserved but kinetic energy decreases; 0 < e < 1.
  • Completely inelastic: bodies stick and move with common velocity vf; maximum kinetic energy loss.

2Key Concepts

  • 🧲Internal forces cancel pairwise; only external impulse can change system momentum.
  • ⏱️Short collision time ⇒ use impulse, not average force details: FavgΔt = Δp.
  • 🎯1D elastic shortcut: relative speed of separation = relative speed of approach.
  • 📐2D: conserve momentum separately in x and y; energy adds extra equation only if elastic.
  • 🎱For equal masses, one at rest, perfectly elastic: outgoing velocities are perpendicular.

31D COLLISION TOOLKIT (ALONG A LINE)

  • Momentum: m1u1 + m2u2 = m1v1 + m2v2.
  • Restitution: e = v2 − v1u1 − u2, with speeds along the collision line.
  • Elastic ⇒ e = 1 ⇒ v2 − v1 = u1 − u2.
  • 1D elastic results: v1 = (m1−m2)u1+2m2u2m1+m2.
  • 1D elastic results: v2 = 2m1u1+(m2−m1)u2m1+m2.

4ENERGY LOSS + 2D COLLISIONS

  • Kinetic energy loss: ΔK = Ki − Kf ≥ 0 for inelastic collisions.
  • Completely inelastic (stick): vf = m1u1+m2u2m1+m2.
  • Energy loss in sticking (1D/any direction): ΔK = m1m22(m1+m2)(u1−u2)² along same line.
  • 2D momentum: px conserved and py conserved separately.
  • Equal masses, one at rest, elastic: v1 ⟂ v2 (billiard right-angle result).
