Werner's Theory
of Coordination
Compounds
Postulates:
1. Metal shows two types of valency:
(i) Primary valency: ionisable, satisfies oxidation state
(ii) Secondary valency: non-ionisable, satisfied by neutral molecules or anions
2. Every metal tends to satisfy both its primary and secondary valencies
3. Secondary valency = coordination number (CN)
4. Ligands arranged around metal in definite geometry
Example:
Co: primary valency = 3 (3 ions ionise)
Co: secondary valency = 6 (6 coordinate)
Formula: [Co(NH3)6]Cl3
Proposed by Alfred Werner (1893); Nobel
Prize 1913
Primary valency = oxidation number of metal
Secondary valency = coordination number
: [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl → precipitates
1 with
: [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2 → precipitates
2
: [Co(NH3)6]Cl3 → precipitates 3
: [Co(NH3)3Cl3] → precipitates 0
Verified by conductivity and precipitation experiments

Important
Definitions
Coordination entity: central metal atom/ion + ligands
Central atom/ion: Lewis acid; accepts electron pairs
Ligand: Lewis base; donates lone pair to central metal
Coordination number (CN): total number of
σ-bonds from ligands to metal
(= number of donor atoms directly bonded to metal)
Coordination sphere: central atom + ligands; written in [ ]
Counter ions: ions outside coordination sphere
Coordination polyhedron: spatial arrangement of donor atoms around metal
Oxidation number: charge on metal if all ligands removed with their electron pairs
Homoleptic: all ligands same type — e.g., [Co(NH3)6]3+
Heteroleptic: different ligands — e.g., [Co(NH3)4Cl2]+
Ligands provide lone pairs → coordinate (dative) bond formed Central atom usually
transition metal (but not restricted to) ≠ oxidation state; they are independent
Common CNs: 2, 4, 6 (most stable and common: 6)
= 2: linear (e.g., [Ag(NH3)2]+)
= 4: tetrahedral or square planar
= 6: octahedral (most common for transition metals)
Oxidation number can be zero (e.g., [Ni(CO)4], Ni = 0)

Nomenclature
of Coordination
Compounds
Rules (IUPAC):
1. Cation named before anion (as in ionic compounds)
2. Within coordination entity:
(a) Ligands named before central metal
(b) Anionic ligands: suffix '-o' (e.g., = chlorido, = cyanido, = hydroxido)
(c) Neutral ligands: usual name (exceptions:
= ammine, = aqua, = carbonyl, = nitrosyl)
(d) Cationic ligands: suffix '-ium'
3. Ligands listed alphabetically (prefix di/tri etc. not counted for order)
4. Oxidation state of metal in parentheses as
Roman numeral
5. Complex cation/neutral: metal name unchanged
6. Complex anion: metal name ends in '-ate' (Latin name for some metals)
Fe → ferrate, Cu → cuprate, Au → aurate, Pb → plumbate, Sn → stannate
Prefixes: di, tri, tetra, penta, hexa (simple ligands) bis, tris, tetrakis, pentakis, hexakis (complex

Isomerism in Coordination
Compounds
A. Structural Isomerism:
1. Ionisation isomers: differ in ions inside/outside coordination sphere
[Co(NH3)5Br]SO4 vs [Co(NH3)5SO4]Br
2. Hydrate/Solvate isomers: differ in inside/ outside sphere
[Cr(H2O)6]Cl3 (violet) vs [Cr(H2O)5Cl]Cl2·H2O (grey-green) vs [Cr(H2O)4Cl2]Cl·2H2O (dark
green)
3. Linkage isomers: ambidentate ligand binds via different atom
[Co(NH3)5(NO2)]Cl2 (nitro, N-bonded) vs
[Co(NH3)5(ONO)]Cl2 (nitrito, O-bonded)
4. Coordination isomers: ligands distributed differently in cation and anion
[Co(NH3)6][Cr(CN)6] vs [Cr(NH3)6][Co(CN)6]
B. Stereoisomerism:
1. Geometrical (cis-trans) isomerism:
Square planar [MA2B2]: cis (same ligands adjacent) vs trans (opposite)
Octahedral [MA4B2]: cis vs trans
Octahedral [MA3B3]: fac (facial) vs mer (meridional)
2. Optical isomerism:
Non-superimposable mirror images = enantiomers
Rotate plane polarised light: d (dextro, +) and l (laevo, −)
e.g., [Co(en)3]3+: Δ (right-handed) and Λ (left- handed)
cis-[CoCl2(en)2]+ also shows optical isomerism trans-[CoCl2(en)2]+ does NOT (has plane of
symmetry)
Isomers: same molecular formula, different arrangements
Ionisation isomers: different number of ions in solution → different conductivity
Hydrate isomers: water outside sphere can be removed by dehydration
Linkage isomers distinguished by IR spectroscopy
Square planar MA2B2: cis = same side; trans
= opposite; only 2 isomers
Octahedral MA3B3: fac = one triangular face has 3A; mer = 3A in meridional plane
Optical isomers rotate polarised light in opposite directions
Chiral molecule = no plane, centre, or alternating axis of symmetry

Valence Bond Theory (VBT)
Metal provides empty hybrid orbitals; ligands donate lone pairs
Inner orbital complexes: use (n-1)d orbitals for hybridisation
d2sp3 hybridisation → octahedral (inner orbital)
dsp2 hybridisation → square planar (inner orbital)
Outer orbital complexes: use nd orbitals for
hybridisation
sp3d2 hybridisation → octahedral (outer orbital)
sp3 hybridisation → tetrahedral
Common hybridisations & geometries:
=2: sp → linear
=4: sp3 → tetrahedral; dsp2 → square planar
=6: d2sp3 → octahedral (inner); sp3d2 → octahedral (outer)
Examples:
[Fe(CN)6]4-: Fe2+(d6), strong field → d2sp3 → inner, low spin, diamagnetic
[FeF6]3-: Fe3+(d5), weak field → sp3d2 → outer, high spin, 5 unpaired
[Ni(CN)4]2-: Ni2+(d8), strong → dsp2 → square planar, diamagnetic
[NiCl4]2-: Ni2+(d8), weak → sp3 → tetrahedral, 2 unpaired
[Co(NH3)6]3+: Co3+(d6), strong → d2sp3 → inner, diamagnetic
Strong field ligands (cause pairing): >
> > en > > py
Weak field ligands (don't cause pairing):
> > > >
Inner orbital (low spin): ligands cause pairing of d electrons first
Outer orbital (high spin): d electrons remain
unpaired
VBT limitations: cannot explain colour, spectra, or thermodynamic stability of
complexes
Does not predict magnitude of magnetic moment quantitatively in all cases

Crystal Field Theory (CFT) — Basic
Concepts
Ligands treated as point charges (anions) or point dipoles (neutral)
Metal-ligand interaction: purely electrostatic
In free metal ion: all 5 d orbitals degenerate (same energy) In octahedral field: d orbitals split into two
sets:
eg (higher energy): dz2 and dx2−y2 (point towards ligands)
t2g (lower energy): dxy, dyz, dxz (point between ligands)
Crystal field splitting energy (CFSE/Δo):
eg = +0.6 Δo (above barycentre)
t2g = −0.4 Δo (below barycentre)
Pairing energy (P): energy required to pair 2 electrons in one orbital
If Δo > P → low spin (strong field); electrons pair in t2g
If Δo < P → high spin (weak field); electrons fill all
5 d orbitals first
In tetrahedral field: d orbitals split into e and t2 sets
Δt = 4/9 Δo (tetrahedral splitting much smaller)
No tetrahedral complex is low spin (Δt too small)
In square planar field: dxy stabilised; dx2−y2 destabilised most
CFT explains: colour, magnetic properties, CFSE, stability
barycentre: weighted average energy of all d orbitals remains constant
CFSE: stabilisation energy due to splitting
t2gᵃ egᵇ: CFSE = a(−0.4Δo) + b(+0.6Δo)
For d0 and d10: CFSE = 0
Maximum CFSE: d3 and d8 (octahedral, strong field d6)
Spectrochemical series (ligand field
strength):

< ox2- < < < py < < en < 2-

Colour in Coordination
Compounds
d-d transition: electron absorbs visible light photon → jumps from t2g to eg
Energy: ΔE = hν = hc/λ = Δo
Colour observed = complementary colour of light absorbed
Complementary colour pairs:
Violet (400nm) ↔ Yellow-green
Blue (450nm) ↔ Orange
Blue-green (490nm) ↔ Red
Green (570nm) ↔ Violet
Yellow (580nm) ↔ Violet
Orange (600nm) ↔ Blue
Red (620nm) ↔ Blue-green
Examples:
[Ti(H2O)6]3+: absorbs green/yellow (~500nm) → purple/violet
[Cu(H2O)4]2+: absorbs red (~800nm) → blue
[Ni(H2O)6]2+: absorbs red → green
Anhydrous : no d-d transition → white/ colourless
Condition for colour: partially filled d orbitals
AND ligand field splitting d0 (Sc3+, ) and d10 (Zn2+, ): colourless (no d-d transition
possible) (Mn = +7, d0) is intensely purple: charge transfer (CT) transition, not d-d
Charge transfer transitions: much more intense than d-d transitions (orbitally allowed)
Higher Δo (stronger ligand field) → shorter λ absorbed → different colour
Colour depends on: metal, oxidation state, nature of ligands, geometry

Bonding in Metal
Carbonyls
Metal carbonyls: metal + ligands; metal in zero oxidation state
Examples:
[Ni(CO)4]: , d10, tetrahedral, diamagnetic
[Fe(CO)5]: , d8, trigonal bipyramidal
[Cr(CO)6]: , d6, octahedral
[Mn2(CO)10]: Mn–Mn bond; each , d7
[Co2(CO)8]: Co–Co bond; each
Effective Atomic Number (EAN) rule (18-electron rule):
Stable metal carbonyls obey 18-electron (noble gas) configuration
[Ni(CO)4]: = 10e- + 4×CO(2e-) = 18e-
[Fe(CO)5]: = 8e- + 5×CO(2e-) = 18e-
[Cr(CO)6]: = 6e- + 6×CO(2e-) = 18e-
Bonding in metal carbonyls:
σ-bond: lone pair on C of → empty metal d orbital (σ-donation)
π back-bond: filled metal d orbital → empty π* of (back-donation)
Synergic bonding: strengthens both M–C bond and weakens C≡O bond
is the strongest field ligand (due to back- bonding)
Back-bonding: π* of accepts electron density from metal
This strengthens M–C bond (shorter, stronger) and weakens C≡O (longer, lower frequency)
Synergic bonding explains why binds so strongly to metals
EAN rule: used to predict formula of metal carbonyls
Odd-electron metals form binuclear carbonyls with M–M bond
