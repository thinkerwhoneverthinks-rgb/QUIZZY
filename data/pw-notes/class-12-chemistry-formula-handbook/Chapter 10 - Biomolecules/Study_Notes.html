Proteins
Four Levels of Structure (NCERT)
(1°) Primary Structure:
Specific sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide chain
Held by: covalent peptide bonds
(2°) Secondary Structure:
Folding of polypeptide chain
(a) α-helix: right-handed coil
Held by: intrachain H-bonds
3.6 residues per turn
(b) β-pleated sheet: zigzag arrangement
Held by: interchain H-bonds
(3°) Tertiary Structure:
3D folding of entire polypeptide
Held by: H-bonds, disulphide bonds (–S– S–),
hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds
Gives functional/native conformation
(4°) Quaternary Structure:
Assembly of 2 or more polypeptide subunits
e.g., Haemoglobin: 4 subunits (2α + 2β)
Held by: same forces as 3° structure
Primary determines all higher structures
α-helix examples: Keratin (hair, nails, wool)
β-sheet examples: Silk fibroin
Disulphide bond:
–Cys–S–S–Cys– Strongest covalent bond in 3° structure
Globular proteins: Spherical (enzymes, hormones, Hb)
Fibrous proteins: Long fibres (structural, keratin, collagen, silk)

Denaturation
of Proteins
Definition (NCERT):
Disruption of native 3D structure of protein without breaking peptide bonds Loss of
biological activity
Causes of denaturation:
Physical: heat, UV radiation, mechanical
Chemical: strong acids/bases, urea, detergents, organic solvents, heavy metal salts
What breaks during denaturation:
✖ Peptide bonds (NOT broken)
✔ H-bonds
✔ Hydrophobic interactions
✔ Disulphide bonds (partial)
✔ Ionic interactions
Examples (NCERT):
Boiling an egg: albumin denatures
Curdling of milk: casein denatures
Coagulation of egg white on heating
Renaturation:
Removal of denaturing agent can sometimes restore native structure (refolding)
Primary structure is NOT lost on denaturation
Sequence of AA unchanged; only 3D shape disrupted
Enzymes lose catalytic activity on denaturation
Chaperone proteins help in refolding
Urea: disrupts H-bonds (common denaturing agent in lab)

Enzymes
Properties &
Mechanism (NCERT)
Definition:
Biological catalysts; mostly proteins
(some RNA = ribozymes)
Properties (NCERT):
(i) Highly specific (one enzyme → one substrate)
(ii) Increase rate without being consumed
(iii) Active at optimum pH and temperature
(iv) Lower activation energy (Ea)
Mechanism (Lock & Key):
E + S ⇌ ES → E + P
E = enzyme, S = substrate
ES = enzyme-substrate complex
P = product
Induced fit model:
Active site changes shape to fit substrate
Factors affecting enzyme activity:
Temperature: optimum ~37°C (human enzymes)
pH: optimum ~7.4 (blood enzymes);
Pepsin ~2 (stomach); Trypsin ~8
Inhibitors: competitive (block active site) non- competitive (alter active site)
Cofactors:
Inorganic ions (e.g., Zn2+ in carbonic anhydrase)
Coenzymes: organic (e.g., NAD+, FAD, vitamins)
Prosthetic groups: tightly bound cofactors
Active site:
Specific region on enzyme where substrate binds
Apoenzyme:
Protein part only Holoenzyme:
Apoenzyme + cofactor Km (Michaelis constant): substrate conc. at half Vmax Low Km
→ high affinity
Competitive inhibitor:
Structurally similar to substrate; reversible
Most enzymes are named by adding '–ase' to substrate

Nucleic Acids
DNA & RNA
Monomers = Nucleotides:
Nucleotide = Nitrogenous base + Sugar + Phosphate
Nucleoside = Nitrogenous base + Sugar (no phosphate)
Nitrogenous bases (NCERT):
Purines: Adenine (A), Guanine (G)
Pyrimidines: Cytosine (C), Thymine (T) [DNA only] Uracil (U) [RNA only, replaces T]
Sugars:
DNA: 2-Deoxyribose (C5H10O4)
RNA: Ribose (C5H10O5)
Watson-Crick base pairing (NCERT):
A = T (2 hydrogen bonds) [DNA]
G ≡ C (3 hydrogen bonds)
A = U (2 hydrogen bonds) [RNA]
DNA double helix (NCERT):
Two antiparallel strands
Right-handed helix (B-form)
Diameter: ~2 nm
Pitch (per turn): ~3.4 nm
Base pairs per turn: 10
Distance between base pairs: 0.34 nm
RNA types:
mRNA: carries genetic message from DNA
tRNA: carries amino acids to ribosome (cloverleaf structure; anticodon loop)
rRNA: structural component of ribosome
3',5'-phosphodiester bond links nucleotides
DNA: double stranded
RNA: usually single stranded
Chargaff's rules:
%A = %T
%G = %C (in double-stranded DNA)
Central dogma:
DNA → RNA → Protein
Codon: 3 bases on mRNA coding for one amino acid
Anticodon: on tRNA
Genome: total DNA in an organism

DNA Replication, Transcription &
Translation
(Central Dogma)
Central Dogma of Molecular Biology:
DNA → (Replication) → DNA
DNA → (Transcription) → mRNA
mRNA → (Translation) → Protein
DNA Replication (NCERT):
Semi-conservative replication
Each strand acts as template
New strand synthesised 5'→3' direction
Enzyme: DNA Polymerase
Primer required (short RNA)
Transcription:
Template strand (antisense): 3'→5'
mRNA synthesised 5'→3'
Enzyme: RNA Polymerase
Location: nucleus (eukaryotes)
Translation:
mRNA read 5'→3' at ribosome
Codon (3 bases on mRNA) → 1 amino acid
64 codons total; 61 sense + 3 stop codons
Start codon: AUG (Met)
Stop codons: UAA, UAG, UGA
Genetic code is universal & degenerate
Genetic code:
Universal: same in all organisms
Degenerate: one AA can have multiple codons
Unambiguous: one codon → one AA
Wobble hypothesis: 3rd base of codon less specific
tRNA: adaptor molecule
5' – anticodon –3' carries AA at 3' –CCA– end
Ribosomes: site of translation 70S (prokaryote)
80S (eukaryote)
