Classification of Haloalkanes
Based on number of halogen atoms:
Monohaloalkane: R–X (1 halogen)
Dihaloalkane: R(X)2 (2 halogens)
Trihaloalkane: R(X)3 (3 halogens)
Based on C–type:
1°: –CH2X | 2°: –CHX | 3°: ≡ C–X
X = F, Cl, Br, I
General formula: Cnn+1X for monohaloalkane.
Primary (1°): halogen on C bonded to 1 other C.
Secondary (2°): halogen on C bonded to 2 C atoms.
Tertiary (3°): halogen on C bonded to 3 C atoms.

Common names: Methyl chloride (CH3Cl), Ethyl bromide (C2H5Br), etc.

Nature of C–X Bond
C–X bond is polar covalent (Cλ+–Xλ–)
Bond length: C–F < C–Cl < C–Br < C–I
Bond enthalpy: C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I
Reactivity: R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F
Electronegativity: F > Cl > Br > I
Higher polarity of C–X bond → more susceptibility to nucleophilic attack.
C–I bond is weakest (longest), hence most reactive toward nucleophiles.

SN1 Reaction
(Unimolecular
Nucleophilic
Substitution)
Rate = k[R–X]
Step 1: R–X → R+ + X- (slow, rate- determining)
Step 2: R+ + Nu- → R–Nu (fast)
Racemization (if chiral centre)
Reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3X
SN1 favoured by: 3° substrate, polar protic solvent, weak nucleophile.
Carbocation intermediate; more stable carbocation
→ faster SN1.
Rearrangements possible (hydride/methyl shift).
Racemic mixture formed due to planar carbocation attacked from both sides.
7
E2 Elimination (Bimolecular)
Rate = k[R–X][Base]
One-step: Base removes β-H simultaneously as X- leaves
Anti-periplanar geometry required (β-H and X must be anti)
Saytzeff’s rule: Major product = more substituted alkene
Favoured by: strong bulky base, high temperature, 2°/3° halides.
Example: (CH3)3C–Br + KOH(alc.) → (CH3)2C= +
Competing with SN2; higher temperature favours
E2.
8
E1 Elimination (Unimolecular)
Rate = k[R–X]
Step 1: R–X → R+ + X- (slow)
Step 2: Base removes β-H from R+ (fast) → alkene
Competes with SN1 at 3° halides
Favoured by: weak base, polar protic solvent, 3° halides, high temperature.
Often accompanies SN1 (same conditions).
Saytzeff product preferred.

18
Reactivity of Haloarenes vs
Haloalkanes
Haloarenes are much less reactive than haloalkanes toward nucleophilic substitution
Reasons:
1. C–X bond has partial double bond character (resonance)
2. C–X bond shorter & stronger in Ar–X
3. Phenyl cation (from Ar–X ionization) is highly unstable
4. π electron cloud repels nucleophile
Resonance makes C–X bond stronger in ArX.
Bond length: C–Cl in chlorobenzene (1.69 Å) < in chloroalkane (1.77 Å).
Nucleophilic substitution in haloarenes requires harsh conditions (high T, high P).

Electrophilic Aromatic
Substitution (EAS) of Haloarenes
Halogens are ortho-para directors (o/p directors)
Despite being deactivating (electron- withdrawing by induction)
Main products: ortho + para substituted
Due to lone pair donation by X into ring (+M
> –I effect)
+M (mesomeric donation) of X → makes o/p positions electron-rich.
–I (inductive withdrawal) → deactivates ring overall.
Halogen = weak deactivator but o/p director.
In EAS, electrophile attacks more electron-rich positions (o and p).
