Amines: organic derivatives of ammonia (NH3) One or more H atoms replaced by alkyl/aryl groups
Classification by degree of substitution:
Primary (1°): R– (one H replaced)
Secondary (2°): R2NH (two H replaced)
Tertiary (3°): R3N (three H replaced)
Classification by nature of R group:
Aliphatic: R = alkyl e.g.,
Aromatic: R = aryl e.g., (aniline)
Mixed: e.g., – (N-methylaniline)
Aliphatic IUPAC: alkan-1-amine
= methanamine; = ethanamine
(CH3)2NH = N-methylmethanamine
(CH3)3N = N,N-dimethylmethanamine
Aromatic amines: parent = aniline (C6H5NH2)
o-, m-, p- for substituted anilines
N-methyl for substituent on N
Quaternary ammonium salt: R4N+X- (N has 4 bonds, positive charge)

Physical Properties of Amines
State: lower amines (CH3NH2, (CH3)2NH, (CH3)3N) are gases at RT
– amines: gases; higher amines: liquids or solids
Smell: lower aliphatic amines smell like fish/ammonia
Aniline and higher aryl amines: characteristic disagreeable smell
Boiling points:
1° > 2° amines (for same MW) due to stronger
H-bonding (2 N–H bonds vs 1)
3° amines: lowest bp (no N–H bonds, no H-bonding)
Amines have lower bp than alcohols (N– weaker than O–)
Solubility:
Lower amines (C1–): miscible with water (H-bonding with water)
Solubility decreases with increasing carbon chain
Aniline: slightly soluble in water; soluble in organic solvents
Density: less than water Colour: most amines are colourless (oxidise slowly in air → coloured)
H-bonding in amines: N– (weaker
than O– in alcohols)
Boiling point order (similar MW): 1° amine > 2° amine > 3° amine
But all amines have lower bp than corresponding alcohols
Aniline: oily, colourless liquid; turns brown on exposure to air due to oxidation
Trimethylamine (tertiary): most fishy smell; present in fish
Water solubility: amines form H-bonds with water → soluble up to
Aromatic amines more toxic than aliphatic; aniline is absorbed through
skin

Basic Character of Amines
Amines are Lewis bases (donate lone pair from N) and Brønsted bases
R– + ⇌ R– +
Kb = [R–NH3+][OH-]/[R–]
pKb = −log Kb; higher pKb = weaker base
Basic strength order — Aliphatic amines (in gas phase):
3° > 2° > 1° > (purely inductive effect)
Basic strength order — Aliphatic amines (in aqueous solution):
2° > 1° > 3° > (for methyl amines due to solvation) (CH3)2NH > > (CH3)3N >
Kb values (approx):
= 4.4×10-4; (CH3)2NH = 5.4×10-4; (CH3)3N
= 5.9×10-5
Aromatic vs aliphatic amines:
Aliphatic amines >> Aniline (C6H5NH2)
Aniline: Kb ≈ 3.8×10-10 (very weak base)
Substituted anilines:
p-NO2-aniline < p-Cl-aniline < aniline < p-CH3-aniline
< p-OCH3-aniline (electron withdrawing groups decrease basicity; electron donating increase)
Basicity order in aqueous solution: influenced by 3 factors:
(i) Inductive effect: +I of alkyl groups increases electron density on N →
more basic
(ii) Solvation (hydration): + ions
stabilised by water → increases basicity
Solvation order: 1° > 2° > 3° (more N–H bonds → more H-bonding with water)
(iii) Steric effect: bulky groups hinder protonation → decreases basicity
In aqueous solution, 2° wins because: +I (2 alkyl groups) + solvation (1 N–H left)
3° has most +I but least solvation and most steric → ends up less basic than 2°
Aniline very weak base: lone pair on
N delocalised into benzene ring → less available for protonation
EWG on ring: further reduces lone pair availability → even weaker base
EDG on ring: slightly increases basicity
Diphenylamine (Ka = 10-13) and triphenylamine: almost non-basic

Distinction Between 1°, 2° and 3° Amines
Test 1: Nitrous acid test (NaNO2 + , 0–5°C):
1°: effervescence (N2 gas) for aliphatic; stable diazonium for aromatic
2°: yellow oily N-nitrosamine
3° aliphatic: no visible reaction; forms soluble salt
3° aromatic: green/blue colour (C-nitroso product)
Test 2: Carbylamine test (CHCl3 + ):
1°: foul smell (isocyanide formed) 2° and 3°: no reaction
Test 3: Hinsberg test (C6H5SO2Cl + ):
1°: forms KOH-soluble sulphonamide (clear solution)
2°: KOH-insoluble sulphonamide (precipitate)
3°: no reaction
Test 4: Acylation (acetyl chloride or acetic
anhydride):
1° and 2°: amide formed (less basic smell, precipitate if solid) 3°: does not react (no N–H bond to substitute)

Comparison of Basicity
— Summary
Basicity order (in water, for common amines):
(CH3)2NH > > (CH3)3N >
(C2H5)2NH > > (C2H5)3N >
Aromatic amine vs ammonia:
<< (lone pair delocalisation weakens basicity)
Substituted anilines (para):
p- > p- > aniline > p-Cl > p-Br > p-
(EDG increases basicity, EWG decreases basicity)
Diphenylamine < aniline (two rings withdraw more electron density)
Triphenylamine ≈ neutral (barely basic)
Amide vs amine:
R–– << R– (amide lone pair delocalised into C=O → very weak base)
Three competing factors for aqueous basicity of aliphatic amines:
(i) +I effect: 3° > 2° > 1° → increases basicity
(ii) Solvation: 1° > 2° > 3° → increases basicity (more N–H for H-bonding
with water)
(iii) Steric hindrance: 3° > 2° > 1° → decreases basicity
Net result in water: 2° wins for methyl and ethyl amines
For bulky groups (like tert-butyl): steric effect dominant → basicity order may
change
Pyridine (aromatic): pKb = 8.75 (lone pair in sp2 orbital, not in π system →
less basic than aliphatic but more than aniline)
Pyrole (aromatic): lone pair part of aromatic π system → almost non-basic
