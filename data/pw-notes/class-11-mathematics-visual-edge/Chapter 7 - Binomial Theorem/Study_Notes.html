BINOMIAL THEOREM

TOPIC1

NEED FOR BINOMIAL THEOREM

1Definition & Introduction

  • Need: For higher powers like (a+b)5, repeated multiplication becomes long and error-prone.
  • Binomial theorem provides a direct way to write the fully simplified expansion of (a+b)n (here, for positive integers n).
  • It is especially useful when a number is close to a convenient base: write it as (base ± small) and expand.
  • Coefficients follow a fixed combination pattern nCr (same as Pascal’s triangle).
  • In successive terms, power of a decreases by 1, power of b increases by 1, and the total power stays n.

2Diagram

Higher powers make direct multiplication bulky; binomial theorem provides a direct expansion pattern.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩For large n, repeated multiplication creates many intermediate terms before simplification.
  • ⚡Binomial theorem gives the final collected expansion directly.
  • 🔁Term pattern: coefficients are nCr; powers of a descend and b ascend.
  • 🎯Near-base numbers like 98=100−2 and 101=100+1 become easier to handle after rewriting.
  • 🧠nCr counts ways to choose r times "b" from n factors of (a+b).

4WHY REPEATED MULTIPLICATION BECOMES HARD

  • To expand (a+b)5, you must multiply (a+b) five times and then collect like terms.
  • Intermediate expressions quickly grow in number of terms.
  • Most mistakes happen while collecting signs and coefficients.
  • For positive integer n, the final expansion must have exactly (n+1) terms.

5WHAT BINOMIAL THEOREM ADDS (CHAPTER RESTRICTION: POSITIVE INTEGERS)

  • Gives the fully simplified expansion without writing messy intermediate products.
  • Powers follow: an, an−1b, ..., bn.
  • Coefficients follow Pascal’s triangle / combinations: nC0, nC1, ..., nCn.
  • Helpful for numbers near bases like 100, 1000, 1 (rewrite as base ± small).

6Repeated Multiplication vs Binomial Pattern

7Solved Q&A

1
Why is (98)5 harder than (98)2 by direct multiplication?
Answer:
For a square/cube, you can use known identities like (a−b)2 and (a−b)3 quickly.
For a 5th power, repeated multiplication produces many intermediate terms before simplification.
Binomial theorem avoids these intermediate steps by giving a direct term pattern.
✓  Higher powers make manual multiplication lengthy and error-prone; binomial theorem gives a direct expansion.
2
How does rewriting near a base show the need for binomial theorem?
Answer:
Rewrite a difficult number as base ± small, e.g., 101 = 100 + 1 or 999 = 1000 − 1.
Then (base ± small)n becomes an expansion where powers of the base are easy to compute.
Binomial theorem supplies the correct coefficients and term structure for any positive integer n.
✓  Near-base rewriting becomes practical only when a systematic expansion rule (binomial theorem) is available.

8⭐Important Points

  • Main need: avoid long repeated multiplication for high powers of a binomial.
  • For positive integer n, (a+b)n has exactly (n+1) terms in its expansion.
  • Term structure: power of a decreases by 1 each step; power of b increases by 1 each step.
  • Coefficients come from combinations and match Pascal’s triangle entries.
  • Near-base calculations become systematic: write number as (base ± small) and expand.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Trying to expand high powers by repeated multiplication and losing terms during collection.
  • Forgetting that the number of terms should be (n+1) for positive integer n.
  • Mixing up the descending/ascending power pattern of a and b in successive terms.
  • Confusing Pascal’s triangle coefficients for different n (wrong row).

10💡Memory Technique

“DESCEND–ASCEND–CHOOSE”
DESCEND: power of a goes n, n−1, ..., 0.ASCEND: power of b goes 0, 1, ..., n.CHOOSE: coefficients are nC0, nC1, ..., nCn.
TOPIC2

PATTERNS IN BINOMIAL EXPANSIONS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Binomial expansion (for non-negative integer n) expresses (a+b)n as a sum of terms with powers of a and b.
  • For non-negative integer n, the expansion has exactly n+1 terms.
  • Across successive terms: power of a decreases by 1, power of b increases by 1.
  • In every term, (power of a) + (power of b) = n.
  • Coefficients follow binomial coefficients nCr (Pascal triangle pattern).

2Diagram

Across terms: a exponent steps down, b exponent steps up, sum stays n.


3Key Concepts

  • 🔢Term structure (n is a non-negative integer): Tr+1 = nCr an−rbr, r = 0 to n
  • 📉Exponents shift: an → an−1 → … → a0
  • 📈Exponents shift: b0 → b1 → … → bn
  • 🧩Index-sum invariant: (n−r)+r = n in every term
  • 🧱Coefficients are symmetric: nCr = nCn−r

4FROM (a+b)⁰ TO (a+b)⁴: SEE THE PATTERN

  • (a+b)0 = 1 → terms = 1
  • (a+b)1 = a + b → terms = 2
  • (a+b)2 = a2 + 2ab + b2 → terms = 3
  • (a+b)3 = a3 + 3a2b + 3ab2 + b3 → terms = 4
  • (a+b)4 = a4 + 4a3b + 6a2b2 + 4ab3 + b4 → terms = 5

5SUCCESSIVE TERMS: QUICK CHECKLIST

  • Total terms = n+1; first term is an, last term is bn (for non-negative integer n).
  • General term: coefficient × an−rbr; r increases 0,1,2,…,n.
  • Degree check: each term has total degree n (sum of exponents).
  • Coefficient symmetry gives matching pairs equidistant from ends.

6PATTERN SPOTTING IN (a+b)ⁿ TERMS

Sum of exponents (of a and b)n+0 = n(n−r)+r = n0+n = n

Write the pattern of powers using r = 0,1,2,…,n (n is a non-negative integer)
(a+b)n = nC0anb0 + nC1an−1b1 + … + nCran−rbr + … + nCna0bn
Hence the (r+1)th term (counting from 1) is

1

Solution:
Here n=7 and 4th term ⇒ r+1=4 ⇒ r=3.

T4 = 7C3(2x)433.

Multiply: 35×16×27 = 560×27 = 15120.
✓  4th term = 15120x4.

Solution:
General term: Tr+1 = 5Cr x5−r 2r.
Need power of x to be 3: 5−r = 3 ⇒ r = 2.
So coefficient of x3 is 5C2 22.
5C2=10 and 22=4 ⇒ 10×4=40.
✓  Coefficient of x3 is 40.

9⭐Important Points

  • Number of terms in (a+b)n is n+1 (count r = 0 to n), for non-negative integer n.
  • Exponents follow an−rbr; track r to avoid wrong power.
  • Sum of exponents in each term is always n (useful for quick verification).
  • First and last coefficients are 1; coefficients are symmetric about middle term(s).
  • Middle term(s): if n is even, one middle term; if n is odd, two middle terms.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Counting terms incorrectly (forgetting r=0 term), writing n terms instead of n+1.
  • Making both powers decrease or both increase; correct pattern is opposite shifts.
  • Using wrong r when asked for k-th term (remember k-th term ⇒ r=k−1).
  • Forgetting to raise numerical parts (like 3 or 2x) to the correct power.

11💡Memory Technique

“Down-Up-Sum = n”
Down: exponent of a goes n, n−1, …, 0.Up: exponent of b goes 0, 1, …, n.Quick check: in every term, exponents add to n.

TOPIC3

PASCAL'S TRIANGLE

1Definition & Introduction

  • Pascal’s triangle is an array where row n gives coefficients of (a+b)n.
  • Each row starts and ends with 1; rows are indexed n=0,1,2,…
  • Every interior entry equals the sum of the two entries above it (additive rule).
  • Row n contains (n+1) numbers; it is symmetric about the middle.
  • Also called Meru Prastara, described by Pingla in ancient India.

2Key Concepts

  • 🧱Additive pattern: each entry = upper-left + upper-right
  • 🧮Row n equals nC0, nC1, …, nCn
  • 🪞Symmetry: nCr = nCn−r
  • 📌For index 5, row is 1 5 10 10 5 1
  • ⚙️Use row + power pattern to expand (p+q)n without repeated multiplication

3HOW TO FORM THE TRIANGLE (FAST RULES)

  • Start with row n=0: 1.
  • Next row: write 1 at both ends.
  • Each interior number = (upper-left) + (upper-right).
  • Row n is symmetric: read same left→right and right→left.

4USING A ROW TO EXPAND (p+q)n

  • Take coefficients from row n: nC0 … nCn.
  • Write terms: (p)n, (p)n−1(q), …, (q)n.
  • Multiply each term by the matching coefficient; total terms = n+1.
  • For (2x+3y)5, use row: 1 5 10 10 5 1.

5Formulas

Pascal’s triangle entries are combinations:
Row n = nC0, nC1, …, nCn
Additive rule comes from combinations:
nCr = n−1Cr−1 + n−1Cr
Use coefficients to write the binomial expansion:
(a+b)n = Σr=0n nCr an−rbr
n =positive integer index in (a+b)n
r =term position (0 to n)
nCr =binomial coefficient = n!r!(n−r)!
a, b =two terms of the binomial (can be numbers/variables/expressions)
Row-to-expansion rule
(p+q)n has coefficients from row n of Pascal’s triangle

7⭐Important Points

  • Row index equals the power n in (a+b)n; do not shift by 1.
  • Total terms in expansion = n+1; powers: an→a0, b0→bn.
  • Coefficients are symmetric: 1 5 10 10 5 1 for n=5.
  • Additive rule: boundary 1’s; interior from sums of adjacent upper entries.
  • For (2x+3y)5, include factors 2k and 3k inside each term.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Using row 6 instead of row 5 for power 5 (index mismatch).
  • Forgetting to apply powers to 2 and 3 in (2x) and (3y) terms.
  • Mixing power pattern: writing x powers increasing instead of decreasing.
  • Arithmetic slips in middle terms: 10·8·9 = 720 (often miscomputed).

TOPIC4

BINOMIAL COEFFICIENTS AS nCr

1Definition & Introduction

  • Binomial coefficient nCr counts ways to choose r objects from n and equals the r-th entry in Pascal’s row n.
  • nCr = n!r!(n−r)!, defined for integers with 0 ≤ r ≤ n, n ≥ 0.
  • Pascal row n written in combination form is nC0, nC1, …, nCn (so you need not build earlier rows).
  • Edge values: nC0 = nCn = 1.
  • Symmetry: nCr = nCn−r (row reads same forwards/backwards).
  • Pascal addition rule: (n−1)Cr−1 + (n−1)Cr = nCr generates each interior entry.

2Diagram

Each Pascal entry equals a combination nCr; row n is nC0…nCn.


3Key Concepts

  • 🔺Pascal triangle row n = coefficients in (a+b)n = nC0…nCn
  • 🧩Combinations meaning: choose r items; order ignored ⇒ nCr
  • ↔️Fast computation: use symmetry nCr=nCn−r to reduce factorial work
  • 🧱Row index 7 directly: 7C0 to 7C7 gives full row without writing rows 0–6

4REWRITING PASCAL’S TRIANGLE IN nCr FORM

  • Row index n has (n+1) entries: nC0, nC1, …, nCn.
  • First and last entries are 1 because nC0=nCn=1.
  • Interior entries satisfy Pascal identity: nCr = (n−1)Cr−1 + (n−1)Cr.
  • Symmetry gives quick fill: entry positions r and n−r are equal.

5ROW FOR INDEX 7 (DIRECTLY, WITHOUT BUILDING TRIANGLE)

  • Write the row as 7C0, 7C1, 7C2, 7C3, 7C4, 7C5, 7C6, 7C7.
  • Compute: 7C0=1, 7C1=7, 7C2=21, 7C3=35.
  • Use symmetry: 7C4=35, 7C5=21, 7C6=7, 7C7=1.
  • So row 7 numbers are 1, 7, 21, 35, 35, 21, 7, 1.

6Formulas

Define combinations using factorials (valid for integers 0 ≤ r ≤ n):
nCr = n!r!(n−r)!
Show Pascal identity from factorial form (for 1 ≤ r ≤ n−1):
(n−1)Cr−1 + (n−1)Cr = (n−1)!(r−1)!(n−r)! + (n−1)!r!(n−1−r)!
Take common factor (n−1)!r!(n−r)!:
= (n−1)!r!(n−r)![r + (n−r)] = (n−1)!·nr!(n−r)! = n!r!(n−r)! = nCr
Symmetry (swap r and n−r in the factorial formula):
nCr = nCn−r
n =Row index / total objects (non-negative integer)
r =Chosen objects / position in row (integer, 0 ≤ r ≤ n)
n! =Factorial: n! = n(n−1)(n−2)…1, and 0! = 1
Direct row-writing rule
Row n in Pascal’s triangle: nC0, nC1, …, nCn

7Solved Examples

1
Write the complete row for index 7 of Pascal’s triangle using nCr, and evaluate the numbers.
Solution:
Row 7 is 7C0, 7C1, 7C2, 7C3, 7C4, 7C5, 7C6, 7C7.
7C0=1 and 7C7=1.
7C1=7!1!6!=7 ⇒ by symmetry 7C6=7.
7C2=7!2!5!=7×62×1=21 ⇒ 7C5=21.
7C3=7!3!4!=7×6×53×2×1=35 ⇒ 7C4=35.
✓  Row 7 = 1, 7, 21, 35, 35, 21, 7, 1.
2
Using combination form, find the 4th entry (from the left) in row index 10 of Pascal’s triangle.
Solution:
Row 10 entries are 10C0, 10C1, 10C2, 10C3, … (counting from left starts at r=0).
4th entry from left corresponds to r=3, so value is 10C3.
10C3=10!3!7!=10×9×83×2×1.
Compute: 10×9×86 = 7206 = 120.
✓  The 4th entry in row 10 is 120.

8⭐Important Points

  • Pascal row numbering starts at n=0: row 0 is just 1 (= 0C0).
  • In row n, the (r+1)-th entry from left equals nCr.
  • Always ensure 0 ≤ r ≤ n; otherwise nCr is not defined in this syllabus.
  • Use nCr=nCn−r to avoid big factorials.
  • Pascal identity builds next row quickly and is consistent with factorial definition.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Using wrong r due to entry counting starting from 1 instead of 0.
  • Forgetting symmetry and doing long factorial computations unnecessarily.
  • Writing nCr with r outside 0…n (e.g., 7C8).
  • Cancelling factorials incorrectly: not reducing before multiplying.

10💡Memory Technique

“Row n = ‘choose r’ list”
Say aloud: Row n is choose-0 to choose-n.To get k-th entry from left, use r = k−1 then compute nCr.To halve work, mirror it: … nC2, nC1, nC0 on the right.

BINOMIAL THEOREM (STATEMENT)

1Definition & Introduction

  • For positive integer n, (a+b)n expands into a sum of terms with binomial coefficients.
  • The expansion has (n+1) terms, with powers of a decreasing and powers of b increasing.
  • The coefficient of each term is a combination number nCr (called a binomial coefficient).
  • General term (r+1)th is nCr an−rbr, where r=0,1,2,…,n.
  • In every term, the sum of exponents of a and b equals n.

2Diagram

Each next term decreases power of a by 1 and increases power of b by 1.

3Key Concepts

  • 📌Statement: (a+b)n = nC0an + nC1an−1b + … + nCnbn
  • 🔁Term structure: a power: n to 0; b power: 0 to n
  • 🧷General term: (r+1)th term Tr+1=nCran−rbr
  • 🧩Binomial coefficient (for integers 0 ≤ r ≤ n): nCr= n!r!(n−r)!
  • 🧠Total terms: r runs 0→n, so exactly n+1 terms appear

4TERM STRUCTURE (WHAT CHANGES FROM TERM TO TERM?)

  • There are n+1 terms: from r=0 (first term) to r=n (last term).
  • In term with parameter r: exponent of a is n−r, exponent of b is r.
  • Every term has total degree (n−r)+r=n.

5HOW TO LOCATE A SPECIFIC TERM QUICKLY

  • The (r+1)th term is Tr+1=nCran−rbr.
  • The term containing ak has k=n−r ⇒ r=n−k (must be an integer between 0 and n).
  • The term containing bm has r=m directly (with 0 ≤ m ≤ n).
  • This statement is for positive integer n, so the expansion has finite n+1 terms.

6Formulas

Binomial theorem (statement form):
(a+b)n = nC0an + nC1an−1b + nC2an−2b2 + … + nCnbn
Compact summation form:
(a+b)n = ∑r=0n nCran−rbr
Definition of binomial coefficient (integers 0 ≤ r ≤ n):
nCr= n!r!(n−r)!
General term (r+1)th term:
Tr+1=nCran−rbr
n =positive integer index (n = 1,2,3,...)
a, b =algebraic quantities (numbers/variables/expressions)
r =term parameter, integer with 0 ≤ r ≤ n
nCr =binomial coefficient (number of combinations of r from n)
Tr+1 =(r+1)th term in the expansion (counting starts from 1)
Core statement + general term
(a+b)n = ∑r=0n nCran−rbr and Tr+1=nCran−rbr

7Solved Examples

1

Solution:
Use (a+b)n=∑r=0n nCran−rbr with a=2x, b=3y, n=5.
Binomial coefficients: 5C0=1, 5C1=5, 5C2=10, 5C3=10, 5C4=5, 5C5=1.
Write the terms: (2x)5 + 5(2x)4(3y) + 10(2x)3(3y)2 + 10(2x)2(3y)3 + 5(2x)(3y)4 + (3y)5.
Compute: (2x)5=32x5; 5(2x)4(3y)=5·16x4·3y=240x4y.
10(2x)3(3y)2=10·8x3·9y2=720x3y2.
10(2x)2(3y)3=10·4x2·27y3=1080x2y3.
✓  (2x+3y)5=32x5+240x4y+720x3y2+1080x2y3+810xy4+243y5
2
Find the 4th term in the expansion of (x+2)6.
Solution:
For (a+b)n, the (r+1)th term is Tr+1=nCran−rbr.
Here a=x, b=2, n=6. 4th term ⇒ r+1=4 ⇒ r=3.
So T4=6C3x6−3·23.
Compute: 6C3= 6!3!3!=20 and 23=8.
Thus T4=20·x3·8=160x3.
✓  4th term = 160x3

8⭐Important Points

  • For this chapter section, the statement is used for positive integer n, giving a finite expansion.
  • General term indexing: (r+1)th term corresponds to exponent of b being r.
  • Number of terms is n+1; first term has b0, last term has a0.
  • Degree check: each term has total power n; helps verify expansions quickly.
  • Symmetry of coefficients: nCr=nCn−r.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Using wrong r for the k-th term: forgetting k=r+1.
  • Dropping a0 or b0 terms; they equal 1 but must be included.
  • Swapping exponents: writing arbn−r instead of an−rbr.
  • Incorrect combination values, especially middle terms like 6C3=20.

10💡Memory Technique

“ABC of each term: A-power, Binomial, C-power”
Think: C(n,r) sits in the middle, powers sit on sides.Left power: an−r (decreases), right power: br (increases).r goes 0→n, so you automatically get first to last term.

INDUCTION PROOF (PRINCIPLE OF MATHEMATICAL INDUCTION)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Mathematical induction is a method to prove a statement P(n) for all natural numbers.
  • Step 1: Prove the base case (usually P(1) or P(0)).
  • Step 2: Assume P(k) is true for an arbitrary natural number k (induction hypothesis).
  • Step 3: Using the hypothesis, prove P(k+1) is true (induction step).
  • Then P(n) is true for every natural number in the intended range.

2Diagram

Standard induction flow: base case → induction hypothesis → induction step → conclusion.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩Write the exact statement P(n) with its domain (e.g. for all n≥1).
  • ✅Base case: check the first allowed value (n=0 or n=1) carefully.
  • 🔁Induction hypothesis: assume P(k) is true; do not re-prove it.
  • 🔗Induction step: transform the (k+1) case until the k case appears.
  • 🧠Conclusion: base case + (P(k)⇒P(k+1)) implies truth for all n in the range.

4HOW TO DO THE INDUCTION STEP (COMMON PATTERNS)

  • Goal: start from the expression in P(k+1) and rewrite it to involve the form in P(k).
  • Often use algebraic rewriting like splitting: f(k+1)=f(k)+[extra term], or factoring: A(k+1)=A(k)·[factor].
  • After substitution of the induction hypothesis, simplify to reach exactly the required statement.

5EXAMPLE (BRIEF): BINOMIAL THEOREM VIA INDUCTION

  • Let P(n): (a+b)n=∑r=0n nCran−rbr for n≥1.
  • Base case n=1 is immediate: (a+b)=1C0a+1C1b.
  • Induction step uses (a+b)k+1=(a+b)(a+b)k, index shift, then Pascal identity.

6Solved Q&A

1
Why is it necessary to assume P(k) for an arbitrary k, and not only verify a few values like n=1,2,3?
Answer:
Checking finitely many cases only proves the statement for those values.
Induction requires a link: proving P(k)⇒P(k+1) ensures the truth propagates from the base case to all larger integers.
Without the implication step, there is no guarantee beyond the checked values.
✓  Induction works because it establishes a chain from the base case to every next integer; finite checking cannot do this.
2
In an induction proof, what is the most common logical mistake with the induction hypothesis?
Answer:
Treating P(k) as something to be proved again, instead of using it as an allowed assumption inside the induction step.
Using P(k+1) (or a stronger statement than P(k)) while trying to prove P(k+1), which becomes circular.
✓  The key mistake is circular reasoning: using the result for (k+1) (directly or indirectly) instead of only using the assumed P(k).

7⭐Important Points

  • State the domain clearly (e.g. n≥1 or n≥0) before starting.
  • Base case must match the smallest allowed n in the domain.
  • In the induction step, only assume P(k) and prove P(k+1); do not assume P(k+1).
  • Final line should explicitly invoke the principle of induction to conclude "true for all n".

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Wrong base case (proving P(1) when the statement starts at n=0, or vice versa).
  • Not specifying the domain of n, leading to a mismatch in base case/conclusion.
  • Circular use of P(k+1) while proving P(k+1).
  • Proving P(k)⇒P(k+1) but forgetting to combine it with the base case.

9💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: B-A-S-C (Base–Assume–Show–Conclude)”
B: Prove the base case.A: Assume P(k) (induction hypothesis).S: Show P(k+1) using the hypothesis.C: Conclude P(n) for all n in the domain.

SUMMATION FORM AND PROPERTIES (BINOMIAL THEOREM)

1Definition & Introduction

  • For a positive integer n, (a+b)n = ∑k=0n nCk an−kbk.
  • Here nCk are binomial coefficients (same as the n-th row of Pascal triangle).
  • Expansion has exactly (n+1) terms corresponding to k=0,1,2,…,n.
  • In the k-th term (starting k=0), power of a decreases, power of b increases.
  • In every term, (power of a) + (power of b) = n; also use b0=1, a0=1.

2Diagram

Σ form organizes terms by k: a-exponent falls, b-exponent rises, sum of exponents stays n.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩General term: Tk+1 = nCkan−kbk, k=0…n
  • 🔢Number of terms = n+1; first term k=0, last term k=n
  • 📉Powers: a : n,n−1,…,0 and b : 0,1,…,n (successive terms differ by 1)
  • ⚖️Index-sum rule: (n−k)+k=n in every term
  • 🏷️Binomial coefficient: nCk=n!k!(n−k)!, with symmetry nCk=nCn−k

4Σ-NOTATION: HOW TO READ IT

  • ∑k=0n means: add terms obtained by putting k=0,1,2,…,n.
  • Term at k=0 gives nC0anb0 = an (since nC0=1, b0=1).
  • Term at k=n gives nCna0bn = bn (since nCn=1, a0=1).
  • In general, coefficient for an−kbk is exactly nCk.

5KEY PROPERTIES EXAMINERS LOVE

  • Number of terms is n+1 (not n): because k takes n+1 integer values from 0 to n.
  • Power progression: successive terms change exponents by 1: a: (n−k)→(n−k−1), b: k→k+1.
  • Index-sum rule: exponents of a and b always add to n; quickly checks term correctness.
  • Coefficient symmetry: nCk=nCn−k pairs terms equidistant from ends.
  • Edge coefficients: nC0=nCn=1; next ones are nC1=nCn−1=n.

6Formulas

8⭐Important Points

  • Always state k-range: k=0 to n gives exactly n+1 terms.
  • Use Tk+1=nCkan−kbk to target a specific power quickly.
  • Index-sum rule (n−k)+k=n is the fastest self-check for exponents.
  • Endpoints: first term an, last term bn; both have coefficient 1.
  • Coefficient symmetry: coefficients mirror about the middle: nCk=nCn−k.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing Σ from k=1 to n and losing the k=0 term (misses an).
  • Using wrong general term: mixing akbn−k with an−kbk unintentionally.
  • Forgetting b0=1 or a0=1, leading to missing first/last term.
  • Taking number of terms as n instead of n+1.

SPECIAL CASES OF EXPANSION (BINOMIAL THEOREM)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Special cases arise by choosing a, b in (a+b)n to get quick standard expansions.
  • For (x−y)n, signs alternate because powers of (−y) contribute (−1)k.
  • For (1+x)n, coefficients are nCk with all positive terms.
  • For (1−x)n, coefficients are nCk with alternating signs.
  • Setting x=1 yields identities: sum of coefficients =2n, alternating sum =0 (for n≥1).

2Diagram

Flow: choose substitutions to get (x−y)n, (1±x)n, and key coefficient identities.

3Key Concepts

  • 🔁Sign rule: (−y)k = (−1)kyk ⇒ alternating +,−,+,−,…
  • 📌Coefficient sum: put a=b=1 ⇒ (1+1)n=2n=∑k=0nnCk
  • ⚖️Alternating sum: put a=1,b=−1 ⇒ (1−1)n=0=∑k=0n(−1)knCk (for n≥1)
  • 🧩In (1±x)n, the coefficient of xk is (±1)knCk

4STANDARD VARIANTS (WRITE DIRECTLY)

  • (x−y)n = ∑k=0n nCk xn−k(−y)k = ∑ nCk(−1)k xn−kyk
  • So terms alternate signs: xn − nC1xn−1y + nC2xn−2y2 − … + (−1)nyn
  • (1+x)n = ∑k=0n nCk xk (all positive)
  • (1−x)n = ∑k=0n (−1)knCk xk (alternating signs)

5CONSEQUENCES (COEFFICIENT SUMS)

  • Sum of coefficients of (1+x)n equals its value at x=1: 2n.
  • Alternating sum of coefficients of (1+x)n equals its value at x=−1: 0 (for n≥1).
  • More generally: ∑k=0n(−1)knCk = (1−1)n (for n≥1).

APPLICATIONS AND EXAMPLES (BINOMIAL THEOREM)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Binomial theorem expands (a+b)n (positive integer n) into a sum of n+1 terms using binomial coefficients.
  • Typical use: rewrite numbers as (easy base ± small) and expand to compute quickly.
  • Also used to compare magnitudes by keeping first few terms (all remaining terms positive when x>0).
  • Useful to prove divisibility/remainder statements by factoring out a fixed power like 25, 64, etc.
  • Gives standard identities such as (a+b)4−(a−b)4 and symmetric sums (x+1)6+(x−1)6.

2Diagram

Binomial coefficients can be read from Pascal’s triangle (row gives coefficients).

3Key Concepts

  • 🔢General term: Tr+1 = nn!r!(n−r)! an−rbr, for r=0…n
  • 📌Number of terms in (a+b)n is n+1; powers of a decrease, powers of b increase; sum of exponents is always n.
  • 🧱For (x−y)n, signs alternate: coefficient has factor (−1)r with yr.
  • ⚡Quick comparison: for x>0, in (1+x)n all terms after 1+nx are positive, so it is strictly greater than 1+nx.
  • 🧩Remainder/divisibility: expand (1+m)n; terms from m2 onward share factor m2.

4FAST NUMERICAL COMPUTATION

  • Rewrite number near a base: N = A ± h, then expand (A±h)n.
  • Example idea: (98)5 = (100−2)5; only 6 terms.
  • For minus case, signs alternate: (A−h)n = An − nAn−1h + nn!2!(n−2)!An−2h2 − …

5COMPARISON, IDENTITIES, AND DIVISIBILITY

  • Comparison (x>0): (1+x)n = 1 + nx + (positive terms) ⇒ (1+x)n > 1 + nx.
  • Even/odd symmetry: (x+1)n + (x−1)n cancels odd powers of 1; doubles even-power terms.
  • Remainder idea: write 6n = (1+5)n; all terms from 52 onward have factor 25, enabling mod 25 results.

8⭐Important Points

  • To expand, fix coefficients from nC0 to nCn and attach powers: an−rbr.
  • In (x−y)n, sign of term with yr is (−1)r.
  • For quick magnitude: if x>0, then (1+x)n > 1 + nx (since remaining terms are positive).
  • For remainder/divisibility, express number as (1+m) and separate 1 + nm from terms containing m2 (or higher).
  • Useful identities come from adding/subtracting expansions of (a+b)n and (a−b)n (cancellation of odd/even terms).

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Wrong general term: mixing up powers as arbn−r instead of an−rbr.
  • For (a−b)n, forgetting alternating signs (using all +).
  • Arithmetic slips with large bases (e.g., 1005, 1004×2) and with 2², 2³, …
  • Using comparison inequality without checking x>0 (needed so omitted terms are positive).
