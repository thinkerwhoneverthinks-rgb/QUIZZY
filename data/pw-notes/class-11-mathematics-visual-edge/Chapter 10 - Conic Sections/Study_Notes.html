CONIC SECTIONS

TOPIC1

CONIC SECTIONS OVERVIEW

1Definition & Introduction

  • Conic sections are curves obtained by intersecting a plane with a double-napped right circular cone.
  • A cone is generated by rotating a line (generator) around a fixed line (axis) at constant semi-vertical angle α.
  • Let β be the angle made by the intersecting plane with the cone’s vertical axis; comparing β with α classifies the conic.
  • If the plane meets one nappe only (and not the vertex): circle / ellipse / parabola; if it meets both nappes: hyperbola.
  • If the plane passes through the vertex, the section is degenerate: point, line, or pair of intersecting lines.

2Key Concepts

  • 🗿Vertex splits the cone into two nappes; a non-degenerate hyperbola requires cutting both nappes.
  • 📐Classification key: compare the plane angle β with the cone angle α.
  • 🧠Apollonius gave the names parabola and hyperbola.
  • 🛰️Applications: planetary motion (ellipse), reflectors/antennas (parabola), optics & navigation (circle).

3CLASSIFICATION BY ANGLES (α vs β)

  • Circle: β = 90°; plane ⟂ axis; cuts one nappe (not through vertex).
  • Ellipse: α < β < 90°; cuts one nappe completely.
  • Parabola: β = α; plane ∥ a generator; cuts one nappe.
  • Hyperbola: 0 ≤ β < α; plane cuts both nappes.

4DEGENERATE SECTIONS (PLANE THROUGH VERTEX)

  • Point: α < β ≤ 90°; plane meets the cone only at the vertex.
  • Line: β = α; plane contains a generator (degenerate parabola).
  • Pair of intersecting lines: 0 ≤ β < α (degenerate hyperbola).
  • Later, conics are also studied using coordinate geometry via standard equations and geometric properties.

6Solved Q&A

1
A plane makes angle β = 90° with the cone axis and intersects one nappe away from the vertex. Identify the conic.
Answer:
β = 90° means the plane is perpendicular to the axis.
Such a section (not through the vertex) is a non-degenerate circle.
✓  Circle.
2
For a cone with semi-vertical angle α = 35°, a plane intersects it with β = 20° and does not pass through the vertex. Identify the conic.
Answer:
Here 0 ≤ β < α because 20° < 35°.
This forces the plane to cut both nappes.
Since it does not pass through the vertex, the section is non-degenerate: a hyperbola.
✓  Hyperbola.

7⭐Important Points

  • Exam keyword: a double-napped cone is needed to obtain a non-degenerate hyperbola.
  • Angle test: β = α ⇒ parabola (plane ∥ generator).
  • Circle needs β = 90° and must not pass through the vertex.
  • Degenerate cases occur only when the plane passes through the vertex.
  • Non-degenerate circle/ellipse/parabola cut one nappe; non-degenerate hyperbola cuts both.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Thinking a hyperbola can be formed by cutting only one nappe.
  • Mixing up α (axis–generator angle) with β (plane–axis angle).
  • Calling the vertex-cut line a non-degenerate parabola (it is degenerate).
  • Forgetting to check whether the plane passes through the vertex before naming the section.

TOPIC2

SECTIONS OF A CONE

1Definition & Introduction

  • Conic sections are curves formed by intersecting a plane with a double-napped right circular cone.
  • A right circular cone is generated by rotating a line (generator) about a fixed line (axis) keeping angle α constant.
  • The fixed point of intersection is the vertex; it splits the cone into two parts called nappes.
  • Let β be the angle between the intersecting plane and the cone axis; the value of β decides the conic type.
  • Cutting away from the vertex gives non-degenerate conics; cutting through the vertex gives degenerate conics.

2Diagram

Cone parameters: semi-vertical angle α; plane makes angle β with axis; napes are above/below vertex.

3Key Concepts

  • 📌Semi-vertical angle α = angle between generator and axis (fixed for a given cone).
  • 🧭Plane angle β is measured with the axis (not with the base).
  • ⭕β = 90° ⇒ plane ⟂ axis ⇒ circle (cuts one nappe).
  • 🥚α < β < 90° ⇒ ellipse (cuts one nappe, not parallel to generator).
  • 🪃β = α ⇒ parabola (plane ∥ a generator).
  • 🧨0° ≤ β < α ⇒ hyperbola (plane meets both nappes).

4CLASSIFICATION BY ANGLES (NON-DEGENERATE)

  • Circle: β = 90° (plane ⟂ axis), intersects exactly one nappe.
  • Ellipse: α < β < 90°, intersects one nappe and is a closed curve.
  • Parabola: β = α, plane ∥ generator; curve is open and unbounded.
  • Hyperbola: 0° ≤ β < α, plane meets both nappes; two open branches.

5DEGENERATE SECTIONS (PLANE THROUGH VERTEX)

  • Point: α < β ≤ 90° and plane passes through the vertex.
  • Single straight line: β = α and plane contains a generator (degenerate parabola).
  • Pair of intersecting lines: 0° ≤ β < α through vertex (degenerate hyperbola).

1

Answer:
Given: plane does not pass through the vertex ⇒ non-degenerate case.

Such a one-nappe, closed intersection is an ellipse.
✓  Ellipse
2
For a fixed cone with semi-vertical angle α, identify the conic for each case (plane not through vertex): (i) β = 90° (ii) β = α (iii) 0° ≤ β < α.
Answer:
(i) β = 90° ⇒ plane ⟂ axis ⇒ circle.
(ii) β = α ⇒ plane ∥ generator ⇒ parabola.
(iii) β < α ⇒ plane meets both nappes ⇒ hyperbola.
✓  (i) Circle (ii) Parabola (iii) Hyperbola

8⭐Important Points

  • Compare β with α; this comparison classifies the conic section.
  • β is measured with the axis of the cone (vertical axis in the standard figure).
  • Hyperbola occurs only when the plane intersects both nappes.
  • Parabola is the boundary case when the plane is parallel to a generator.
  • Degenerate sections occur only when the cutting plane passes through the vertex.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Taking β as the angle between plane and generator instead of plane and axis.
  • Assuming any slant cut gives ellipse; forgetting that β < α gives hyperbola.
  • Mixing up ‘cone angle’ (2α) with ‘semi-vertical angle’ (α).
  • Forgetting the special case β = 90° (circle) when plane is perpendicular to axis.

10💡Memory Technique

If β > α, the plane stays within one nappe ⇒ ellipse (or circle at 90°).β = 90° ⇒ plane ⟂ axis ⇒ circle.β = α ⇒ plane ∥ generator ⇒ parabola.If β < α, the plane cuts both nappes ⇒ hyperbola.

TOPIC3

DEGENERATE CONICS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Degenerate conics are conic sections obtained when the cutting plane passes through the vertex of a right circular cone.
  • Type depends on angle β made by plane with cone axis, compared with semi-vertical angle α of the cone.
  • If α < β ≤ 90°, the plane through the vertex does not contain a generator and the intersection reduces to the single point V.
  • If β = α, the plane through the vertex contains exactly one generator; the section is that straight line (degenerate parabola).
  • If 0 ≤ β < α, the plane through the vertex cuts both nappes; the section is a pair of intersecting straight lines (degenerate hyperbola).

2Diagram

Degenerate sections occur only when the cutting plane passes through the vertex V.

3Key Concepts

  • 📐Compare angles: α = semi-vertical angle; β = plane–axis angle
  • 📍α < β ≤ 90° (plane through vertex) ⇒ intersection is only the point V
  • 📏β = α (through vertex) ⇒ plane contains a generator ⇒ one line
  • ✖️0 ≤ β < α (through vertex) ⇒ intersects both nappes ⇒ two lines
  • 🧠Degenerate parabola: line; degenerate hyperbola: pair of intersecting lines

4CLASSIFICATION (PLANE THROUGH VERTEX)

  • Point: α < β ≤ 90° ⇒ plane meets cone only at V.
  • Straight line: β = α ⇒ plane contains a generator ⇒ one line.
  • Pair of intersecting lines: 0 ≤ β < α ⇒ plane cuts both nappes ⇒ two generators.

5LINK WITH CARTESIAN FORMS (USEFUL IN QUESTIONS)

  • A general second-degree curve: Ax2 + 2Hxy + By2 + 2Gx + 2Fy + C = 0.
  • It represents a pair of straight lines iff the determinant Δ = 0.
  • If the pair passes through origin: Ax2 + 2Hxy + By2 = 0; put y = mx to get the two slopes.
  • For H = 0: Ax2 + By2 = 0 gives real (non-coincident) lines only if AB < 0.

9Solved Examples

1
Check whether x2 − 5xy + 6y2 = 0 represents a degenerate conic. If yes, find the two lines.
Solution:
It is a homogeneous second-degree equation, so it passes through the origin and can represent a pair of straight lines.
Put y = mx ⇒ x2(1 − 5m + 6m2) = 0.
So 6m2 − 5m + 1 = 0.
Factor: 6m2 − 3m − 2m + 1 = (3m − 1)(2m − 1) = 0.
Thus m = 13 or m = 12.
Hence the lines are y = 13x and y = 12x.
✓  Yes; it is a pair of intersecting lines: 3y − x = 0 and 2y − x = 0.
2
Find the degenerate hyperbola (pair of lines) represented by x2 − 9y2 = 0 and give the acute angle between the lines.
Solution:
Factor: x2 − 9y2 = (x − 3y)(x + 3y) = 0.
So the lines are x − 3y = 0 and x + 3y = 0.
Their slopes are m1 = 13 and m2 = −13.
Angle formula: tanθ = |(m1 − m2)/(1 + m1m2)|.
Compute: numerator = 13 − (−13) = 23; denominator = 1 + (−19) = 89.
So tanθ = (23) ÷ (89) = (23) × (98) = 34.
✓  Pair of lines: x − 3y = 0 and x + 3y = 0; acute angle θ = tan−1(3/4).

10⭐Important Points

  • Degenerate conics here mean plane passes through the vertex of the cone.
  • Always compare β with α: β > α ⇒ point; β = α ⇒ line; β < α ⇒ pair of lines (all with plane through vertex).
  • When β = α, plane contains a generator; intersection is that generator line.
  • When 0 ≤ β < α, plane meets both nappes through V giving two generators.
  • In coordinate geometry, a pair of lines is a degenerate second-degree curve with determinant Δ = 0.

11❌Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up α (cone angle) with β (plane angle).
  • Saying β = α gives two lines; actually it gives one generator line.
  • Assuming every Δ = 0 case is intersecting lines; it may also be coincident lines.
  • While factoring, missing common factors leading to wrong line equations.

12💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: GEP (Greater–Equal–Pair)”
β Greater than α (and ≤ 90°), through vertex ⇒ Point at V.β Equal to α, through vertex ⇒ plane contains Generator ⇒ one line.β less than α, through vertex ⇒ hits both nappes ⇒ Pair of lines.

CIRCLE EQUATION

1Definition & Introduction

  • A circle is the set of all points in a plane equidistant from a fixed point (centre).
  • The fixed point is the centre C(h,k); the constant distance is the radius r.
  • For any point P(x,y) on the circle, distance CP equals r.
  • Using distance formula gives the standard equation: (x−h)²+(y−k)²=r².
  • Expanding yields the general form x²+y²+2gx+2fy+c=0; recover centre and radius by completing squares.

2Diagram

Circle: every point P(x,y) is at distance r from centre C(h,k).

3Key Concepts

  • 📍Centre–radius idea: all boundary points satisfy CP=r.
  • 📏Distance formula converts the geometric definition to an algebraic equation.
  • 🟦Standard form: (x−h)²+(y−k)²=r².
  • 🔁General → standard by completing squares in x and y separately.
  • ✅From general form, r²=g²+f²−c: if r²>0 real circle; =0 point circle; <0 no real circle.

4DERIVATION (STANDARD FORM)

  • Let centre C(h,k), radius r, and P(x,y) on circle.
  • By definition: CP=r (equidistant condition).
  • Distance formula: CP²=(x−h)²+(y−k)².
  • Hence (x−h)²+(y−k)²=r².

5GENERAL FORM ↔ STANDARD FORM (COMPLETING SQUARES)

  • Expand standard: x²+y²−2hx−2ky+(h²+k²−r²)=0.
  • Write general: x²+y²+2gx+2fy+c=0.
  • Centre from general: (−g,−f); radius² = g²+f²−c.
  • Complete squares: (x+g)²+(y+f)²=g²+f²−c.

8Solved Examples

1
Find the equation of the circle with centre (3, −2) and radius 5.
Solution:
Standard form: (x−h)²+(y−k)²=r².
Here h=3, k=−2, r=5 ⇒ (x−3)²+(y+2)²=25.
Expanded (optional): x²−6x+9 + y²+4y+4 = 25 ⇒ x²+y²−6x+4y−12=0.
✓  (x−3)²+(y+2)²=25
2
Find the centre and radius of the circle x²+y²−8x+6y−11=0.
Solution:
Group terms: (x²−8x)+(y²+6y)=11.
Complete squares: x²−8x+16=(x−4)²; y²+6y+9=(y+3)².
Add same constants both sides: (x−4)²+(y+3)² = 11+16+9 = 36.
So centre is (4, −3) and radius is √36=6.
✓  Centre (4, −3), radius 6

9⭐Important Points

  • In standard form, centre is read directly as (h,k) and radius as r.
  • General form for a circle: equal coefficients of x² and y², and no xy term.
  • From x²+y²+2gx+2fy+c=0, centre = (−g,−f).
  • Radius² = g²+f²−c; check sign before taking √.
  • Completing squares: after making coefficient of x² (or y²) equal to 1, add (linear coefficient2)² inside the bracket.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting to square the radius: writing (x−h)²+(y−k)²=r instead of r².
  • Sign error in centre: from x²+2gx..., centre is (−g,−f), not (g,f).
  • Completing square but not balancing the equation by adding the same constant to RHS.
  • Mixing up (y−k)² vs (y+k)² when k is negative.

11💡Memory Technique

“SHIFT–SQUARE–RADIUS”
Circle = shifted origin: replace x by (x−h) and y by (y−k).Then square and add: (x−h)²+(y−k)².Right side is always r² (never r).

TOPIC5

PARABOLA: DEFINITION AND AXIS

1Definition & Introduction

  • A parabola is the set of points P in a plane such that distance PF equals the perpendicular (shortest) distance from P to a fixed line called the directrix.
  • The fixed point F is the focus; the focus does not lie on the directrix in the non-degenerate case.
  • Distance from a point to a line always means perpendicular distance.
  • The axis of the parabola is the line passing through the focus and perpendicular to the directrix.
  • The parabola is symmetric about its axis.

2Diagram

Parabola: set of all points P such that PF equals the perpendicular distance from P to the directrix.

3Key Concepts

  • NoneLocus condition: PF equals perpendicular distance from P to the directrix.
  • NoneAxis: the unique line through the focus perpendicular to the directrix; it is the symmetry line.
  • NonePoints equidistant from focus and directrix occur in symmetric pairs about the axis.
  • NoneDegenerate case: if focus lies on directrix, the locus becomes the perpendicular line through that point.

4WHY THE AXIS IS PERPENDICULAR TO THE DIRECTRIX

  • Let M be the foot of the perpendicular from focus F to the directrix.
  • The line FM is perpendicular to the directrix by construction and passes through F.
  • Reflection about FM keeps the directrix fixed and keeps PF unchanged (focus is on FM).
  • So points on the parabola occur in mirror pairs about FM; hence FM is the symmetry axis.

5DEGENERATE CASE (FOCUS ON DIRECTRIX)

  • If focus F lies on the directrix, then for any point P, the perpendicular distance from P to the directrix is at most PF.
  • Equality PF = distance(P, directrix) happens only when P lies on the line through F perpendicular to the directrix.
  • Therefore the locus is that perpendicular line (degenerate parabola).

7Solved Q&A

1
Focus is a point F and directrix is the vertical line x = constant. Without finding any equation, what is the direction of the axis?
Answer:
The axis must pass through the focus and be perpendicular to the directrix.
A line perpendicular to a vertical line is horizontal.
✓  The axis is the horizontal line through the focus.
2
A point P is chosen so that PF equals its perpendicular distance to the directrix. If P is reflected across the axis, will the reflected point also lie on the parabola?
Answer:
Reflection about the axis keeps the focus on the axis fixed, so distance to focus remains the same after reflection.
The directrix is perpendicular to the axis, so perpendicular distance to the directrix is also preserved under this reflection.
✓  Yes. The reflected point satisfies the same equality and lies on the parabola.

8⭐Important Points

  • Distance from a point to a line means perpendicular distance.
  • Axis is defined geometrically: the line through the focus perpendicular to the directrix.
  • The parabola is symmetric about its axis.
  • If focus lies on the directrix, the parabola degenerates into the line through that point perpendicular to the directrix.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Using non-perpendicular (slant) distance to the directrix instead of the shortest distance.
  • Thinking the directrix is the axis; they are different lines with a perpendicular relationship.
  • Assuming the axis must be an x-axis or y-axis; that is only for a chosen coordinate setup.

10💡Memory Technique

“F ⟂ D gives A”
Focus F is a point; Directrix D is a line.Axis A is the line through F perpendicular to D.Parabola is symmetric about A.

TOPIC6

STANDARD PARABOLA EQUATIONS

1Definition & Introduction

  • A parabola is the locus of a point whose distance from a fixed point (focus) equals its distance from a fixed line (directrix).
  • Standard forms assume vertex at origin and axis along a coordinate axis (x-axis or y-axis).
  • Parameter a (>0) equals the distance from vertex to focus (and to directrix).
  • If equation contains y2, axis is x-axis; if it contains x2, axis is y-axis.
  • Sign before the linear variable decides opening: + opens toward +axis, − opens toward −axis.

2Diagram

Standard right-opening parabola y² = 4ax with vertex V at origin, focus F(a,0) and directrix x = −a.

3Key Concepts

  • NoneFor y2=4ax: focus (a,0), directrix x=−a, opens right
  • NoneSquared term tells axis; sign of the linear term (x or y) tells opening direction
  • NoneLatus rectum length for any standard parabola = 4a
  • NoneParabola is symmetric about its axis; vertex is midpoint between focus and directrix

4DERIVATION (y2=4ax)

  • Take vertex at origin; focus F(a,0), directrix l: x=−a.
  • Let P(x,y) be any point on parabola; drop PB ⟂ directrix, so B(−a,y).
  • Definition: PF = PB. Square to avoid roots; simplify to get y2=4ax.
  • Conversely, any point satisfying y2=4ax also satisfies PF=PB.

5ORIENTATION RULES (ALL 4 STANDARD FORMS)

  • If y2 present ⇒ axis is x-axis; if x2 present ⇒ axis is y-axis.
  • y2=4ax opens right; y2=−4ax opens left.
  • x2=4ay opens up; x2=−4ay opens down.
  • For each case: vertex (0,0); focus lies on axis at distance a; directrix is opposite at distance a.

9Solved Examples

1
For the parabola y2=12x, find focus, directrix, axis, and latus rectum length.
Solution:
Compare y2=12x with y2=4ax ⇒ 4a=12 ⇒ a=3.

2

ELLIPSE GEOMETRY AND PARAMETERS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Ellipse: locus of points P such that PF1 + PF2 is constant (= 2a).
  • Fixed points F1, F2 are foci; midpoint O of F1F2 is the centre.
  • Line through foci is major axis; perpendicular through centre is minor axis.
  • Major axis length = 2a, minor axis length = 2b, distance between foci = 2c (a > b > 0).
  • Always: 2a > 2c (sum constant is greater than distance between foci).

2Diagram

Centre O, foci F₁,F₂; major axis 2a, minor axis 2b, focal separation 2c.

3Key Concepts

  • 📍At a vertex P on major axis: PF1 + PF2 = (a+c)+(a−c)=2a.
  • 📏At an end of minor axis Q: F1Q = F2Q = √(b²+c²).
  • 🧩Parameter link: a² = b² + c² ⇒ c = √(a² − b²).
  • 🎯Eccentricity: e = c/a, so for an ellipse 0 < e < 1.
  • 🧭Foci are at distance c from centre along major axis; vertices at distance a.

4KEY RELATION: a, b, c

  • Choose vertex point on major axis: distances to foci are a−c and a+c.
  • Choose end of minor axis Q: both distances to foci equal √(b²+c²).
  • Same ellipse ⇒ same constant sum: 2√(b²+c²) = 2a ⇒ a² = b² + c².
  • Hence c = √(a² − b²); and b = √(a² − c²).

5ECCENTRICITY AND SHAPE

  • e = c/a measures how stretched the ellipse is (closer to 1 ⇒ more elongated).
  • For a circle: c = 0 ⇒ e = 0; ellipse has 0 < e < 1.
  • Given a and e: c = ae, and b = a√(1 − e2).

STANDARD ELLIPSE EQUATION

1Definition & Introduction

  • An ellipse is the locus of points P such that PF1 + PF2 = 2a (constant).
  • For standard position: centre at origin, axes along coordinate axes, with a ≥ b > 0.
  • If foci are on x-axis: foci (±c,0) and equation x²a² + y²b² = 1.
  • If foci are on y-axis: foci (0,±c) and equation x²b² + y²a² = 1.
  • Link between parameters: c² = a² − b² and eccentricity e = ca (so 0 < e < 1).
  • Symmetry test: standard ellipses are symmetric about both x-axis and y-axis.

2Diagram

Standard ellipse with centre O(0,0), major axis along x-axis; vertices (±a,0), co-vertices (0,±b), foci (±c,0).

3Key Concepts

  • 🎯Major axis is along the variable whose squared term has the larger denominator.
  • 📌Intercepts: x-intercepts ±a (or ±b), y-intercepts ±b (or ±a) from the standard form.
  • 🧭Foci lie on the major axis: (±c,0) for x-major; (0,±c) for y-major.
  • 🪞Axis tests: only x² and y² (no odd powers) ⇒ symmetry about both coordinate axes.
  • ⚖️Always keep a ≥ b; swap labels if needed after putting in standard form.

4DERIVATION SNAPSHOT (FOCI ON x-AXIS)

  • Take foci F1(−c,0), F2(c,0); point P(x,y) on ellipse.
  • Geometric condition: PF1 + PF2 = 2a, with 0 < c < a.
  • Distance forms: PF1 = √((x+c)²+y²), PF2 = √((x−c)²+y²).
  • After squaring twice and simplifying: x²a² + y²a²−c² = 1.
  • Put b² = a² − c² to get x²a² + y²b² = 1.

5SYMMETRY & AXIS TESTS (EXAM FAST CHECKS)

  • Only even powers of x and y ⇒ symmetric about both x-axis and y-axis.
  • Swap test: replace x by −x and/or y by −y; equation unchanged ⇒ symmetry confirmed.
  • Intercepts: set y=0 gives x-intercepts; set x=0 gives y-intercepts.
  • Major axis direction: bigger denominator corresponds to a² and hence major axis.
  • Bounds: for x-major, −a ≤ x ≤ a and −b ≤ y ≤ b.

HYPERBOLA STANDARD FORMS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Hyperbola: set of points P such that |PF1 − PF2| = constant (= 2a).
  • The “difference” means farther distance minus nearer distance; hence non‑negative.
  • Fixed points F1, F2 are foci; midpoint is the centre O.
  • Line through foci is transverse axis; perpendicular through centre is conjugate axis.
  • Hyperbola intersects transverse axis at vertices; distance between vertices = 2a.

2Diagram

Standard position: centre at origin; transverse axis along x-axis; vertices (±a,0), foci (±c,0), and c²=a²+b².

3Key Concepts

  • 🎯For centre at origin: foci at (±c,0) or (0,±c); vertices at (±a,0) or (0,±a).
  • 📏Parameter relation: b2 = c2 − a2 (so c > a and b2 > 0).
  • 🧭Standard forms: x2a2 − y2b2 = 1 (transverse along x), or y2a2 − x2b2 = 1 (transverse along y).
  • 🧮Eccentricity e = ca > 1 for a non-degenerate hyperbola; foci are at distance ae from centre.
  • ⚖️Equilateral hyperbola: a = b ⇒ equation becomes x2 − y2 = a2 (or swapped).

4STANDARD FORMS (CENTRE AT ORIGIN)

  • Transverse along x: x2a2 − y2b2 = 1; vertices (±a,0), foci (±c,0).
  • Transverse along y: y2a2 − x2b2 = 1; vertices (0,±a), foci (0,±c).
  • Conjugate axis length = 2b; transverse axis length = 2a.
  • No real points between x = −a and x = +a for the x-transverse form; similarly no real points between y = −a and y = +a for the y-transverse form.

5PARAMETERS & ECCENTRICITY QUICK LINK

  • Given a, b: compute c by c2 = a2 + b2 (equivalently b2 = c2 − a2).
  • Eccentricity: e = ca > 1; larger e ⇒ branches more “open”.
  • Equilateral hyperbola: a = b ⇒ c = √2a and e = √2.

7Formulas

Take foci at (−c,0), (c,0) and constant difference 2a (0 < a < c).
√((x+c)2+y2) − √((x−c)2+y2) = 2a
After isolating a radical and squaring twice, the equation simplifies to a standard quadratic form with denominator c2−a2 (which must be positive).
x2a2 − y2c2−a2 = 1
Define b2 = c2 − a2 (>0) to obtain the usual standard form.
x2a2 − y2b2 = 1
a =Semi-transverse axis; vertices at distance a from centre
b =Semi-conjugate axis; conjugate axis length = 2b
c =Distance of each focus from centre; foci at distance c
e =Eccentricity; e = ca (> 1 for a non-degenerate hyperbola)
Core standard results
b2 = c2 − a2
c2 = a2 + b2
e = ca (>1)
x2a2 − y2b2 = 1 or y2a2 − x2b2 = 1

9Solved Examples

1
For the hyperbola x29 − y216 = 1, find a, b, c, foci, vertices and eccentricity.
Solution:
Compare with x2a2 − y2b2 = 1 ⇒ a2=9, b2=16 ⇒ a=3, b=4.
Use c2 = a2 + b2 = 9+16 = 25 ⇒ c = 5.
Vertices: (±a,0) = (±3,0); foci: (±c,0) = (±5,0).
Eccentricity: e = ca = 53.
✓  a=3, b=4, c=5; vertices (±3,0); foci (±5,0); e=5/3.
2
A hyperbola has centre at origin, transverse axis along y-axis, vertices (0,±4) and eccentricity e = 3/2. Find its equation.
Solution:
Transverse along y ⇒ standard form: y2a2 − x2b2 = 1 with vertices (0,±a).
Given vertices (0,±4) ⇒ a = 4 ⇒ a2 = 16.
Given e = ca = 3/2 ⇒ c = ae = 4×3/2 = 6 ⇒ c2 = 36.
Use b2 = c2 − a2 = 36 − 16 = 20.
Substitute: y216 − x220 = 1.
✓  y216 − x220 = 1.

10⭐Important Points

  • Constant difference in definition equals 2a (use a vertex point to verify).
  • For hyperbola, c2 = a2 + b2 (unlike ellipse where c2 = a2 − b2).
  • The axis corresponding to the positive term is the transverse axis.
  • e > 1 for every non-degenerate hyperbola; e → 1 corresponds to a limiting (parabolic) case.
  • Equilateral hyperbola: a = b (so denominators equal in standard form).

11❌Common Mistakes

  • Using |PF1 − PF2| = 2c instead of 2a.
  • Writing b2 = a2 − c2 (wrong sign for hyperbola).
  • Mixing ellipse and hyperbola relations for c2.
  • Choosing transverse axis from larger denominator instead of the positive term.

12💡Memory Technique

“HYPERBOLA: “Plus for c2, Positive term decides axis””
Hyperbola has a difference ⇒ equation has a minus between squared terms.c2 = a2 + b2: think “hyper is extra” ⇒ use plus.Look for the positive fraction term: that variable gives the transverse axis.
