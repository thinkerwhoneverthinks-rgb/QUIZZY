TOPIC1

ANGLE AND ROTATION

1Definition & Introduction

  • An angle is the measure of rotation of a ray about its initial point (vertex).
  • The original ray is the initial side; the ray after rotation is the terminal side.
  • The fixed point about which the ray rotates is the vertex.
  • If rotation is anticlockwise, the angle is positive.
  • If rotation is clockwise, the angle is negative.
  • Angle measure tells how much rotation takes the initial side to the terminal side.

2Diagram

Angle as directed rotation: anticlockwise is positive,
clockwise is negative.

3Key Concepts

  • KeyDirected angle: order matters; initial → terminal fixes sign and angle.
  • KeyOne revolution means a full turn back to the same ray position.
  • KeyPositive angle: terminal side reached by anticlockwise rotation.
  • KeyNegative angle: terminal side reached by clockwise rotation.
  • KeyVertex stays fixed; only the ray rotates about the vertex.

4LANGUAGE OF A DIRECTED ANGLE

  • Specify vertex, then initial side, then the final direction (terminal side).
  • Same two rays can represent different angles if rotations differ (e.g., one extra full turn).
  • The terminal side shows only the final position; it does not show the path taken.

5SIGN CONVENTION (EXAM STANDARD)

  • Anticlockwise rotation (initial → terminal) ⇒ positive angle.
  • Clockwise rotation (initial → terminal) ⇒ negative angle.
  • In diagrams, show direction using a curved arrow to avoid sign errors.

7Solved Q&A

1
A ray OA is rotated about O to reach OB. The rotation is clockwise. What is the sign of the angle AOB?
Answer:
Angle sign depends on the direction of rotation from initial side to terminal side.
Clockwise rotation ⇒ negative directed angle.
✓  The angle AOB is negative.
2
In a diagram, a student draws the same initial and terminal rays but does not show the rotation arrow. Why can the angle be ambiguous?
Answer:
An angle is a directed rotation, not just two rays.
Without a curved arrow, direction (ACW or CW) is unknown.
Also, extra full revolutions can give different angles with the same terminal side.
✓  Because direction and number of revolutions are not specified; the same rays can represent different directed angles.

8⭐Important Points

  • Angle is defined via rotation of a ray, not merely the opening between two rays.
  • Always identify vertex, initial side, and terminal side.
  • Sign convention: ACW positive, CW negative.
  • In answers/diagrams, show direction with a curved arrow to secure the sign.
  • Same terminal side can correspond to many angles if full revolutions differ.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Treating an angle as just two rays and forgetting the direction of rotation.
  • Swapping initial and terminal sides; this changes the directed angle (often flips the sign).
  • Assuming clockwise is positive due to clock-hand intuition.
  • Not fixing the vertex; moving the vertex changes the angle.

DEGREE MEASURE

1Definition & Introduction

  • 1° is the angle equal to 1360 of one complete revolution.
  • One complete revolution around a point is 360°.
  • Degree is subdivided: 1° = 60′ (minutes) and 1′ = 60″ (seconds).
  • Hence 1° = 3600″; and 1′ = 160°, 1″ = 13600°.
  • Positive angles are measured anticlockwise; negative angles clockwise (sign convention).

2Diagram

Quadrantal angles
+90°: anticlockwise direction (positive)
−90°: clockwise direction (negative)
These angles are on a full revolution (reference or zero angle).

3Key Concepts

  • 🔁Revolution idea: full turn = 360°, half turn = 180°, quarter turn = 90°
  • 🧩Sexagesimal subdivision: degree → minute (′) → second (″), each step by 60
  • 🧭Quadrantal degree angles: multiples of 90° (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, 360°)
  • ➕Angles beyond 360° are allowed; add/subtract 360° to get coterminal angles

4MINUTE & SECOND (′, ″): SEXAGESIMAL SUBDIVISION

Degree → Minute → Second
  • 1° = 60′; therefore 1′ = 160°.
  • 1′ = 60″; therefore 1″ = 13600°.
  • DMS to degrees idea: add whole degrees + minutes as a fraction of 60 + seconds as a fraction of 3600.
  • Decimal degrees to DMS idea: multiply the fractional part by 60 to get minutes; multiply the new fractional part by 60 to get seconds.

5TYPICAL DEGREE ANGLES & COTERMINALITY

  • 90° is a quarter turn, 180° is a straight angle, 360° is a full turn.
  • Quadrantal angles in degrees: 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, 360° (multiples of 90°).
  • Angles like 420° and −30° are valid; they represent rotations beyond/against the positive direction.
  • Coterminal angles: adding or subtracting 360° gives the same terminal side.

7Solved Q&A

1
How do you convert 25° 42′ 30″ into decimal degrees (method)?
Answer:
Use the idea: decimal degrees = whole degrees + minutes as a part of 60 + seconds as a part of 3600.
Write it as: 25 + (42 out of 60) + (30 out of 3600), then evaluate if a decimal is needed.
Keep the final value in degrees (°) and round only at the end if required.
✓  Convert minutes to a fraction of a degree by dividing by 60, seconds by dividing by 3600, then add to the degree part.
2
How do you convert a decimal degree like 73.256° into DMS (method)?
Answer:
The whole-number part is degrees.
Multiply the fractional part by 60 to get minutes; the whole-number part is minutes.
Multiply the new fractional part by 60 to get seconds (may be decimal seconds).
If seconds or minutes reach 60, carry to the next unit.
✓  Degrees = integer part; minutes = integer part of (fraction×60); seconds = remaining fraction×60.

8⭐Important Points

  • Definition: 1° = 1360 of a revolution; hence 360° is a full turn.
  • Always keep minutes and seconds less than 60 by carrying/borrowing.
  • Symbols: degree (°), minute (′), second (″) are different units.
  • Quadrantal degree angles are multiples of 90°; they lie on coordinate axes.
  • Coterminal degree angles differ by whole turns of 360° and share the same terminal side.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Treating 1′ as 1 degree (wrong); it is 160°.
  • Confusing decimals with DMS: 40.20° is not the same as 40°20′.
  • Forgetting that 1° = 3600″ (some students write 60″).
  • Stopping after minutes in decimal→DMS conversion without converting the leftover minutes into seconds.

10💡Memory Technique

“60–60 ladder (D → M → S)”
Think: Degree → Minute → Second is a two-step ladder.Each step down multiplies by 60; each step up divides by 60.So 1° = 60′ and then 1° = 3600″ (because 60 minutes each with 60 seconds).

DEGREE–RADIAN CONVERSION

1Definition & Introduction

  • Degree and radian are two units to measure the same angle (rotation).
  • In a full circle: 360° corresponds to 2π radians (unit circle arc length idea).
  • Hence π rad = 180°, the key bridge between the two systems.
  • To convert, multiply by the correct factor: π/180 or 180/π.
  • When writing an angle without ° sign, it is usually interpreted in radians.

2Diagram

Radian comes from arc length: on a unit circle, arc length 1 subtends 1 radian.

3Key Concepts

  • 🔁Core identity: 2π rad = 360° ⇒ π rad = 180°
  • 🧮Radian measure = π180 × (degree measure)
  • 📐Degree measure = 180π × (radian measure)
  • ⚡Common angles: 30°↔π/6, 45°↔π/4, 60°↔π/3, 90°↔π/2

4WHY π RAD = 180° (MINI DERIVATION)

  • A complete revolution is 360° by definition of degree measure.
  • On a unit circle, circumference = 2π, so full revolution = 2π radians.
  • Equate the same rotation: 360° = 2π rad ⇒ 180° = π rad.

5CONVERSION WORKFLOW + COMMON ANGLES

  • Degree → rad: multiply by π180, then simplify.
  • Rad → degree: multiply by 180π; keep exact unless asked.
  • Common angles: 30°=π/6, 45°=π/4, 60°=π/3, 90°=π/2, 180°=π, 360°=2π.
  • Also: 1° = π/180 rad ≈ 0.01746 rad; 1 rad ≈ 57.3°.

9⭐Important Points

  • Always use π rad = 180°; don’t approximate π unless asked.
  • Simplify radian answers to a multiple of π (exact form).
  • Common angles must be instant: 30°, 45°, 60°, 90° ↔ π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2.
  • Angles without ° are assumed radians in most trigonometry/calculus contexts.
  • For negative angles, conversion keeps the negative sign unchanged.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Using wrong factor (multiplying degrees by 180/π instead of π/180).
  • Forgetting to convert minutes/seconds to degrees before converting to radians.
  • Writing π/4 as 45° without mentioning it is radians (unit confusion).
  • Approximating π early, causing avoidable rounding errors in later steps.

TRIG FUNCTIONS ON UNIT CIRCLE

1Definition & Introduction

  • On the unit circle (radius 1), for angle x from +x-axis, point P(a,b) defines cosx=a, sinx=b.
  • Because OP=1, coordinates satisfy a²+b²=1 ⇒ cos²x+sin²x=1.
  • This definition works for any real x (positive/negative, large), using rotation on the circle.
  • Angles differing by 2π land on the same point on the unit circle ⇒ sine/cosine repeat (period 2π).
  • Quadrantal angles are multiples of π2; their coordinates give exact sin, cos values instantly.

2Diagram

Unit circle: if P(a,b) is the terminal point for angle α, then
cos α = a and sin α = b.

3Key Concepts

  • 📍Coordinate meaning: cosx is x-coordinate, sinx is y-coordinate of P.
  • 🔁Periodicity: sin(2nπ+x)=sinx, cos(2nπ+x)=cosx, n∈Z.
  • 🧩Identity from radius: cos²x+sin²x=1 for all real x.
  • ⛔Zeros: sinx=0 at x=nπ; cosx=0 at x=(2n+1)π2.

4QUADRANTAL (AXIS) ANGLES: INSTANT VALUES

  • Quadrantal angles: x=0, π2, π, 3π2, 2π, ... (multiples of π2).
  • Axis points: A(1,0), B(0,1), C(−1,0), D(0,−1).
  • So: cos0=1, sin0=0; cosπ2=0, sinπ2=1.
  • cosπ=−1, sinπ=0; cos3π2=0, sin3π2=−1; cos2π=1, sin2π=0.

5PERIODICITY & ZEROS (MOST-ASKED RESULTS)

  • Same terminal point after full turns: x and 2nπ+x give identical sin, cos values.
  • Hence: sin(2nπ+x)=sinx and cos(2nπ+x)=cosx, n∈Z.
  • sinx=0 ⇔ b=0 ⇔ point on x-axis ⇔ x=nπ.
  • cosx=0 ⇔ a=0 ⇔ point on y-axis ⇔ x=(2n+1)π2.

Solution:
(i) sinx is the y-coordinate on unit circle; sinx=0 means the point lies on the x-axis.
The terminal side hits the x-axis at angles 0, π, 2π, … and also negative multiples.
So sinx=0 ⇒ x=nπ, n∈Z.
(ii) cosx is the x-coordinate; cosx=0 means the point lies on the y-axis.
The terminal side hits the y-axis at π2, 3π2, … and negatives.

✓  (i) x=nπ; (ii) x=(2n+1)π2, where n∈Z

10⭐Important Points

  • Always interpret sinx, cosx as coordinates on unit circle, not only right triangles.
  • Quadrantal angles give exact values from points (±1,0) and (0,±1).
  • Period 2π: reducing angles by multiples of 2π simplifies evaluation fast.
  • sinx=0 at integer multiples of π; cosx=0 at odd multiples of π2.
  • Identity cos²x+sin²x=1 comes directly from radius 1 (Pythagoras/distance).

11❌Common Mistakes

  • Using degrees inside formulas meant for radians (2π periodicity is in radians).
  • Thinking sin repeats after π (it does not); only tan and cot have period π.
  • Forgetting that angles like 10π+π3 reduce to π3, not to 0.
  • Mixing up zeros: writing sinx=0 at (2n+1)π2 (that is for cos).

SIX TRIG FUNCTIONS & FUNDAMENTAL IDENTITIES

1Definition & Introduction

  • tan x = sinxcosx, cot x = cosxsinx (where denominator ≠ 0).
  • sec x = 1cosx, cosec x = 1sinx (reciprocals).
  • tan and cot are ratios of sin and cos; sec and cosec are reciprocals of cos and sin.
  • All identities assume the involved functions are defined (avoid division by zero).
  • Core Pythagorean base: sin2x + cos2x = 1 leads to tan–sec and cot–cosec identities.

2Diagram

Unit circle idea
On the unit circle, a point has coordinates (cos θ, sin θ), so
x = cos θ and y = sin θ.

3Key Concepts

  • 🔁Reciprocal pairs: sec↔cos, cosec↔sin, cot↔tan.
  • ➗Undefined points: tan, sec undefined when cosx=0; cot, cosec undefined when sinx=0.
  • 🧩Pythagorean trio: sin2x+cos2x=1 ⇒ 1+tan2x=sec2x and 1+cot2x=cosec2x.
  • 🧠Fast conversion: replace tan by sin/cos and sec by 1/cos (similarly for cot, cosec).

4DEFINITIONS (RATIOS + RECIPROCALS)

  • tanx = sinxcosx, defined when cosx ≠ 0.
  • cotx = cosxsinx, defined when sinx ≠ 0.
  • secx = 1cosx, cosecx = 1sinx.
  • Reciprocals: tanx·cotx=1, secx·cosx=1, cosecx·sinx=1 (when defined).

5WHY THE IDENTITIES WORK (ONE-LINE DERIVATIONS)

  • From sin2x + cos2x = 1, divide by cos2x to get 1 + tan2x = sec2x.
  • From sin2x + cos2x = 1, divide by sin2x to get 1 + cot2x = cosec2x.
  • Use these to convert between tan↔sec and cot↔cosec quickly in problems.

10⭐Important Points

  • Always check denominator: tan, sec need cosx ≠ 0; cot, cosec need sinx ≠ 0.
  • From tan = sin/cos and cot = cos/sin, sign depends on quadrant signs of sin and cos.
  • Use identities to avoid triangles: tan given ⇒ sec via 1+tan²=sec²; cot given ⇒ cosec via 1+cot²=cosec².
  • sec and cosec are never between −1 and 1; their ranges are y ≤ −1 or y ≥ 1.
  • When taking square root (sec or cosec), choose sign using quadrant information.

11❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing secx = 1/sinx or cosecx = 1/cosx (swapped reciprocals).
  • Forgetting ± while taking square root from sec2x or cosec2x.
  • Using 1−tan2x=sec2x (wrong sign in identity).
  • Cancelling incorrectly in cot = cos/sin: a/bc/b = a/c only if same denominator is nonzero and handled carefully.

SIGNS, DOMAIN, RANGE & PERIODICITY (TRIG FUNCTIONS)

1Definition & Introduction

  • For an angle x, the sign of trig functions depends on the quadrant of its terminal side.
  • Using unit circle point P(cosx, sinx): sign of sin is sign of y, sign of cos is sign of x-coordinate.
  • Even-odd: sin is odd and cos is even; hence tan, cot are odd; sec is even; cosec is odd.
  • Domain: sin, cos are defined for all real x; others are undefined where denominator is 0.
  • Range: sin, cos ∈ [−1, 1]; sec, cosec ∈ (−∞, −1] ∪ [1, ∞); tan, cot ∈ R.
  • Periodicity: sin, cos, sec, cosec have period 2π; tan and cot have period π.

2Diagram

Unit-circle quadrant sign idea (ASTC): decide signs quickly from quadrant.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧭ASTC: Quadrant I all +; II only sin,cosec +; III only tan,cot +; IV only cos,sec +.
  • 🧩Undefined points: tan,sec at x = (2n+1)π/2; cot,cosec at x = nπ.
  • 🔁Repeat rules: sin(x+2π)=sinx, cos(x+2π)=cosx, tan(x+π)=tanx.
  • ⚖️Even-odd check: even ⇒ symmetric about y-axis; odd ⇒ symmetric about origin.

4QUADRANT SIGN TABLE (ALL 6 FUNCTIONS)

  • QI (0, π/2): sin +, cos +, tan +, cosec +, sec +, cot +.
  • QII (π/2, π): sin +, cos −, tan −, cosec +, sec −, cot −.
  • QIII (π, 3π/2): sin −, cos −, tan +, cosec −, sec −, cot +.
  • QIV (3π/2, 2π): sin −, cos +, tan −, cosec −, sec +, cot −.

5EVEN-ODD NATURE (USING sin(−x), cos(−x))

  • sin(−x)=−sin x ⇒ sin is odd ⇒ cosec is odd (reciprocal keeps odd nature for nonzero inputs).
  • cos(−x)=cos x ⇒ cos is even ⇒ sec is even.
  • tan(−x)=sin(−x)/cos(−x)=−tan x ⇒ tan is odd; similarly cot is odd.
  • Exam use: sign/value at negative angles comes directly from even/odd + quadrant.

7Solved Q&A

1
Find domain and fundamental period of f(x) = secx + tanx (no calculation; use properties).
Answer:
secx and tanx are defined only when cosx ≠ 0.
cosx=0 at x=(2n+1)π/2, so these points must be excluded from the domain.
tan has period π but sec has period 2π, so the sum cannot have period π.
Check idea: at x→x+π, tan repeats but sec changes sign, so the whole expression changes.
✓  Domain: R excluding x=(2n+1)π/2 (n∈Z); Fundamental period: 2π.
2
Evaluate sin(−765°) using odd property and periodicity (angle reduction).
Answer:
Odd property: sin(−θ)=−sin θ, so sin(−765°)=−sin(765°).
Reduce 765° by 360°: 765° = 2×360° + 45°, so sin(765°)=sin(45°).
Use standard value: sin 45° = √2/2.
✓  sin(−765°)=−√2/2.

8⭐Important Points

  • Quadrant signs come from (cosx, sinx) on the unit circle.
  • sin, cos are defined for all real x; others fail only where denominator becomes 0.
  • Undefined sets: cosx=0 at (2n+1)π/2; sinx=0 at nπ.
  • Ranges: sec/cosec never lie strictly between −1 and 1; only ≤−1 or ≥1.
  • If any term has fundamental period 2π (like sec), the sum usually needs 2π (not π).

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Mixing undefined points: writing tan undefined at nπ instead of (2n+1)π/2.
  • Assuming period of sec is π (it is 2π; it changes sign after π).
  • Writing range of sec/cosec as all reals; forgetting (−1,1) is excluded.
  • Using ASTC but forgetting reciprocal functions keep the same sign as the base function in a quadrant.

GRAPHS AND BEHAVIOUR (TRIGONOMETRIC FUNCTIONS)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Behaviour means how a trig function increases/decreases and tends to ±∞ on an interval.
  • Use signs in quadrants + monotonic table to sketch one period, then repeat using periodicity.
  • Monotonicity is checked per quadrant, not on the whole real line.
  • For tan, cot, sec, cosec: identify where the denominator becomes 0 → vertical asymptotes.
  • Statements like “tan → ∞” mean values become arbitrarily large as x approaches an asymptote.

2Diagram

One branch of y = tan x on (−π/2, π/2): increasing with vertical asymptotes at x = −π/2 and x = π/2.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧭Quadrant signs (ASTC): I all +, II sin +, III tan +, IV cos +
  • 🔁Periods: sin, cos, sec, cosec → 2π; tan, cot → π
  • 🧱Asymptotes: tan, sec at x=(2n+1)π/2; cot, cosec at x=nπ
  • 📌Range: sin, cos ∈ [−1,1]; tan, cot ∈ ℝ; sec, cosec ∈ (−∞,−1] ∪ [1,∞)
  • 📈Sketch recipe: mark zeros/extrema/asymptotes → choose branch sign → apply periodicity

4MONOTONIC BEHAVIOUR BY QUADRANT (0 TO 2π)

  • I: sin ↑ 0→1; cos ↓ 1→0; tan ↑ 0→∞; cot ↓ ∞→0; sec ↑ 1→∞; cosec ↓ ∞→1
  • II: sin ↓ 1→0; cos ↓ 0→−1; tan ↑ −∞→0; cot ↓ 0→−∞; sec ↑ −∞→−1; cosec ↑ 1→∞
  • III: sin ↓ 0→−1; cos ↑ −1→0; tan ↑ 0→∞; cot ↓ ∞→0; sec ↓ −1→−∞; cosec ↑ −∞→−1
  • IV: sin ↑ −1→0; cos ↑ 0→1; tan ↑ −∞→0; cot ↓ 0→−∞; sec ↓ ∞→1; cosec ↓ −1→−∞

5SKETCHING CHECKLIST (FAST GRAPH DRAWING)

  • Step 1: Use period: sin/cos/sec/cosec repeat every 2π; tan/cot repeat every π.
  • Step 2: Mark key points: zeros, maxima/minima (sin/cos), and vertical asymptotes (tan/cot/sec/cosec).
  • Step 3: Decide sign using quadrant sign table; then connect using monotonic behaviour.
  • Step 4: For sec/cosec: branches lie only above 1 or below −1; never enter the band (−1,1).

7Solved Q&A

1
Why does tan x have vertical asymptotes at x = (2n+1)π/2?
Answer:
tan x is defined as the ratio of sin x to cos x.
A ratio becomes undefined when its denominator becomes 0.
cos x becomes 0 at odd multiples of π/2: x = (2n+1)π/2, n ∈ Z.
Near these points, tan x takes arbitrarily large positive/negative values, so the graph approaches a vertical line.
✓  Because tan x is undefined when cos x=0, its vertical asymptotes are at x=(2n+1)π/2.
2
On 0 ≤ x ≤ 2π, describe how y = sec x looks (asymptotes and which side the branches lie on).
Answer:
sec x is the reciprocal of cos x, so it is undefined when cos x=0.
On 0 ≤ x ≤ 2π, cos x=0 at x=π/2 and x=3π/2, giving two vertical asymptotes.
Where cos x is positive, sec x is positive and lies above 1.
Where cos x is negative, sec x is negative and lies below −1.
It never takes values between −1 and 1.
✓  Asymptotes at x=π/2 and 3π/2; branches are above 1 where cos>0 and below −1 where cos<0, with no part in (−1,1).

8⭐Important Points

  • Monotonic statements are on open intervals inside quadrants; exclude asymptote points.
  • For tan/cot, always specify the correct open intervals split by odd multiples of π/2 or by multiples of π.
  • sec and cosec never take values between −1 and 1; their graphs have a gap around the x-axis band.
  • To sketch reciprocal graphs: zeros of sin/cos become asymptotes of cosec/sec.
  • Use periodicity to extend: replicate one correct period rather than redrawing from scratch.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Drawing tan/cot crossing an asymptote line or placing tan asymptotes at nπ.
  • Assuming sin and cos are monotonic on [0, 2π] (they change direction across quadrants).
  • For sec/cosec, drawing branches inside (−1,1) or touching the x-axis.
  • Mixing up where tan is negative: only in quadrants II and IV (from sin/cos signs).

SUM–DIFFERENCE IDENTITIES

1Definition & Introduction

  • Sum–difference identities express trig functions of (x±y) using functions of x and y.
  • Core pair: cos(x±y) and sin(x±y); tan and cot follow by division (where defined).
  • Angle-shift identities (like π/2−x) convert between sine and cosine quickly.
  • Used to compute exact values (15°, 75°), simplify expressions, and prove identities.
  • When using tan(x±y) written as sin(x±y)/cos(x±y), ensure cos(x±y) ≠ 0. When using the tan-in-terms-of-tan formula, also require cos x ≠ 0 and cos y ≠ 0 (so tan x, tan y exist) and 1 ∓ tan x tan y ≠ 0.

2Diagram

Unit circle idea
On the unit circle
P = (cos x, sin x)
Q = (cos(x+y), sin(x+y))

On the unit circle, combining rotations by x and y gives a point corresponding to angle (x + y).

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩Remember the sign: cos has minus in (x+y), plus in (x−y).
  • 🔁Co-function: sin u = cos(π/2−u) and cos u = sin(π/2−u).
  • ➗tan(x±y) = sin(x±y) ÷ cos(x±y); watch for zero denominators.
  • 🧠Angle-shifts: π/2 swaps sin↔cos; π changes signs using quadrants.

4DERIVATION SNAPSHOT (COACHING-STYLE)

  • On the unit circle, a point at angle x has coordinates (cos x, sin x).
  • A further rotation by y corresponds to multiplying by the rotation matrix for angle y.
  • Equate the x-coordinate and y-coordinate after rotation to obtain cos(x+y) and sin(x+y).
  • Get difference formulas by replacing y → −y and using cos(−y)=cos y, sin(−y)=−sin y.
  • Finally, tan and cot formulas follow from tan = sin/cos and cot = cos/sin (where defined).

5ANGLE-SHIFTS (MOST USED IN JEE/BOARDS)

  • π/2 shift: cos(π/2−x)=sin x, sin(π/2−x)=cos x.
  • π shift: cos(π−x)=−cos x, sin(π−x)=sin x; cos(π+x)=−cos x, sin(π+x)=−sin x.
  • 2π shift: cos(2π−x)=cos x, sin(2π−x)=−sin x.
  • Convert any angle to a reference angle first, then apply quadrant signs.

MULTIPLE & COMPOUND ANGLES

1Definition & Introduction

  • Multiple-angle formula expresses sin, cos, tan of nx in terms of sinx, cosx, tanx.
  • Compound-angle transformations rewrite sums/differences into products, and products into sums.
  • Used to simplify expressions, compute exact values, and convert products to solvable sums.
  • Most identities come from sum–difference formulas by substituting y=x (double-angle) or 2x+x (triple-angle).
  • Domain caution: tan formulas require denominators ≠ 0 (avoid undefined angles).

2Diagram

Core idea: transform form to factor or simplify.
Many identities start with sum–difference identities, then
specialize to 2x, 3x, and product–sum conversions.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩Derive double-angle by putting y=x in sin(x±y), cos(x±y), tan(x±y).
  • 🔁Pick the right form of cos2x: use 1−2sin²x when sinx known; 2cos²x−1 when cosx known.
  • 🧠For sums like cosx+cosy, convert to product to factor/cancel.
  • 🎯For products like 2sinxcosy, convert to sum to combine terms.

4WHEN TO USE WHICH TRANSFORMATION?

  • If expression has 2x or 3x, rewrite using double/triple-angle to reduce powers.
  • If expression has cosx±cosy or sinx±siny, convert to product to factor.
  • If expression has product like sinxcosy, convert to sum to combine like terms.
  • To prove identities, try converting both sides into the same building blocks: sinx, cosx, tanx.

5EXAM TRICKS (JEE/Boards)

  • Replace sin²x or cos²x using cos2x to lower the highest power.
  • Use cosx+cosy factoring to cancel with denominators like 2cos((x+y)/2).
  • For exact values: set up a quadratic using tan2x formula (e.g., tan(π/8)).
  • Always check quadrants/signs when taking square roots from sin² or cos².
