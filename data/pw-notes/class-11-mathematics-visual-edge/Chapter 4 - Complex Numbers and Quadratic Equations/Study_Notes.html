COMPLEX NUMBERS AND QUADRATIC EQUATIONS

TOPIC1

NEED FOR COMPLEX NUMBERS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Complex numbers are introduced by extending ℝ so that equations like x2=−1 become solvable.
  • In ℝ, x2≥0 for every real x; hence x2=−1 has no real solution.
  • Define i such that i2=−1; then x2=−1 has solutions x=±i.
  • A complex number has the form a+ib, where a, b∈ℝ; ℝ becomes a subset of ℂ.
  • For quadratic ax2+bx+c=0, if discriminant D=b2−4ac<0, real roots don’t exist but complex roots do.

2Diagram

Extension ℝ → ℂ allows solutions when a square must be negative (e.g., x²=−1).

3Key Concepts

  • 🚫Real squares are non-negative: x2≥0 ⇒ cannot equal −1 in ℝ.
  • ➕Introduce i with i2=−1 to keep solutions for polynomial equations.
  • 📌Quadratic formula still works; when D<0, √D = i√(−D).
  • 🧾If coefficients are real, non-real roots occur in conjugate pairs: p±iq.

4CORE MOTIVATION: x² + 1 = 0

  • Given x2+1=0 ⇒ x2=−1.
  • In ℝ, x2≥0 for all x∈ℝ ⇒ contradiction ⇒ no real solution.
  • Define i as √(−1) with i2=−1.
  • Then x2=−1 has exactly two solutions in ℂ: x=i and x=−i.

5QUADRATICS WITH NEGATIVE DISCRIMINANT

  • Quadratic: ax2+bx+c=0; discriminant D=b2−4ac.
  • If D<0, then √D is not real, so roots are not in ℝ.
  • In ℂ, write √D=i√(−D) to compute roots.
  • For real coefficients and D<0: x=−b2a ± i√(−D)2a.

10⭐Important Points

  • Reason for ℂ: real squares cannot be negative, but equations demand √(negative).
  • Define i by i2=−1; then √(−1)=i and (−i) also squares to −1.

  • If coefficients are real, non-real roots occur in conjugate pairs.

TOPIC2

DEFINITION OF COMPLEX NUMBERS

1Definition & Introduction

  • A complex number is any number of the form z=a+ib, where a, b are real numbers.
  • The symbol i is defined by i2=−1, so it is a solution of x2+1=0.
  • For z=a+ib, the real part is Rez=a and the imaginary part is Imz=b.
  • Here ib means i×b; b itself is real (it is the coefficient of i).
  • Two complex numbers a+ib and c+id are equal iff a=c and b=d.

2Diagram

Argand plane view: z=a+ib corresponds to point (a, b).

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩Every complex number corresponds to a unique ordered pair of real numbers (a, b).
  • 🔎Rez is the part without i; Imz is the coefficient of i.
  • 🟦Real numbers are complex numbers with Imz=0 (i.e., b=0).
  • 🟪Pure imaginary numbers have Rez=0 (i.e., a=0).
  • ⚖️Complex number equality requires matching both real parts and imaginary coefficients.

4WHY i IS NEEDED

  • x2+1=0 has no real solution since x2≥0 for all real x.
  • Define i so that i2=−1; then solutions are x=i and x=−i.

5STANDARD FORM AND UNIQUENESS

  • Standard form: write every complex number as a+ib with a, b real.
  • Uniqueness: if a+ib=c+id, then necessarily a=c and b=d.

7Solved Q&A

1

Answer:

Use the equality rule: if a+ib=c+id, then real parts are equal and coefficients of i are equal.
So, set 4x equal to 3, and separately set (3x−y) equal to −6.
Solve the resulting two real linear equations simultaneously (no mixing real with imaginary parts).
✓  Equate real parts and equate coefficients of i; then solve the two real equations.
2

Answer:
Match z with a+ib: here a=−7, b=12.
Therefore Rez=−7 and Imz=12.
To test equality with 5 + i(12), both real parts must be equal and both imaginary coefficients must be equal.
Since −7 is not equal to 5, the two complex numbers are not equal even though the imaginary coefficients match.
✓  Rez=−7, Imz=12; not equal because real parts differ.

8⭐Important Points

  • i is defined by i2=−1 to extend the real number system.
  • In z=a+ib, both a and b are real numbers.
  • Imz equals the coefficient b, not the term ib.
  • Equality of complex numbers means matching real parts and matching imaginary coefficients separately.
  • Any real number a can be written as a+i0.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing Imz=ib instead of Imz=b.
  • Comparing a real part on one side with an imaginary coefficient on the other side.
  • Assuming b is an imaginary number; actually b is real.
  • Treating i like a real square root and applying real-number root rules blindly to negatives.

10💡Memory Technique

Rez = part with NO i.Imz = number multiplying i (the coefficient).Equality: match Re with Re, and match Im with Im.

TOPIC3

ADDITION AND SUBTRACTION (COMPLEX NUMBERS)

1Definition & Introduction

  • If z1=a+ib, z2=c+id, then z1+z2=(a+c)+i(b+d).
  • Addition is component-wise in the ordered pair view: (a,b)+(c,d)=(a+c,b+d).
  • Closure: sum of two complex numbers is again a complex number.
  • Subtraction is defined by z1−z2=z1+(−z2) where −(c+id)=−c+i(−d).
  • Additive identity is 0+i0 (denoted 0): z+0=z.
  • Additive inverse of z=a+ib is −z=−a+i(−b) so that z+(−z)=0.

2Diagram

Add: (a,b) + (c,d) = (a + c, b + d)
Subtract: (a,b) − (c,d) = (a − c, b − d)
Argand-plane view: addition/subtraction act like vector
addition/subtraction of (Re, Im) components.

3Key Concepts

  • 1Rule: combine real parts; combine imaginary parts with i kept outside.
  • 2Closure holds for + and −: result stays in the form x+iy.
  • 3Commutative: z1+z2=z2+z1.
  • 4Associative: (z1+z2)+z3=z1+(z2+z3).
  • 5Identity 0 and inverse −z make complex numbers an abelian group under addition.

4WORKING RULES (STANDARD FORM)

  • Write each number as a+ib before operating.
  • Add: (a+ib)+(c+id)=(a+c)+i(b+d).
  • Subtract: (a+ib)−(c+id)=(a−c)+i(b−d).
  • Shortcut: z1−z2=z1+(−z2); change both signs in z2 then add.

5KEY PROPERTIES (EXAM READY)

  • Closure: if z1,z2 are complex, then z1±z2 is complex.
  • Commutative (only for +): z1+z2=z2+z1.
  • Associative (only for +): (z1+z2)+z3=z1+(z2+z3).
  • Identity: 0=0+i0 gives z+0=z.
  • Inverse: for each z, there exists −z such that z+(−z)=0.

9⭐Important Points

  • Always convert to a+ib form; then operate separately on parts.
  • 0=0+i0 acts as additive identity for every complex number.
  • Additive inverse: −(a+ib)=−a+i(−b); it flips both parts.
  • For subtraction, distribute the minus sign across both real and imaginary parts.
  • Addition is commutative and associative; subtraction is neither commutative nor associative.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing z1−z2 as (a−c)+i(b+d) by forgetting the minus on d.
  • Changing sign of only the real part while forming −z instead of changing both parts.
  • Assuming subtraction is commutative: using z1−z2=z2−z1.
  • Mixing up: Im(z) is b, not ib.

POWERS OF i

1Definition & Introduction

  • The imaginary unit i is defined by i2 = −1, so its powers repeat every 4.
  • Positive powers follow: i1=i, i2=−1, i3=−i, i4=1.
  • For any integer k: i4k=1, i4k+1=i, i4k+2=−1, i4k+3=−i.
  • Negative powers use reciprocals: i−n = 1/in (for integer n) and still follow the same mod 4 cycle.
  • To evaluate in for integer n, reduce n modulo 4 (remainder 0,1,2,3) and map to {1, i, −1, −i}.

2Diagram

Cycle of powers
Four-step repeating cycle for all integer powers of i.

3Key Concepts

  • cyclCycle length 4: 1 → i → −1 → −i → 1
  • reciNegative powers: i−1=−i, i−2=−1, i−3=i, i−4=1
  • modCompute by writing n=4k+r, where r∈{0,1,2,3}
  • resuAlways simplify to one of: 1, i, −1, −i

4FAST METHOD (MOD 4 REDUCTION)

  • Write exponent n as n = 4k + r, with r ∈ {0,1,2,3}.
  • Then in = i4k+r = (i4)k · ir = 1 · ir.
  • Remainder map: r=0 → 1, r=1 → i, r=2 → −1, r=3 → −i.

5NEGATIVE POWERS (NO CONFUSION)

  • Use i−n = 1/in (integer n), then simplify using the same cycle.
  • Key values: i−1=−i, i−2=−1, i−3=i, i−4=1.
  • Shortcut: reduce the exponent modulo 4 and use the equivalent remainder in {0,1,2,3}.

  • Only four final answers possible: 1, i, −1, −i.

  • Keep track of sign: i3=−i and i−1=−i.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting: odd powers are imaginary (±i), even powers are real (±1).
  • Wrong reciprocal: 1/i = −i, not i.

SQUARE ROOTS OF NEGATIVE REAL NUMBERS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Imaginary unit i is defined by i2 = −1.
  • The equation x2 = −1 has two solutions: x = ±i.
  • By convention, the symbol √(−1) denotes the principal square root, i.e. √(−1)=i.
  • For a > 0, the principal square root of −a is √(−a) = i√a.
  • The identity √a · √b = √(ab) is reliable for a, b ≥ 0 (reals), but it is not reliable when both radicands are negative reals (with principal values).

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩Equation x2=−1 has two roots ±i; principal √(−1) means i.
  • ✳️For a > 0: √(−a) = i√a (principal), and (√(−a))2=−a.
  • ⚠️With principal values, √a · √b may differ from √(ab) when a<0 and b<0.
  • 🔁Safe simplification: rewrite each negative radicand using i, then multiply.

4CORE RESULTS (EXAM-USEFUL)

  • √(−1) denotes principal root i, but x2=−1 has roots ±i.
  • For a>0: principal √(−a) = i√a; all square roots of −a are ±i√a.
  • Identity: −a = a·(−1) = a·i2 (for a>0).
  • Powers of i: i0=1, i1=i, i2=−1, i3=−i, repeats every 4.

5WHY √a √b = √(ab) FAILS FOR TWO NEGATIVES (PRINCIPAL VALUES)

  • If we (wrongly) force √(−1)√(−1)=√( (−1)(−1) ) then it becomes √(−1)√(−1)=√1=1.
  • But principal √(−1)=i, so √(−1)√(−1)=i·i=i2=−1.
  • Contradiction (1=−1) shows the product rule is not applicable in this setting for two negative real radicands.
  • Safe method: convert √(−a) and √(−b) to i√a and i√b (with a,b>0), then multiply.

  • For a>0: principal √(−a) = i√a, which is purely imaginary.
  • For w≠0, the equation z2=w has two solutions (z and −z); for w=0 it has one solution.

  • To simplify square roots of negatives, first factor out −1 and use √(−1)=i (principal).

  • Saying √(−1) has only one square root as a solution of x2=−1; the equation has two roots ±i.
  • Using √a√b=√(ab) blindly when a<0 and b<0.
  • Forgetting i2=−1 while multiplying, causing sign errors.
  • Writing √(−a) = −√a; incorrect because √(−a) is imaginary for a>0.

ALGEBRAIC IDENTITIES (COMPLEX NUMBERS)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Algebraic identities expand/factor expressions in z1, z2 exactly like real variables.
  • They follow from closure, commutativity, associativity, and distributive law of complex numbers.
  • Key results: squares and cubes of (z1 ± z2) and factorization of z12 − z22.
  • Useful for simplification, comparing real/imag parts, and converting to a + ib form.
  • Valid for all complex numbers; no special restriction on z1, z2.

2Diagram

How identities for complex numbers are proved: expand →
rearrange → simplify using i² = −1 .

3Key Concepts

  • ➕Square: (z1 + z2)2 = z12 + 2z1z2 + z22
  • ➖Square: (z1 − z2)2 = z12 − 2z1z2 + z22
  • 🧊Cube: (z1 ± z2)3 has 4 terms with coefficients 1,3,3,1
  • ✂️Factorization: z12 − z22 = (z1 + z2)(z1 − z2)
  • ⚡During numeric simplification, use i2 = −1 to reduce powers of i

4MICRO-DERIVATION: (z1 + z2)2

  • Start: (z1 + z2)2 = (z1 + z2)(z1 + z2).
  • Distribute: = z12 + z1z2 + z2z1 + z22.
  • Use commutativity: z1z2 = z2z1 ⇒ middle terms add to 2z1z2.

5HOW TO APPLY IN CALCULATION (EXAM SPEED)

  • For squares: compute z12, z22, and 2z1z2 separately; then add signs.
  • For cubes: use (a ± b)3 = a3 ± 3a2b + 3ab2 ∓ b3 with a=z1, b=z2.
  • Always reduce i powers: i2=−1, i3=−i, i4=1.

Solution:
Combine first: (2 + 3i + 1 − 2i) = (3 + i).
Square: (3 + i)2 = 32 + 2·3·i + i2.
Compute: = 9 + 6i − 1 = 8 + 6i.
✓  8 + 6i

(z1 − z2) = (5−2i) − (3+i) = 2 − 3i.
Multiply: (8 − i)(2 − 3i) = 16 − 24i − 2i + 3i2.

✓  13 − 26i

9⭐Important Points

  • All polynomial identities in real variables remain valid for complex numbers (same algebraic laws).
  • In expansions, order can be swapped: z1z2 = z2z1 (commutativity).
  • For (−) cube: signs alternate, but the 3z1z22 term stays positive.
  • Difference of squares is the fastest way to compute A2 − B2 without expanding both squares.
  • After expansion, reduce i2→−1 to reach standard form a + ib.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing (z1 − z2)2 = z12 − z22 (missing middle term).
  • Forgetting i2=−1, keeping terms like +3i2 instead of −3.
  • Wrong sign pattern in (a − b)3, especially the last term and the 3ab2 term.
  • Expanding fully when difference of squares factorization gives quicker multiplication.

11💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: 1-2-1 for square, 1-3-3-1 for cube”
Square: coefficients go 1,2,1 and the middle sign matches ± in (a ± b)2.Cube: coefficients go 1,3,3,1; for (a − b)3 signs go +,−,+,−.Remember: difference of squares is "sum times difference".
