TOPIC1

KOSSEL–LEWIS APPROACH

1Definition & Introduction

  • Kossel (1916) and Lewis (1916) explained valency as atoms attaining noble-gas configuration (octet/duplet).
  • Lewis model: a positive kernel (nucleus + inner electrons) with valence electrons in the outer region.
  • Only valence electrons participate in bonding; inner (core) electrons are shielded and largely inert.
  • Stability is achieved by electron transfer (forming ions → ionic bond) or electron sharing (forming shared pairs → covalent bond).
  • Lewis dot symbols represent valence electrons; dots help predict typical combining capacity (valency) for main-group elements.

2Diagram

Two routes to octet: transfer → ionic; sharing → covalent; only valence electrons are involved.
 

3Key Concepts

  • 1Octet rule: atoms tend to reach 8 valence e− (H/He aim for duplet).
  • 2Kernel: positive core; chemical behaviour mainly decided by outer-shell electrons.
  • 3Ionic bond: complete transfer of e− → oppositely charged ions attract (non-directional).
  • 4Covalent bond: sharing of one or more electron pairs between atoms (directional).
  • 5Lewis symbol: number of dots = number of valence electrons of the free atom.
  • 6p-block valency shortcut (typical): dots ≤ 4 → valency = dots; dots > 4 → valency = 8 − dots.

4HOW VALENCY IS EXPLAINED (LEWIS DOTS)

  • Write the element symbol and place dots for valence electrons (outer-shell electrons).
  • Main-group idea: atoms react to complete octet/duplet, like the nearest noble gas.
  • For p-block (typical): if dots ≤ 4, valency = dots; if dots > 4, valency = 8 − dots.
  • Examples: Na has 1 dot → loses 1 e−; Cl has 7 dots → gains 1 e−.

5BOND FORMATION PATHWAYS (CORE IDEAS)

  • Ionic (electrovalent): metal loses e−, non-metal gains e−; attraction holds ions.
  • Covalent: two atoms contribute one e− each to a shared pair; both count it in their octet.
  • Single/double/triple covalent bonds correspond to sharing 1/2/3 electron pairs.
  • Lewis approach predicts bonding tendency, not 3-D shape or bond energies.

7⭐Important Points

  • Kossel–Lewis is the first electronic theory of valency based on noble-gas inertness.
  • Lewis model uses a positive kernel and focuses on achieving an octet/duplet for many main-group atoms.
  • Bonding involves only valence electrons; inner electrons do not participate directly.
  • Two mechanisms: transfer → ions (ionic bond) and sharing → shared pairs (covalent bond).
  • Lewis dot symbols show valence e− and help infer common (typical) valency for main-group elements.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Assuming inner-shell electrons participate in bonding; Kossel–Lewis uses mainly valence electrons.
  • Confusing valence electrons with valency; valency is combining capacity, not always equal to electron count.
  • Applying octet rule blindly to all elements/compounds without noting exceptions (incomplete/expanded/odd-electron).
  • Drawing Lewis dots equal to atomic number instead of group valence electrons.

TOPIC2

LEWIS SYMBOLS AND OCTET RULE

1Definition & Introduction

  • Lewis symbol is an element’s chemical symbol with dots showing valence electrons.
  • Only valence-shell electrons are shown; core electrons are generally not involved in bonding.
  • Octet rule: atoms tend to gain/lose/share electrons to get 8 valence electrons like the nearest noble gas.
  • Duplet rule: H and He are stable with 2 electrons in the 1s shell.
  • Lewis symbols help predict typical valency and whether bonding is via electron transfer or sharing.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🔵No. of Lewis dots = valence electrons (main-group elements).
  • 🧩Main-group shortcut: valency = dots when dots ≤ 4; valency = 8 − dots when dots > 4.
  • 🏛️Octet seeks the nearest noble gas configuration (e.g., Na → Ne; Cl → Ar).
  • 🤝Covalent bond = shared electron pair; each shared pair counts toward octet of both atoms.
  • ➕Ionic bond idea: one atom loses e− (cation), other gains e− (anion) to complete octet.

4HOW TO WRITE LEWIS SYMBOLS (DOT SYMBOLS)

  • Write the element symbol; add dots equal to valence electrons.
  • For main-group: valence electrons = group number (1–2) or group number − 10 (13–18).
  • Place dots one on each side before pairing; maximum 8 dots for s,p valence shell.
  • Examples: Na•, Mg••, Al•••, C••••, O has 6 dots, Cl has 7 dots, Ne has 8 dots.

5OCTET & DUPLET: WHAT EXAMS ASK MOST

  • Atoms form bonds to resemble the nearest noble gas (stable outer configuration).
  • Metals usually lose e− to reach a noble-gas core; nonmetals usually gain or share e−.
  • In Cl2, each Cl contributes 1 electron to a shared pair → Cl–Cl; both get octet.
  • Hydrogen follows duplet: H shares 1 pair in H2 to reach 2 electrons like He.

7⭐Important Points

  • Lewis symbol shows only valence electrons; these largely control bonding and valency in main-group elements.
  • Octet rule: stability when valence shell has 8 electrons (s,p); duplet for H/He.
  • Quick valency: dots ≤ 4 → valency = dots; dots > 4 → valency = 8 − dots.
  • In covalent bonds, a shared pair contributes to octet of both atoms.
  • Nearest noble-gas idea links typical ions: Na+(Ne), Mg2+(Ne), Cl−(Ar), O2−(Ne).

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Using total electrons instead of valence electrons for Lewis dots.
  • Mixing up valence electrons and valency; forgetting 8 − dots when dots are 5–7.
  • Drawing too many dots on one side first; not placing single dots before pairing.
  • Assuming octet rule works for all elements without exceptions (it has known limitations).

TOPIC3

COVALENT BOND BASICS

1Definition & Introduction

  • A covalent bond is formed when two atoms share electron pair(s) to attain stable valence shells (Lewis idea).
  • Langmuir (1919) coined the term covalent bond for the shared electron-pair concept.
  • Sharing 1 pair of electrons gives a single bond (written as “−”).
  • Sharing 2 pairs gives a double bond (written as “=”).
  • Sharing 3 pairs gives a triple bond (written as “≡”).
  • Covalent bonding commonly occurs between non-metals with comparable electronegativities.

2Key Concepts

  • 👥Each shared pair counts for both atoms’ octet/duplet in Lewis description.
  • ➖Single bond: one shared pair; example Cl–Cl in Cl2.
  • 🟰Double bond: two shared pairs; examples O=C=O in CO2, C=C in C2H4.
  • ≡Triple bond: three shared pairs; examples N≡N in N2, C≡C in C2H2.
  • 🧠Bond symbol counts shared pairs: “−” = 1, “=” = 2, “≡” = 3.

3CLASSIFICATION BY SHARED ELECTRON PAIRS

  • Single bond: 1 shared pair (2 electrons) between two atoms; symbol “−”.
  • Double bond: 2 shared pairs (4 electrons); symbol “=”.
  • Triple bond: 3 shared pairs (6 electrons); symbol “≡”.
  • Multiple bonds are still covalent: sharing increases to satisfy octet requirements.
  • Common examples: Cl–Cl, C=C, O=C=O, N≡N, C≡C.

4STANDARD EXAMPLES (WHAT TO RECOGNISE IN EXAMS)

  • Cl2: each Cl shares 1 electron → one shared pair → Cl–Cl.
  • CO2: carbon completes octet by forming two double bonds → O=C=O (two shared pairs per C–O).
  • Ethene, C2H4: the C–C linkage is C=C (double bond).
  • Nitrogen, N2: each N shares 3 pairs → very strong N≡N (triple bond).
  • Ethyne, C2H2: the C–C linkage is C≡C (triple bond).

6⭐Important Points

  • Langmuir (1919) named the shared electron pair bond as “covalent bond”.
  • Bond type depends on the number of shared pairs: 1 (single), 2 (double), 3 (triple).
  • Write and interpret symbols correctly: “−, =, ≡” correspond to 1, 2, 3 shared pairs.
  • Know standard examples: Cl–Cl, O=C=O, C=C, N≡N, C≡C.
  • Covalent bonds arise mainly between non-metals; octet completion is the Lewis driver.

7❌Common Mistakes

  • Thinking a double bond means “two bonds” but forgetting it specifically means two shared electron pairs.
  • Misreading CO2: writing O–C–O (single bonds) and assuming octets are satisfied.
  • Confusing triple bond symbol “≡” with “=” in quick MCQs.
  • Mixing up examples: writing C≡C for ethene or C=C for ethyne.

8💡Memory Technique

“Symbol = pair count”
Dash count = pair count: one dash “−” → 1 pair; two dashes “=” → 2 pairs; three “≡” → 3 pairs.Hook examples: Cl–Cl (1), C=C (2), N≡N (3).

LEWIS STRUCTURES METHOD

1Definition & Introduction

  • A Lewis structure is a 2-D electron-dot picture showing bond pairs and lone pairs of valence electrons.
  • It helps predict connectivity and approximate bonding (single/double/triple), but not true 3-D geometry.
  • Only valence electrons are counted; inner-shell electrons are not shown.
  • For ions, total electrons are adjusted using the net charge (add for −, subtract for +).
  • Goal: give each atom a stable configuration (octet for most main-group atoms; duplet for H).

2Key Concepts

  • 🔢Total valence e− is conserved: bonding rearranges electrons but does not change the total counted.
  • 🎯Skeleton: central atom is usually the least electronegative atom (never H); often the atom with higher bonding capacity.
  • 🔗Draw single bonds first (each bond = one shared pair); then complete terminal octets.
  • ✨If central octet is incomplete (for typical octet-rule molecules), convert a terminal lone pair into a multiple bond.
  • 🧪Best structure generally has minimal formal charges; negative charge prefers more electronegative atom.

3STEPWISE PROCEDURE (EXAM ALGORITHM)

  • Step 1: Add valence electrons of all atoms; adjust for charge: − adds e−, + subtracts e−.
  • Step 2: Draw skeletal structure: central atom is usually least electronegative (never H) and can form multiple bonds.
  • Step 3: Connect atoms using single bonds (each bond uses 2 e−).
  • Step 4: Complete terminal atom octets first (H needs only a duplet).
  • Step 5: Place remaining electrons as lone pairs on the central atom.

4MULTIPLE-BOND FORMATION: WHEN AND HOW

  • In typical octet-rule molecules, multiple bonds are made when single bonds leave the central atom with < 8 electrons.
  • Convert a lone pair on a terminal atom into a bonding pair with the central atom (forms one π bond).
  • Repeat conversion if needed (triple bond possible) while keeping terminal atom octet satisfied.
  • Check: total electron count unchanged; each π bond reassigns an existing lone pair, not a new electron pair.
  • Prefer structures with minimum formal charges; if charges exist, put − on more electronegative atom.

6⭐Important Points

  • Electron count must match: total dots + bonding electrons must equal calculated valence e−.
  • Complete terminal octets before placing lone pairs on the central atom.
  • Single bonds first; then use lone pairs to create π bonds if central octet is short (octet-rule cases).
  • Hydrogen never exceeds duplet and never acts as central atom.
  • For ions, show structure in [ ] with net charge; electron count must reflect charge adjustment.

7❌Common Mistakes

  • Forgetting to add/subtract electrons for ionic charge (especially polyatomic ions).
  • Putting a highly electronegative atom (O/F/Cl) in the center when a less electronegative option exists.
  • Filling the central atom’s octet before completing terminal octets.
  • Creating multiple bonds without checking total electron count or leaving terminal atom with > 8 electrons.
TOPIC5

FORMAL CHARGE

1Definition & Introduction

  • Formal charge (F.C.) is the notional charge assigned to an atom in a Lewis structure by electron bookkeeping.
  • Used for polyatomic ions and molecules where total charge belongs to the whole species, not one atom.
  • Calculated assuming shared (bonding) electrons are split equally between bonded atoms.
  • Helps compare alternative Lewis/resonance structures and pick the most stable contributor.
  • F.C. is not the same as actual charge separation; it does not measure real polarity.

2Key Concepts

  • 🧮Core idea: F.C. compares an atom’s valence e− count to e− it is assigned in a Lewis structure (lone pairs fully + half of bonding e−).
  • 🔍Prefer structures with lowest magnitude of formal charges (close to 0).
  • ⚡If charges are unavoidable: place negative F.C. on the more electronegative atom.
  • ➕Check: sum of all formal charges = overall charge on the molecule/ion.
  • 🧩Different resonance forms can have different F.C. distributions; hybrid has delocalised charge.

3STABILITY RULES (HOW EXAMS SELECT THE BEST CANONICAL FORM)

  • Lowest-energy contributor usually has minimum number and magnitude of formal charges.
  • Avoid large charge separation: prefer 0, ±1 over ±2 when octets are comparable.
  • If a negative formal charge exists, put it on the more electronegative atom (F > O > N > C).
  • Positive formal charge is better tolerated on less electronegative atoms.
  • Do not break a valid octet/duplet only to reduce formal charges (expanded octet is a separate case).

4OZONE (O3) AS A FORMAL-CHARGE CHECK

  • Each resonance form has one O–O single bond and one O=O double bond.
  • Central O is +1, single-bonded terminal O is −1, double-bonded terminal O is 0 (per canonical form).
  • Resonance makes the negative charge effectively delocalised over the terminal atoms.
  • Formal charge explains electron-bookkeeping in each canonical structure; it does not represent real charge separation.

6⭐Important Points

  • Compute F.C. per atom only after a valid Lewis structure (octets/duplets first).
  • For any structure: ΣF.C. = overall charge (fast verification in exams).
  • Most stable resonance contributors usually have smaller formal charges and less charge separation.
  • Negative F.C. is preferred on more electronegative atoms; positive F.C. on less electronegative atoms.
  • Formal charge is a bookkeeping tool; it does not measure real bond polarity.

7❌Common Mistakes

  • Using total valence electrons of the ion as the valence count for each atom (wrong idea).
  • Counting bonds instead of bonding electrons (single=2 e−, double=4 e−, triple=6 e−).
  • Not counting all lone-pair electrons fully on that atom.
  • Skipping the check that ΣF.C. equals the net charge on the species.
TOPIC6

OCTET RULE LIMITATIONS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Octet rule limitation: some stable molecules/ions cannot be represented with every (non-hydrogen) atom having 8 valence electrons in a simple Lewis picture.
  • Octet rule works best for many second-period covalent compounds (C, N, O, F based).
  • Failures occur when the central atom has < 8 electrons, the molecule has odd total electrons, or the central atom has > 8 electrons.
  • Even when an octet is possible, the Lewis octet idea does not predict shape or relative stability of alternative structures.
  • In ionic solids (e.g., LiCl), simple octet counting does not capture the actual bonding picture in the crystal.

2Diagram

Four high-yield ways the octet rule fails in Lewis structures.
 

3Key Concepts

  • 🟦Incomplete octet: central atom has < 8 electrons in Lewis structure (common for Be, B, Al compounds).
  • 🟪Odd-electron species: total valence electrons odd → at least one unpaired electron; octet cannot hold for all atoms.
  • 🟧Expanded octet: period ≥ 3 central atoms may have > 8 electrons in Lewis structure (PF5, SF6).
  • 🟩Conceptual gaps: octet rule alone cannot predict geometry or compare stability/energy; noble-gas compounds exist.

41) INCOMPLETE OCTET (ELECTRON-DEFICIENT MOLECULES)

  • Occurs when the central atom ends up with < 8 electrons around it in the Lewis structure.
  • BeH2: Be forms two single bonds → only 4 electrons around Be in Lewis picture.
  • BF3: B forms three B–F bonds → only 6 electrons around B; trigonal planar experimentally.
  • LiCl: octet transfer picture (Li+Cl−) does not describe bonding/structure details in the solid; hence octet rule is limited here.
  • Such electron-deficient species often act as Lewis acids (electron-pair acceptors), e.g., BF3 forms BF4−.

52) ODD-ELECTRON SPECIES (FREE RADICALS)

  • If total valence electrons are odd, giving all atoms complete octets is impossible in a Lewis picture.
  • NO: 5 (N) + 6 (O) = 11 valence electrons → one electron remains unpaired.
  • NO2: 5 (N) + 2×6 (O) = 17 valence electrons → odd-electron molecule.
  • Such molecules are typically paramagnetic due to at least one unpaired electron.
  • Lewis structures can be drawn, but at least one atom will show an incomplete octet and/or an unpaired electron.

7⭐Important Points

  • Incomplete octet is common for Be (often 4 electrons) and B (often 6 electrons) as central atoms in Lewis structures.
  • Odd-electron species (NO, NO2) contain at least one unpaired electron; octet cannot be satisfied everywhere.
  • Expanded octet is mainly for period 3 and beyond (PF5, SF6, H2SO4) and noble-gas compounds (XeF2).
  • Octet rule alone cannot predict molecular shape; VSEPR is used for geometry.
  • Octet rule does not compare relative stability/energy of possible Lewis structures; formal charge and resonance ideas help.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Assuming every stable molecule must satisfy the octet on the central atom.
  • Calling BF3 unstable just because it has 6 electrons around B in its Lewis structure.
  • For odd-electron molecules, forcing all electrons into pairs and incorrectly completing octets everywhere.
  • Believing expanded octet is possible for second-period elements (C, N, O, F cannot exceed an octet).

IONIC BOND AND LATTICE

1Definition & Introduction

  • An ionic (electrovalent) bond forms by complete electron transfer and coulombic attraction between the resulting ions.
  • Formation depends on cation formation (ionization enthalpy), anion formation (electron gain enthalpy) and lattice formation.
  • Crystal lattice is a 3-D, repeating arrangement of ions; ionic bond is non-directional.
  • Lattice enthalpy (for dissociation) is the energy to separate 1 mol ionic solid into gaseous ions: MX(s) → M+(g) + X−(g).
  • Although electron transfer steps may be endothermic, lattice enthalpy released on crystallisation often makes overall formation favorable.
  • Ionic bonding contrasts with covalent bonding, which involves electron-pair sharing and directional bonds.

3Key Concepts

  • ⚡Coulombic attraction: stronger for higher ionic charges and smaller interionic distance.
  • 🔥Ionization enthalpy (IE) is always positive; low IE metals form cations easily.
  • ➕Electron gain enthalpy (ΔegH) is often negative for halogens (energy released on gaining e−).
  • 🧱Large lattice enthalpy ⇒ stronger ionic crystal, higher melting point, more stability.
  • 🧪NaCl energetics: IE + ΔegH may be net positive, yet crystal forms due to lattice energy.
  • 🔁Lattice enthalpy (dissociation) is +ve; lattice enthalpy of formation is same magnitude, negative sign.

4FORMATION: ENERGETIC STEPS (NaCl AS MODEL)

  • Step 1: M(g) → M+(g) + e− (absorbs IE).
  • Step 2: X(g) + e− → X−(g) (releases ΔegH if negative).
  • Step 3: M+(g) + X−(g) → MX(s) (releases lattice enthalpy of formation).
  • NCERT data point: for NaCl, IE + ΔegH ≈ +147.1 kJ mol−1, but lattice release ≈ −788 kJ mol−1.
  • Hence overall formation is exothermic: large lattice stabilization drives ionic solid formation.

5LATTICE ENTHALPY: WHAT CONTROLS IT?

  • Definition (dissociation): MX(s) → M+(g) + X−(g); ΔlattH is always +ve.
  • Magnitude increases when ionic charges increase: z+z− larger ⇒ stronger attraction.
  • Magnitude increases when ions are smaller: smaller r ⇒ stronger attraction, higher melting point.
  • More lattice enthalpy usually means harder crystal, lower solubility (unless hydration compensates).

8⭐Important Points

  • Ionic bond formation is favored when lattice enthalpy release outweighs electron transfer cost.
  • Lattice enthalpy (dissociation) is always +ve; for crystallisation use same magnitude with negative sign.
  • Higher ionic charges and smaller ionic radii generally increase lattice enthalpy and melting point.
  • Ionic compounds conduct only when ions are mobile: molten state or aqueous solution.
  • Ionic bond is non-directional; solids form extended lattices, not discrete molecules.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Confusing sign: quoting lattice enthalpy of dissociation (+) while writing formation step without changing sign.
  • Thinking electron transfer alone must be exothermic; ignoring lattice stabilization.
  • Assuming ionic solids conduct electricity in solid state (ions are fixed in lattice).
  • Treating ionic bond as directional like covalent bonds; predicting fixed bond angles in ionic solids.

BOND PARAMETERS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Bond parameters are measurable quantities that describe a chemical bond’s size, direction, and strength.
  • Bond length (R): equilibrium internuclear distance between two bonded atoms.
  • Bond angle (∠A–B–C): angle between two bonds (electron-pair directions) around a central atom.
  • Bond enthalpy (D): energy needed to break 1 mol of a given bond in gaseous state (kJ mol−1).
  • Bond order (B.O.): number of shared electron pairs between two atoms (Lewis); in MO description, it depends on the relative electrons in bonding vs antibonding MOs.
  • Key linkage (trend): Bond order ↑ ⇒ bond length ↓ and bond enthalpy ↑ (shorter bonds are usually stronger).

2Key Concepts

  • 📏Bond length correlates with atomic sizes; for many single bonds, R(A–B) is close to the sum of covalent radii (approximate, not exact).
  • 🧲Bond enthalpy is defined for gas-phase bond dissociation; larger D indicates a stronger bond.
  • 📐Bond angle indicates geometry; H–O–H in water is ≈ 104.5° due to lone-pair repulsions.
  • 🧪For polyatomic molecules, quoted bond enthalpy values are often mean bond enthalpies because successive bond breaks differ.
  • 🔬Measurements: spectroscopy (rotational/vibrational) and X-ray/electron diffraction (structures).
  • 📉Trends for same atoms: bond length single > double > triple, but bond enthalpy triple > double > single.

3HOW BOND LENGTH IS OBTAINED (RADIUS CONCEPTS + METHODS)

  • Covalent radius rc: half the distance between nuclei of two identical covalently bonded atoms.
  • Heteronuclear bond: bond length often correlates with rc(A) + rc(B); deviations occur due to bond type and polarity.
  • van der Waals radius rvdw: half the distance between two like non-bonded atoms in adjacent molecules.
  • Generally: rvdw > rc because non-bonded contact occurs at larger separation.
  • Experimental tools: X-ray diffraction (solids), electron diffraction (gases), spectroscopy (rotational/vibrational).

4BOND ORDER LINKS LENGTH, STRENGTH, AND RESONANCE EFFECT

  • Same atoms: triple bonds are shortest and strongest; then double; then single.
  • Example trend (C–C): 154 pm (single) > 133 pm (double) > 120 pm (triple).
  • Bond order (Lewis): single = 1, double = 2, triple = 3; many isoelectronic species have the same bond order.
  • Resonance can give intermediate bond lengths and fractional bond order; e.g., O3 has equal O–O bond lengths (between single and double).
  • Lone pairs compress bond angles: 109.5° (tetrahedral) → 107° (NH3) → 104.5° (H2O).

6⭐Important Points

  • Mean bond enthalpy is an average; individual successive bond dissociations can differ.
  • Bond angle is measured mainly by spectroscopy and reflects molecular shape; H–O–H ≈ 104.5° is standard.
  • Correlation (general): higher bond order usually means shorter bond length and higher bond enthalpy.

7❌Common Mistakes

  • Treating covalent-radius additivity as exact for all bonds and all bond types.
  • Mixing up rc and rvdw; forgetting that rvdw is for non-bonded atoms.
  • Using bond enthalpy values from liquids/solids; definition requires gas phase bond breaking.
  • Assuming the same bond enthalpy for a bond type in all molecules without considering the molecular environment.

RESONANCE AND POLARITY

1Definition & Introduction

  • Resonance: when one Lewis structure cannot represent the real molecule; actual structure is a resonance hybrid.
  • Canonical forms (contributing structures) differ only in electron positions, not in atomic positions.
  • Hybrid has lower energy than any single canonical form; this extra stability is resonance energy.
  • Bond polarity arises from electronegativity difference, giving partial charges δ+ and δ−.
  • Dipole moment (μ) measures polarity; for polyatomic molecules, μnet is the vector sum of bond dipoles.
  • Fajans' rules: ionic bonds gain covalent character due to polarisation of anion by cation.

2Key Concepts

  • 🧩Resonance hybrid is real; canonical forms are imaginary tools (no oscillation between them).
  • 🔁O3: both O–O bonds equal (128 pm), intermediate between single and double bond lengths.
  • 🔺CO32−: all three C–O bonds equivalent due to delocalisation of π electrons.
  • 🧭Dipole moment is a vector; geometry decides cancellation (CO2, BF3 have μ=0).
  • ⚡Dipole moment increases with larger charge separation and/or larger separation distance (Q and r).
  • 🧲Fajans: small/high-charge cation + large anion ⇒ stronger polarisation ⇒ more covalent character.

3RESONANCE: CANONICAL FORMS vs HYBRID

  • Draw multiple canonical forms only when they have comparable energy and valid octets (as applicable).
  • Only electrons shift (π electrons/lone pairs); nuclei positions remain the same.
  • Hybrid shows delocalisation: bond order becomes fractional and bond lengths become intermediate.
  • O3: two equivalent forms give identical O–O lengths (128 pm), not single 148 pm and double 121 pm.
  • CO32−: three equivalent forms; negative charge delocalised over three oxygens.

4BOND POLARITY & DIPOLE MOMENT (VECTOR IDEA)

  • Heteronuclear bond: electron pair shifts towards more electronegative atom → δ+, δ−.
  • μ increases if charge separation increases and/or the separation distance increases.
  • Molecule dipole: μnet = vector sum of bond dipoles; symmetric shapes often give μ=0.
  • CO2 linear and BF3 trigonal planar: equal dipoles cancel exactly.
  • NH3 vs NF3: lone-pair dipole adds in NH3 but opposes in NF3 → μ(NH3) > μ(NF3).

7⭐Important Points

  • Canonical forms are not real; the molecule does not fluctuate between them (not tautomerism).
  • Resonance hybrid is more stable (lower energy) than any contributing form.
  • Dipole moment is a vector; shape decides whether polar bonds cancel (CO2, BF3).

VSEPR THEORY SHAPES

1Definition & Introduction

  • VSEPR theory predicts shape by minimising repulsions among valence-shell electron domains on the central atom.
  • Proposed by Sidgwick–Powell (1940), refined by Nyholm–Gillespie (1957) with lone-pair effects.
  • Electron domains = bond pairs + lone pairs; a multiple bond counts as one domain for geometry.
  • Repulsion order: lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp because lone pairs occupy more space.
  • Procedure: find steric number → parent electron geometry → place lone pairs to get molecular shape.

2Key Concepts

  • 🧠Steric number (SN) = total electron domains around central atom (bonded + lone).
  • 🧲Lone pairs compress bond angles; greatest compression occurs when more lone pairs are present.
  • 🧭For trigonal bipyramidal parent: lone pairs prefer equatorial positions (fewer 90° interactions).
  • 📐Ideal angles: linear 180°; trigonal planar 120°; tetrahedral 109.5°; TBP 120° (eq–eq), 90° (ax–eq), 180° (ax–ax); octahedral 90°.
  • 🔁Resonance: apply VSEPR to any one canonical structure to estimate geometry.

3PARENT ELECTRON GEOMETRIES (NO LONE PAIR EFFECT SHOWN)

  • SN = 2: linear electron geometry (AB2), 180°; e.g. CO2, BeCl2.
  • SN = 3: trigonal planar (AB3), 120°; e.g. BF3, BCl3.
  • SN = 4: tetrahedral (AB4), 109.5°; e.g. CH4, CCl4.
  • SN = 5: trigonal bipyramidal (AB5), 120° (eq–eq), 90° (ax–eq), 180° (ax–ax); e.g. PCl5.
  • SN = 6: octahedral (AB6), 90°; e.g. SF6.

4LONE-PAIR DISTORTIONS (SAME SN, DIFFERENT MOLECULAR SHAPE)

  • SN 3: AB2E bent; angle < 120°; e.g. SO2.
  • SN 4: AB3E trigonal pyramidal (NH3, 107°); AB2E2 bent (H2O, 104.5°).
  • SN 5: AB4E see-saw (SF4); AB3E2 T-shaped (ClF3); AB2E3 linear (XeF2).
  • SN 6: AB5E square pyramidal (BrF5); AB4E2 square planar (XeF4).
  • In TBP parent, lone pairs go equatorial first → see-saw / T-shape / linear sequence.

  • Using only ABn (bond pairs) instead of steric number (bond pairs + lone pairs) to choose parent geometry.
  • Counting a double bond as two domains; in VSEPR geometry it is one electron domain.
  • Placing a lone pair in an axial position for SN = 5; correct preference is equatorial.
  • Confusing electron geometry (all domains) with molecular shape (atoms only).

VALENCE BOND THEORY (VBT)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Valence Bond Theory explains covalent bonding via overlap of half-filled atomic orbitals and electron-pairing.
  • Proposed by Heitler–London (1927); developed by Pauling using quantum-mechanical wave functions.
  • Bond forms when net attraction (nucleus–electron) exceeds repulsions, lowering potential energy.
  • Greater overlap gives higher electron density between nuclei ⇒ stronger bond and shorter bond length.
  • Predicts directional nature of covalent bonds, but needs hybridisation to match many observed bond angles.

2 Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🤝Heitler–London idea: pairing of two electrons with opposite spins stabilises the molecule.
  • 📉H2 energy minimum at 74 pm; depth corresponds to bond enthalpy 435.8 kJ mol−1.
  • 🧭Covalent bonds are directional because overlap depends on orbital orientation in space.
  • 🟦σ bond: head-on overlap along internuclear axis; maximum overlap ⇒ strongest type.
  • 🟪π bond: sidewise overlap of parallel p orbitals; formed only in addition to a σ bond.
  • 🔄Free rotation: generally allowed in σ-bonded single bonds, restricted when π bond is present.

4ORBITAL OVERLAP & H2 BOND FORMATION

  • As two H atoms approach: new attractions (nucleus–other electron) and repulsions appear.
  • At large distance: energy ≈ that of two isolated H atoms; overlap ≈ zero.
  • Net attraction dominates initially ⇒ potential energy decreases as overlap increases.
  • At 74 pm: energy minimum (most stable); any closer ⇒ sharp rise due to e−–e− and nucleus–nucleus repulsion.
  • Energy difference between separated atoms and minimum equals bond enthalpy: 435.8 kJ mol−1.

5DIRECTIONAL BONDING: SUCCESS + LIMITATION

  • VBT links bond direction to orbital orientation; overlap is maximum along specific directions.
  • Pure p-orbital overlap suggests 90° angles (p orbitals are mutually perpendicular).
  • But observed: CH4 = 109.5°, NH3 = 107°, H2O = 104.5°.
  • So simple VBT (without hybridisation) cannot predict correct bond angles for many molecules.
  • Hybridisation (Pauling) is introduced to generate equivalent, directed orbitals matching shapes.

7⭐Important Points

  • VBT core: covalent bond forms by overlap of half-filled orbitals with opposite-spin electrons.
  • Greater overlap ⇒ stronger bond and shorter bond length (qualitative VBT prediction).
  • H2: equilibrium bond length = 74 pm; bond enthalpy = 435.8 kJ mol−1 (high-yield values).
  • σ bond forms first; π bond(s) form only in addition to σ in double/triple bonds.
  • Double bond = 1σ + 1π; triple bond = 1σ + 2π.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Assuming energy keeps decreasing as atoms get closer; ignoring steep repulsion at short distances.
  • Thinking π bond can exist without σ bond between the same two atoms.
  • Saying σ bonds always rotate freely; steric hindrance can restrict even single bonds.
  • Confusing 'directional covalent bond' with 'directional ionic bond' (ionic is largely non-directional).

HYBRIDISATION CONCEPT

1Definition & Introduction

  • Hybridisation is intermixing of valence atomic orbitals (same atom) to form equivalent hybrid orbitals.
  • Introduced by Pauling to explain directional bonding and correct bond angles not explained by simple s/p orbital overlap.
  • No. of hybrid orbitals formed = no. of atomic orbitals mixed; hybrids are equivalent in energy (degenerate) and similar in shape.
  • Hybrid orbitals orient for minimum electron-pair repulsion (maximum stability); geometry follows the hybrid set.
  • Use steric number (σ bonds + lone pairs on central atom) to predict hybridisation quickly.

2Diagram

Fast exam workflow: count electron domains (steric number) → hybridisation → geometry & approximate angle.
&

3Key Concepts

  • 🎯Steric number 2→sp, 3→sp², 4→sp³, 5→sp³d, 6→sp³d² (count σ bonds + lone pairs).
  • 🧩Multiple bond counts as one electron domain for geometry (VSEPR), but contains 1σ + π bond(s).
  • 📐Ideal angles: sp 180°, sp² 120°, sp³ 109.5°; lone pairs compress: NH₃ 107°, H₂O 104.5°.
  • 🧪Carbon: sp³ (alkanes), sp² (alkenes, planar), sp (alkynes, linear); π bonds use unhybridised p.
  • 🧿Expanded octet (school-level VBT picture): 3rd period onwards can be described using d-orbital participation to write sp³d (TBP) and sp³d² (octahedral) hybrids in many textbook examples.
  • 🔷dsp² hybridisation gives square planar complexes (common for d⁸: Pt(II), Pd(II); also Ni(II) with strong-field ligands like CN⁻).

4CORE TYPES (s & p) + MULTIPLE BONDS OF CARBON

  • sp: 1s+1p → 2 equivalent hybrids; linear; 180°; examples: BeCl₂, C in C₂H₂.
  • sp²: 1s+2p → 3 hybrids in one plane; trigonal planar; 120°; example: BF₃, C in C₂H₄.
  • sp³: 1s+3p → 4 hybrids; tetrahedral; 109.5°; example: CH₄; lp compression: NH₃ 107°, H₂O 104.5°.
  • Ethane (C₂H₆): each C sp³; C–C is σ(sp³–sp³); free rotation about σ bond.
  • Ethene (C₂H₄): each C sp²; C–C = 1σ(sp²–sp²)+1π(p–p); π restricts rotation; molecule planar.

5d-ORBITAL INVOLVEMENT: EXPANDED OCTET & COORDINATION GEOMETRIES

  • For 3rd period and beyond, expanded-octet species are often described (in school-level VBT) using s, p, d hybridisation to accommodate >8 electrons around the central atom.
  • sp³d: trigonal bipyramidal; 3 equatorial (120°) + 2 axial (90° to plane); example: PF₅, PCl₅.
  • In TBP, axial bonds face more 90° interactions → often longer/weaker than equatorial (classic: PCl₅).
  • sp³d²: octahedral; 90°; in ideal octahedral geometry all six positions are equivalent; example: SF₆.
  • dsp²: square planar; 90°; common in d⁸ complexes (Pt(II), Pd(II), and Ni(II) with strong-field ligands like CN⁻); examples: [PtCl₄]²⁻, [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻.

MOLECULAR ORBITAL THEORY (MOT)

1Definition & Introduction

  • MOT: electrons occupy delocalised molecular orbitals (MOs) spread over the whole molecule.
  • MOT (Hund–Mulliken approach): explains properties like paramagnetism of O2 that Lewis/VBT cannot.
  • MOs form by linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) of comparable energy and proper symmetry.
  • From 2 AOs → 2 MOs: one bonding (lower energy) and one antibonding (higher energy).
  • MOs fill by Aufbau, Pauli and Hund rules, similar to atomic electronic configuration.

2Key Concepts

  • ➕Constructive overlap: ψA + ψB → bonding MO; electron density between nuclei increases.
  • ➖Destructive overlap: ψA − ψB → antibonding MO; node forms between nuclei.
  • σσ MO: cylindrical symmetry about bond axis; from s–s, s–pz, pz–pz (head-on).
  • ππ MO: electron density above/below axis; from px/py (sidewise); π and π* are degenerate pairs.
  • 🧲Magnetism: any unpaired electron in MO → paramagnetic; all paired → diamagnetic.
  • 🔢Bond order (MOT): 12(Nb − Na) predicts strength/length/stability.

3LCAO: CONDITIONS & CONSEQUENCES

  • Energy match: AOs must have same/nearly same energy (1s with 1s; 2p with 2p).
  • Symmetry match: same symmetry about molecular axis (pz with pz; not with px/py).
  • Maximum overlap: greater overlap ⇒ larger splitting ⇒ stronger stabilisation of bonding MO.
  • Number rule: number of MOs formed = number of AOs combined (including both bonding and antibonding).
  • Node concept: antibonding MO has a nodal plane/region between nuclei ⇒ reduced density between nuclei.

4MO ENERGY ORDER (PERIOD-2 DIATOMICS) & s–p MIXING

  • For Li2 to N2: σ2s < σ*2s < (π2px=π2py) < σ2pz < (π*2p) < σ*2pz.
  • For O2, F2 (and heavier): σ2s < σ*2s < σ2pz < (π2p) < (π*2p) < σ*2pz.
  • Reason: s–p mixing is significant up to N2, pushing σ2pz above π2p.
  • Filling: apply Hund’s rule inside degenerate π/π* orbitals (maximize unpaired electrons first).
  • Shortcut: Up to N: π before σ(2p); from O: σ(2p) before π.

6⭐Important Points

  • Number of MOs formed equals number of AOs combined; each interacting AO set yields bonding and antibonding MOs.
  • Bonding MO has higher electron density between nuclei; antibonding MO has a node between nuclei.
  • Energy ordering differs: up to N2 (π before σ2p) vs from O2 (σ2p before π).
  • MOT bond order: BO > 0 generally indicates a bond; BO = 0 indicates no net bond (e.g. He2, Be2 in simple MOT).
  • Magnetic nature depends on unpaired electrons in MOs; O2 has two unpaired electrons in π*.

7❌Common Mistakes

  • Using wrong 2p MO order: applying N2-type order to O2 (or vice versa).
  • Forgetting σ2s and σ*2s electrons when counting bonding vs antibonding electrons.
  • Pairing electrons in π* before singly occupying degenerate π* orbitals (violates Hund’s rule).
  • Confusing σ/π (symmetry type) with bonding/antibonding (stabilising vs destabilising).

  • Solubility in water increases for compounds that can H-bond (e.g., lower alcohols).
  • Ice lattice: tetrahedral H-bond network creates open structure → ice floats on water.
