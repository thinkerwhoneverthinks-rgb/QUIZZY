Matter: has mass + occupies space; solids, liquids, gases differ by particle packing and motion

NATURE AND STATES OF MATTER

1Definition & Introduction

  • Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.
  • Matter commonly exists as solid, liquid, or gas (physical states).
  • States differ due to interparticle distance, force of attraction, and particle motion.
  • On heating: solid → liquid → gas; on cooling: gas → liquid → solid.
  • States are interconvertible by changing temperature and/or pressure.

2Diagram

heating
cooling
Particle packing: solid (ordered, close) → liquid (close, mobile) → gas (far, random).

3Key Concepts

  • ⚖️All matter has mass; air is matter and has weight, though its weight is often not noticed.
  • 📦Solids: closely packed, orderly particles → definite shape and definite volume.
  • 💧Liquids: particles close but mobile → definite volume, no definite shape.
  • 💨Gases: particles far apart, rapid random motion → no definite shape or volume.
  • 🌡️Temperature mainly changes kinetic energy; higher T favours liquid/gas states.
  • 🧱Pressure mainly changes spacing; higher pressure favours condensed states (liquid/solid).

4WHY PROPERTIES DIFFER (PARTICLE VIEW)

  • Attractive forces: strongest in solids, intermediate in liquids, weakest in gases.
  • Interparticle space: solid < liquid < gas.
  • Motion: solids vibrate; liquids slide/flow; gases move freely and rapidly.
  • Compressibility: negligible (solid) < low (liquid) << very high (gas).
  • Diffusion: slowest in solids, faster in liquids, fastest in gases.

5INTERCONVERSION BY TEMPERATURE AND PRESSURE

  • Melting (fusion): solid → liquid on heating.
  • Vaporisation/boiling: liquid → gas on heating; lowering pressure lowers the boiling point (boiling occurs when vapour pressure equals external pressure).
  • Condensation: gas → liquid on cooling or increasing pressure.
  • Freezing (solidification): liquid → solid on cooling.
  • General trend: increase T favours gas; increase pressure favours liquid/solid.

7⭐Important Points

  • Matter is identified by mass and occupying space; air qualifies as matter.
  • States differ due to packing and freedom of motion, not due to chemical composition change.
  • Heating increases particle kinetic energy → favours liquid/gas; cooling reverses.
  • Pressure increase brings particles closer → favours gas → liquid and liquid → solid.
  • State changes are physical changes: identity of substance remains the same.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Saying gases have definite volume because they are kept in a container.
  • Confusing interconversion of states with chemical reactions (new substance formation).
  • Assuming particles in liquids are far apart like gases (they are still close).
  • Thinking solids have zero particle motion; they vibrate about mean positions.

CLASSIFICATION OF MATTER

1Definition & Introduction

  • Matter (bulk) is classified into mixtures and pure substances based on composition.
  • Mixture: combination of ≥2 pure substances in a variable ratio; components retain identity.
  • Pure substance: made of one chemical species throughout; fixed composition and definite properties.
  • Mixtures are of two types: homogeneous (uniform) and heterogeneous (non-uniform).
  • Pure substances are of two types: elements (one kind of atom) and compounds (fixed ratio of elements).
  • Mixture components separate by physical methods; compound components separate only by chemical methods.

2 Diagram

Decision tree: composition (variable/fixed) and type (homogeneous/heterogeneous; element/compound).

3Key Concepts

  • 🎯Composition test: fixed → pure substance; variable → mixture.
  • 🧊Homogeneous mixture is single-phase and uniform at the macroscopic level (e.g. air, sugar solution).
  • 🪨Heterogeneous mixture has visibly different parts/phases; composition is non-uniform (e.g. salt + sand).
  • 🧬Element: cannot be broken into simpler substances by chemical means.
  • 🔗Compound: elements combined chemically in a definite ratio; properties differ from constituent elements.
  • 🧰Separation clue: physical method works → mixture; chemical change needed → compound.

4HOW TO IDENTIFY (EXAM QUICK TESTS)

  • If sample shows single phase and uniform properties → homogeneous mixture or pure substance.
  • Check composition: if it can vary without changing identity (e.g. salt in water) → mixture.
  • Check melting/boiling behavior: pure substances show sharp mp/bp; mixtures usually show a range.
  • If components can be separated without forming new substances → mixture.
  • If separation needs bond breaking/forming (a chemical reaction) → compound.

5SEPARATION METHODS (MATCH METHOD TO PROPERTY)

  • Handpicking: visible size/appearance difference (stones from grains).
  • Filtration: insoluble solid from liquid (sand + water).
  • Evaporation / crystallisation: recover dissolved solid (salt/sugar from solution).
  • Distillation: miscible liquids or solvent from solution using bp difference (water from salt solution).
  • Fractional distillation: close bps (components of petroleum; O2/N2 from liquid air).

6MIXTURE vs PURE SUBSTANCE (MOST ASKED)

7⭐Important Points

  • Fixed composition is the key signature of a pure substance; mixtures have variable ratio.
  • Homogeneous mixtures are single-phase at macroscopic scale; heterogeneous mixtures are multi-phase.
  • In compounds, elements are in a definite ratio and properties are different from constituent elements.
  • Mixtures separate by physical methods; compounds require chemical methods to decompose.
  • Examples often asked: air (homogeneous mixture), brass (homogeneous alloy), milk (colloid; heterogeneous mixture), NaCl (compound).

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Calling any uniform sample a pure substance (e.g. salt solution).
  • Thinking elements can be separated into simpler substances by physical methods.
  • Writing "compound components can be filtered" (filtration applies to heterogeneous mixtures with an insoluble solid).
  • Mixing up homogeneous/heterogeneous with pure/impure (different criteria).

9💡Memory Technique

TOPIC5

PROPERTIES OF MATTER: PHYSICAL VS CHEMICAL PROPERTIES

1Definition & Introduction

  • Physical properties are observed/measured without changing a substance’s chemical identity.
  • Chemical properties are observed only when a chemical change occurs (a new substance forms).
  • Properties are used to describe, identify, compare, and predict behaviour of substances.
  • Physical changes: change in state/size/shape; composition unchanged (often reversible).
  • Chemical changes: composition changes; indicators include gas/precipitate formation, heat/light, and persistent colour change.
  • A substance has a unique combination of properties; more properties checked ⇒ stronger identification.

2Diagram


e.g. combustibility, reactivity
Key test: can you observe it without changing chemical identity?

3Key Concepts

  • 👀Physical: colour, odour, melting point, boiling point, density; identity unchanged.
  • 🔥Chemical: combustibility, acidity/basicity, reactivity with O2/acids/bases; identity changes.
  • 🧭Use: physical properties help identify/purify; chemical properties help predict reactions and safe handling.
  • 🧩Characteristic vs non-characteristic: melting/boiling point and density are characteristic at fixed conditions (especially pressure).
  • ⚠️Colour is a physical property, but a colour change during a process may be physical or chemical.

4HOW THEY DESCRIBE & PREDICT BEHAVIOUR

  • Identification: match characteristic physical properties (m.p./b.p./density) with known data at the same conditions.
  • Purity check: pure solids have a sharp melting point; pure liquids have a fixed boiling point at a given pressure.
  • Phase prediction: m.p. and b.p. help decide state at a given temperature and pressure (often room temperature, ~1 atm).
  • Separation: physical property differences enable filtration, distillation, crystallisation (no new substance formed).
  • Reactivity prediction: chemical properties decide if a substance burns, corrodes, or reacts with acids/bases.

5CHEMICAL CHANGE: COMMON SIGNS (NOT PROOF ALONE)

  • Gas evolution, precipitate formation, persistent colour change, heat/light produced, smell change.
  • These signs are suggestive; confirm a chemical change by formation of a new substance (new composition and new set of properties).
  • Combustibility: ability to undergo combustion in O2; products differ from reactant substance.

6Physical vs Chemical Properties

7⭐Important Points

  • Physical properties are observed without changing identity; chemical properties require a chemical change.
  • m.p./b.p./density are commonly used characteristic properties for identification at fixed conditions (especially pressure).
  • Chemical properties help predict reactivity, corrosion, combustibility and safe storage.
  • Chemical change produces substances with new properties; usually not easily reversible.
  • A single observation is rarely conclusive; combine multiple properties for stronger identification.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Treating any colour/odour change as definite proof of a chemical reaction.
  • Calling melting/boiling a chemical change just because heating is involved.
  • Confusing a property (e.g. combustibility) with a process (combustion).
  • Assuming melting/boiling points are always constant; ignoring conditions like pressure.

9💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: Physical = Picture, Chemical = Composition”
Physical: you can take a "picture" (observe) and the substance stays the same.Chemical: composition changes; a new substance forms, so properties reset.
TOPIC6

MEASUREMENT AND SI UNITS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Measurement is expressing a physical property as a number × unit by comparison with a standard.
  • SI (Système International d’Unités) was adopted in 1960 by the 11th CGPM under the Metre Convention (1875).
  • SI has 7 base quantities; all other quantities are derived (e.g. volume, density).
  • In India, national measurement standards are maintained by NPL, New Delhi.
  • Chemistry also uses practical units: L, mL, cm3 for volume; g cm−3 for density.

2Diagram

Measurement framework: property → number + unit; SI base units generate derived units and common lab conversions.

3Key Concepts

  • 🌐A good unit system is universal, stable, reproducible, and convenient.
  • 7️⃣SI base quantities: length, mass, time, current, temperature, amount, luminous intensity.
  • 🔟Prefixes represent powers of 10 (p, n, μ, m, k, M, G).
  • ⚖️Mass (kg) is constant; weight (N) depends on g and location.
  • 🧪Density is mass per unit volume; used for identification, purity checks, and float/sink ideas.
  • 🌡️Kelvin (K) is the SI temperature; Celsius and Fahrenheit are commonly converted in exams.

4SI BASE QUANTITIES (NAME → UNIT)

  • Length l → metre (m); Mass m → kilogram (kg); Time t → second (s).
  • Electric current I → ampere (A); Thermodynamic temperature T → kelvin (K).
  • Amount of substance n → mole (mol); Luminous intensity Iv → candela (cd).
  • Examples of derived units: volume in m3; density in kg m−3.

5PREFIXES + KEY LAB MEASUREMENTS

  • Common prefixes: p = 10−12, n = 10−9, μ = 10−6, m = 10−3, k = 103, M = 106, G = 109.
  • Mass is location-independent; weight changes with g (gravity).
  • Volume relations: 1 L = 1 dm3 = 1000 cm3; 1 mL = 1 cm3.
  • Higher density means more mass packed per unit volume (useful for comparing samples).
  • Kelvin scale has no negative values; conversions may use 273.15 for precision.

7⭐Important Points

  • Write every measurement as number + unit.
  • SI is internationally agreed (CGPM); in India standards are maintained by NPL, New Delhi.
  • Use consistent units for derived quantities (e.g. volume m3, density kg m−3 or g cm−3).
  • Remember: 1 L = 1 dm3 = 1000 cm3; and 1 mL = 1 cm3.
  • Kelvin is the SI temperature scale; 0 K is absolute zero and Kelvin values are not negative.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Using mass and weight interchangeably; weight is a force (N), mass is in kg.
  • Writing wrong litre conversion: correct is 1 L = 10−3 m3.

  • Using 273 instead of 273.15 when the question expects higher precision.

TOPIC7

UNCERTAINTY AND DATA HANDLING

1Definition & Introduction

  • Uncertainty is the unavoidable doubt in any measured value due to instrument and observer limits.
  • A reported measurement contains certain digits + one estimated (uncertain) digit.
  • Scientific notation writes very large/small numbers as N × 10n for easy computation.
  • Significant figures communicate how reliable a measurement/calculation is.
  • Dimensional analysis (factor-label method) converts units by multiplying by unit factors equal to 1.
  • Precision = closeness of repeated readings; accuracy = closeness to true/accepted value.

2Diagram

Workflow: measure → express magnitude in scientific notation → apply significant-figure rules → convert units by dimensional analysis.

3Key Concepts

  • 🔢Scientific notation: keep 1 ≤ N < 10; shift decimal, adjust exponent accordingly.
  • 📌Sig-fig idea: last significant digit is uncertain; do not over-report digits.
  • 🧮×/÷: answer has least significant figures among inputs; +/−: answer has least decimal places.
  • 🎯Precision ≠ accuracy: consistent readings can still be far from the true value (systematic error).
  • 🔁Unit factor method: write conversions as fractions so units cancel like algebra.

4SIGNIFICANT FIGURES: COUNTING + ROUNDING

  • Non-zero digits always significant: 285 cm → 3 s.f.
  • Leading zeros not significant: 0.0032 → 2 s.f.
  • Zeros between non-zeros significant: 2.005 → 4 s.f.
  • Trailing zeros significant only if decimal shown: 0.200 g → 3 s.f.; 200 g (no decimal) is ambiguous.
  • Rounding: removed digit > 5 → increase; < 5 → keep; = 5 → round-to-even (odd up, even stays).

5DIMENSIONAL ANALYSIS (FACTOR-LABEL METHOD)

  • Write the given value, then multiply by unit factors (ratios equal to 1).
  • Choose unit factor orientation to cancel unwanted units diagonally.
  • Use known relations: 1 in = 2.54 cm (exact), 1 L = 10−3 m3, 1 m = 102 cm.
  • Carry units through each step; if units don’t cancel properly, the setup is wrong.
  • Round the final number using the sig-fig rule of the measured inputs.

  • Always report a measurement as number + unit; uncertainty is implied in the last digit.
  • Use scientific notation to avoid loss of place value; keep 1 ≤ N < 10.

TOPIC8

LAWS OF CHEMICAL COMBINATION

1Definition & Introduction

  • Laws of chemical combination are experimental rules describing how elements combine by mass or by gas volume.
  • They were established from careful measurements of reactants/products in chemical reactions (mainly 18th–19th century).
  • They support the idea that compounds have fixed composition and reactions follow simple whole-number ratios.
  • They are foundational for balancing equations, stoichiometry, and the atomic–molecular view of matter.
  • Key set: Conservation of mass, definite proportions, multiple proportions, gaseous volumes, Avogadro’s law.

2Diagram

Two families: mass-based laws (closed system) and gas-volume laws (same T, P).

3Key Concepts

  • ⚖️Lavoisier (1789): total mass stays constant in a closed system.
  • 🧪Proust: a compound always has the same mass % of its elements, whatever the source.
  • 🔢Dalton (1803): for two elements forming many compounds, combining masses show small whole-number ratios.
  • 🎈Gay Lussac (1808): reacting gas volumes (same T, P) are in simple whole-number ratios.
  • 👥Avogadro (1811): equal volumes of gases at same T, P contain equal numbers of molecules.

4THE 5 LAWS (STATEMENT + QUICK EXAMPLE)

  • 1) Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier, 1789): mass of reactants = mass of products (closed system).
  • Balancing equations follows because atoms only rearrange; no net creation/destruction of matter.
  • 2) Definite Proportions (Proust): same compound → same mass ratio (or %) of elements always.
  • Example: natural and synthetic cupric carbonate samples showed identical mass % of Cu, C and O.
  • 3) Multiple Proportions (Dalton, 1803): fixed mass of one element combines with other in small whole numbers.

5JEE/NEET EDGE: WHAT EACH LAW DOES (AND DOES NOT) ASSUME

  • Conservation of mass holds for ordinary chemical changes in a closed setup (no loss of gases).
  • Definite proportions distinguishes compound (fixed composition) from mixture (variable composition).
  • Multiple proportions compares different compounds of same two elements; ratios are of masses.
  • Gaseous volumes requires all gas volumes measured at same temperature and pressure.
  • Avogadro requires molecule concept; supports diatomic gases (H2, O2) to match volume data.

7⭐Important Points

  • Write years with scientists: Lavoisier 1789; Dalton 1803; Gay Lussac 1808; Avogadro 1811.
  • Conservation of mass applies to chemical reactions in a closed system (no escape of gases).
  • Definite proportions is a test of purity and distinguishes compounds from mixtures.
  • Multiple proportions compares different compounds of the same two elements; ratios are small whole numbers.
  • Gaseous volume ratios are valid only when all gases are measured at the same T and P.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Applying conservation of mass to an open beaker reaction where gas escapes, then claiming the law fails.
  • Confusing definite proportions (fixed composition of one compound) with multiple proportions (two compounds).
  • Using gas volumes at different T or P in Gay Lussac/Avogadro statements without converting conditions.
  • Stating Avogadro’s law as "equal volumes contain equal number of atoms" (it is molecules).

DALTON ATOMIC THEORY (1808)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Dalton’s atomic theory (1808): matter is made of tiny atoms; compounds form by fixed-ratio combination of atoms.
  • In chemical reactions, atoms only rearrange; they are neither created nor destroyed (in chemical changes).
  • Atoms of the same element were proposed as identical (same mass, same properties).
  • Atoms of different elements were proposed as different (different masses, properties).
  • Theory strongly supported the laws of chemical combination but had difficulty with gaseous volume observations that were later explained using molecules (Avogadro).

2Diagram

Core picture: atoms (A, B) form compounds in fixed ratios; in chemical reactions, atoms are regrouped.

3Key Concepts

  • 📘John Dalton published A New System of Chemical Philosophy (1808).
  • 🧱Atoms were assumed indivisible and indestructible in ordinary chemical changes (as per the model then).
  • 🧩Compounds have fixed composition: atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios.
  • 🔁Chemical reactions are reorganisation of atoms (basis of balancing equations).
  • ⚖️Explains: conservation of mass, definite proportions, multiple proportions.
  • 🎈Limitation for Gay Lussac volumes: Dalton did not accept same-element atoms combining, so he rejected diatomic molecules like H2, O2.

4DALTON’S POSTULATES (EXAM-READY)

  • All matter is made of atoms, extremely small and indivisible (as assumed then).
  • Atoms of an element are identical in mass and properties.
  • Atoms of different elements differ in mass and properties.
  • Compounds form when atoms combine in fixed, simple whole-number ratios.
  • Chemical reactions involve rearrangement of atoms; no creation or destruction of atoms in chemical changes.

5WHAT IT EXPLAINED WELL (LINK WITH LAWS OF COMBINATION)

  • Conservation of mass: same atoms before and after reaction, only regrouped.
  • Definite proportions: a compound has a fixed set (ratio) of atoms, so fixed composition.
  • Multiple proportions: different compounds form using different simple atom ratios (e.g. 1:1 vs 1:2).
  • Foundation for writing formulae using whole numbers and for balancing chemical equations.
  • Clear distinction between elements (one kind of atom) and compounds (more than one kind of atom).

7⭐Important Points

  • Year and source: 1808, Dalton’s book A New System of Chemical Philosophy.
  • Most tested postulates: indivisible atoms (as assumed), identical atoms of an element, fixed-ratio compounds, rearrangement in reactions.
  • Directly supports: conservation of mass, definite proportions, multiple proportions.
  • Limitation: could not explain Gay Lussac’s gaseous volumes without accepting diatomic molecules/atom–molecule distinction.
  • Modern correction: atoms are divisible (subatomic particles) and elements can have isotopes (non-identical masses).

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing: Dalton explained Gay Lussac’s law (he could not; Avogadro resolved it).
  • Confusing atom with molecule, especially for gases (H2, O2, N2).
  • Assuming Dalton’s postulates are all still valid without modifications (isotopes, subatomic particles).
  • Stating compounds can form in any ratio (Dalton proposed fixed simple ratios).

ATOMIC AND MOLECULAR MASSES

1Definition & Introduction

  • Atomic mass is mass of an atom expressed relative to 12C, taken as exactly 12 u.
  • 1 u (amu) = 112 of the mass of one 12C atom = 1.66056 × 10−24 g.
  • Because atoms are too small to weigh directly, atomic masses are expressed relative to the 12C scale and reported as a numerical value in u.
  • Average atomic mass of an element = abundance-weighted mean of isotopic masses.
  • Molecular mass is sum of atomic masses in one discrete molecule (e.g. H2O).
  • Formula mass is sum of atomic masses in one formula unit for ionic/network solids (e.g. NaCl lattice).

2Diagram

Flow: 12C sets u → isotopes give average atomic mass → compute molecular/formula mass by summation.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧱12C standard fixes the scale: 12C atom = 12 u exactly.
  • 📏Use fractional abundance (not %) in averaging: 20% = 0.20.
  • 🧮Average atomic mass lies between isotopic masses, closer to the more abundant isotope.
  • 🧬Isotopes: same Z, different mass; periodic table shows average value.
  • 🧩Molecular mass: molecules exist; formula mass: ionic lattice has no discrete molecules.

4CARBON-12 SCALE & ATOMIC MASS UNIT (u)

  • 1961 agreement: 12C chosen as standard; its atomic mass defined as exactly 12 u.
  • So, 1 u is fixed as 112 of mass of one 12C atom.
  • Numerical value: 1 u = 1.66056 × 10−24 g (use for atom-to-gram conversions).
  • Atomic masses like 1.0080 u (H) and 15.995 u (16O) are relative to this scale.

5AVERAGE ATOMIC MASS FROM ISOTOPIC ABUNDANCE

  • Convert % abundance to fraction: 99.76% → 0.9976.
  • Use isotopic masses in u (not mass numbers unless explicitly allowed).
  • Average atomic mass = Σ (xi × mi), where Σxi = 1.
  • Check reasonableness: result must lie between smallest and largest isotopic masses.

MOLE CONCEPT AND MOLAR MASS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Mole is the SI unit of amount of substance; 1 mol contains exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 entities.
  • Avogadro constant NA = 6.02214076 × 1023 mol−1 (exact by SI definition).
  • An elementary entity can be an atom, molecule, ion, electron, or a specified group of particles.
  • Molar mass M is mass of 1 mol of entities; unit: g mol−1 (or kg mol−1 in SI).
  • Numerical value of M (in g mol−1) equals atomic/molecular/formula mass (in u).
  • Mole concept bridges microscopic count (entities) and macroscopic mass (grams) for stoichiometry.

2Diagram

Mole is the bridge: entities ↔ mol via NA, and mol ↔ mass via molar mass M.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧮Counting unit: like dozen = 12; mole = 6.02214076 × 1023.
  • 🔁Core conversions: n ↔ N ↔ m using NA and M.
  • 🏷️Always specify entity: “1 mol NaCl” means 1 mol formula units; “1 mol Na+” means ions.
  • ⚗️For compounds: M = sum of atomic masses as per formula (use periodic table values).
  • 📌Amount of substance (n) is an SI base quantity; its SI unit is mol.

4HOW TO COMPUTE MOLAR MASS (M)

  • For an element: M equals its atomic mass in g mol−1 (e.g. O: 16.00 g mol−1).
  • For a molecule: add atomic masses × subscripts (e.g. H2O: 2×1.008 + 16.00 = 18.016 g mol−1).
  • For ionic solids: use formula mass (e.g. NaCl: 23.0 + 35.5 = 58.5 g mol−1).
  • Units check: M is mass per mole; don’t write just “18 g” without “mol−1”.

5FAST CONVERSIONS (STOICHIOMETRY ENTRY POINT)

  • Mass to moles: divide by molar mass (m ÷ M).
  • Moles to mass: multiply by molar mass (n×M).
  • Moles to entities: multiply by NA (n×NA).
  • Entities to moles: divide by NA (N ÷ NA).

COMPOSITION AND STOICHIOMETRY

1Definition & Introduction

  • Stoichiometry is quantitative relation of reactants/products using a balanced chemical equation.
  • Percentage composition gives mass % of each element in a compound.
  • Empirical formula is simplest whole-number atom ratio; molecular formula is actual atom count.
  • Stoichiometric coefficients in a balanced equation represent mole ratios, not mass ratios.
  • Limiting reagent is consumed first and fixes maximum product formed.
  • Solution concentration (M, m, mass %) enables stoichiometry in solutions; dilution uses M1V1=M2V2.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧮Mass %: use molar mass breakdown; good for purity/identity checks.
  • 🧩Empirical-to-molecular: multiply empirical subscripts by n.
  • ⚖️Balancing: change coefficients only; never change subscripts inside a formula.
  • 🚧Limiting reagent: compare n(reactant)coefficient for each reactant; smallest limits.
  • 🧪Molarity depends on temperature (volume); molality does not (mass-based).

4PERCENTAGE COMPOSITION (MASS %)

  • Mass % of element = mass of element in 1 mol compoundmolar mass of compound × 100.
  • If formula known: mass of element in 1 mol = (subscript) × (atomic mass).
  • Sum of all element mass % in a pure compound = 100% (small rounding errors possible).
  • Used to check definite proportions and compare natural vs synthetic samples.

5FORMULA DETERMINATION + STOICHIOMETRY IN SOLUTION

  • Empirical formula: assume 100 g, convert % → g → moles, divide by smallest, make whole numbers.
  • Molecular formula: n = molar massempirical formula mass; multiply all subscripts by n.
  • Balanced equation coefficients give mole ratios; use moles = massmolar mass.
  • Limiting reagent: compute possible product from each reactant; smallest product decides.
  • Molarity M=moles solutevolume of solution; Molality m=moles solutemass of solvent.
