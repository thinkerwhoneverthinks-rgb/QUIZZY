PHYSICAL EQUILIBRIUM

1Definition & Introduction

  • Physical equilibrium is a state of a reversible physical process where forward and reverse rates are equal.
  • It is dynamic: both processes continue, but there is no net observable change.
  • Possible only in a closed system at a given temperature (and pressure where relevant).
  • At equilibrium, measurable properties (vapour pressure, solubility, mass of phases, colour intensity) stay constant.
  • No new substance is formed; only physical state/distribution changes (solid/liquid/vapour/solution).

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🔁Core condition: rate of forward process = rate of reverse process (net change = 0).
  • 🔒Needs a closed vessel; in open systems, matter escapes so equilibrium may not establish.
  • 🌡️At fixed temperature, equilibrium value becomes constant: vapour pressure, solubility, phase amounts.
  • 🧊Solid–liquid equilibrium defines melting/freezing point at a given pressure (e.g., 273 K at 1 atm for water).
  • 💧Liquid–vapour equilibrium gives equilibrium vapour pressure; boiling when vapour pressure equals external pressure.
  • 🧪Dissolution equilibrium in saturated solution: dissolution rate = crystallisation rate.

4WHERE PHYSICAL EQUILIBRIUM OCCURS (EXAM-FAVOURITES)

  • Solid ⇌ Liquid: melting and freezing occur simultaneously; masses of solid and liquid stay constant.
  • Liquid ⇌ Vapour: evaporation and condensation occur simultaneously; vapour pressure becomes constant.
  • Solid ⇌ Vapour: sublimation and deposition occur simultaneously; vapour (or colour intensity) becomes constant.
  • Solute(s) ⇌ Solute(aq): saturated solution; undissolved solid coexists with solution at fixed solubility.
  • Gas(g) ⇌ Gas(aq): dissolution vs escape of gas; dissolved amount becomes constant at fixed conditions.

5KEY CHARACTERISTICS (HOW TO IDENTIFY EQUILIBRIUM IN QUESTIONS)

  • Equilibrium is indicated by a constant value of one parameter at fixed temperature.
  • Examples of constant parameters: vapour pressure, solubility, amount of each phase, colour intensity.
  • Magnitude of the constant value indicates extent (e.g., higher vapour pressure → more volatile liquid).
  • Equilibrium is not the same as equal amounts; it is about equal rates.

7Solved Q&A

1
A beaker of water is kept in an open room. Will liquid–vapour equilibrium be established? Explain.
Answer:
For liquid–vapour equilibrium, rate of evaporation must equal rate of condensation.
In an open system, water vapour diffuses away; condensation back into the beaker is negligible.
Hence evaporation continues with a net loss of liquid; equilibrium is not established for the beaker–vapour system.
✓  No. In an open vessel, vapour escapes so condensation cannot balance evaporation; equilibrium vapour pressure is not established.
2
Ice and water are present together in a closed insulated flask at 273 K and 1 atm. What will remain constant and why?
Answer:
At 273 K and 1 atm, H2O(s) ⇌ H2O(l) can reach equilibrium.
Molecules melt from ice to water while others freeze from water to ice.
At equilibrium, melting rate = freezing rate, so there is no net change.
Therefore the mass of ice and the mass of water remain constant (though microscopic exchange continues).
✓  Amounts of solid and liquid remain constant because melting and freezing occur simultaneously at equal rates (dynamic equilibrium).

8⭐Important Points

  • Dynamic equilibrium: processes continue; only net change becomes zero.
  • Physical equilibrium requires reversibility and a closed system (for matter-exchange processes).
  • At equilibrium, measurable properties are time-invariant at a given temperature.
  • Solid–liquid equilibrium at fixed pressure defines a fixed melting point (normal melting point at 1 atm).
  • Liquid–vapour equilibrium defines equilibrium vapour pressure; boiling when vapour pressure equals external pressure.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Saying process stops at equilibrium (it does not; only net change stops).
  • Thinking equilibrium means equal amounts of phases/species (it means equal rates).
  • Applying liquid–vapour equilibrium ideas to an open vessel without noting vapour escape.
  • For saturated solutions, forgetting that undissolved solute must be present for dissolution equilibrium.

CHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM (DYNAMIC)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Chemical equilibrium is the state of a reversible reaction where rate of forward reaction = rate of reverse reaction.
  • Equilibrium is dynamic: both forward and reverse reactions continue, but net change becomes zero.
  • At equilibrium, concentrations/partial pressures become constant with time (not necessarily equal).
  • Equilibrium can be reached starting from only reactants, only products, or any mixture in a closed system.
  • Evidence for dynamics: isotope labelling shows continuous interconversion even after equilibrium is established.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🔁Reversible reaction: reactants ⇌ products in a closed system (no escape of gases).
  • ⏱️As reaction proceeds: forward rate decreases, reverse rate increases, until both become equal.
  • 📉At equilibrium: [reactants] and [products] are constant, but molecules keep reacting.
  • 🧪Examples: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 (Haber), H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI.
  • 🧬Isotope evidence (D-labelling): scrambling of D into products proves reactions continue at equilibrium.

4HOW EQUILIBRIUM IS ATTAINED (RATE VIEW)

  • Start with only reactants: forward rate high, reverse rate ~0; products build up, reverse rate rises.
  • Start with only products: reverse rate high; reactants form, forward rate rises until both rates match.
  • From any mixture: system adjusts until rf = rr; then composition stays constant.
  • Equilibrium does not mean completion; it means no net progress.

5ISOTOPE EVIDENCE (HABER PROCESS, DEUTERIUM LABELLING)

  • Consider: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 in a closed vessel at constant T.
  • At apparent equilibrium, inject D2 (deuterium) and allow time; ammonia becomes partially deuterated.
  • Formation of NH2D / NHD2 / ND3 shows continued breaking/forming of N–H bonds.
  • Therefore equilibrium is dynamic: reactions continue both ways even when concentrations stay constant.

7Solved Q&A

1
In H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), a student says: "At equilibrium, [H2] = [I2] = [HI]." Is this necessarily true? Explain.
Answer:
Equilibrium condition is rate forward = rate reverse, not "all concentrations equal".
Equilibrium concentrations depend on initial composition and the equilibrium constant at that temperature.
Only requirement: concentrations become constant with time; they can be unequal in general.
✓  Not necessarily true; equilibrium means constant concentrations with time, not equal concentrations.
2
A sealed vessel contains N2 + H2 ⇌ NH3 at equilibrium. If a small amount of D2 is injected and later ND-containing ammonia is detected, what does this prove about equilibrium? (No calculations.)
Answer:
D atoms can enter ammonia only if N–H bonds are continually broken and formed.
This requires both forward and reverse reactions to keep occurring after equilibrium is established.
Therefore equilibrium is dynamic, with no net change though reactions continue.
✓  Equilibrium is dynamic: forward and reverse reactions continue even when macroscopic properties remain constant.

8⭐Important Points

  • Equilibrium requires a closed system and fixed temperature (for a given equilibrium state).
  • At equilibrium: rf = rr and measurable properties become time-invariant.
  • Equilibrium can be attained from reactants side, products side, or any mixture; final state is the same at same T.
  • Concentrations at equilibrium are constant, not necessarily equal or maximum product yield.
  • Isotope labelling (D2) provides strong evidence that equilibrium is not static.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Saying "reaction stops" at equilibrium instead of "net change stops".
  • Assuming equilibrium implies equal concentrations of all species.
  • Forgetting that equilibrium in gases/solutions is established only in a closed container (open system cannot).
  • Confusing speed of reaching equilibrium with extent of reaction at equilibrium.

10💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: "EQUAL RATES, NOT EQUAL AMOUNTS"”
Equilibrium check: rates become equal. Amounts/concentrations just become constant. Isotope mixing later? Then it must be dynamic.

EQUILIBRIUM CONSTANT Kc

1Definition & Introduction

  • Equilibrium constant (Kc) is the constant ratio of equilibrium concentrations per law of mass action.
  • For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD: Kc = [C]c[D]d ÷ [A]a[B]b (only at equilibrium).
  • Concentration was historically called active mass; exponents are stoichiometric coefficients.
  • For a fixed balanced equation, Kc depends only on temperature, not on initial concentrations.
  • Units of Kc depend on Δn = (moles of products) − (moles of reactants) in the written equation.

2Diagram


3Key Concepts

  • 👨‍🔬Guldberg & Waage (1864): law of mass action relates equilibrium concentrations by a constant.
  • 🧮Kc uses equilibrium molarities; each species is powered by its stoichiometric coefficient.
  • 🔁Reverse reaction: Kc(reverse) = 1/Kc(forward).
  • ✖️Multiply equation by n: new Kc = (old Kc)n; divide by n: take nth root.
  • 🌡️Only temperature change alters numerical value of Kc; concentration/pressure changes do not.
  • 📏Units: Kc has units (mol L−1)Δn = molΔn L−Δn (may be unitless if Δn = 0).

4HOW TO WRITE Kc (EXAM ALGORITHM)

  • Step 1: Write the balanced chemical equation with physical states.
  • Step 2: Put products in numerator, reactants in denominator; use equilibrium molarities [ ].
  • Step 3: Raise each [species] to its stoichiometric coefficient.
  • Step 4: Omit pure solids and pure liquids (their active mass is effectively constant).
  • Step 5: Mention temperature with Kc: e.g., Kc(298 K).

5UNITS & Δn (WHY Kc MAY HAVE UNITS)

  • Let Δn = (sum of product coefficients) − (sum of reactant coefficients) for the written equation.
  • Since each [ ] has unit mol L−1, Kc unit = (mol L−1)Δn.
  • If Δn = 0, Kc becomes dimensionless (typical for 1+1 ⇌ 2 type equations).
  • Changing how you write the equation (reverse/scale) changes Δn and hence Kc units consistently.

For H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), at equilibrium: [H2] = 0.20 M, [I2] = 0.10 M, [HI] = 0.40 M. Calculate Kc and its unit.

2
At a certain temperature, for N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g), Kc = 4.0 × 10−3. Find (i) Kc for 2NH3(g) ⇌ N2(g) + 3H2(g), (ii) Kc for 1/2 N2(g) + 3/2 H2(g) ⇌ NH3(g).
Solution:
(i) Reverse reaction ⇒ Kc(reverse) = 1/Kc = 1 ÷ (4.0 × 10−3) = 250
(ii) Halve the equation ⇒ raise Kc to power 1/2: Kc = (4.0 × 10−3)1/2 = √(4.0) × √(10−3)

So Kc ≈ 2.0 × 0.0316 = 0.0632 = 6.32 × 10−2
✓  (i) 250 (ii) 6.32 × 10−2

10⭐Important Points

  • Always use equilibrium concentrations in Kc; not initial or at time t.
  • Exponents in Kc come from balanced coefficients; balancing changes Kc definition.
  • Kc is constant only at fixed T; changing T changes Kc (heating/cooling effect).
  • Reverse and scaling rules are the fastest way to relate constants without re-solving equilibrium.
  • Units are determined by Δn; if Δn = 0, Kc is dimensionless.

11❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing Kc using coefficients from an unbalanced equation.
  • Forgetting to invert Kc when the reaction is reversed.
  • Multiplying coefficients by n but mistakenly multiplying Kc by n (should be power n).
  • Assigning fixed units to Kc; units must follow Δn for the written equation.

KP–KC RELATION

1Definition & Introduction

  • Kp is the equilibrium constant written using partial pressures of gaseous species at equilibrium (with a fixed standard state; in problems, use consistent pressure units throughout).
  • For a gaseous reaction aA(g)+bB(g) ⇌ cC(g)+dD(g): Kp = (pC)c(pD)d ÷ (pA)a(pB)b.
  • Kc uses equilibrium molar concentrations c (mol L−1) in the same stoichiometric form.
  • Using ideal gas behaviour, p = cRT, so each concentration term can be replaced by p/(RT).
  • Result (for gaseous species): Kp = Kc(RT)Δn, where Δn counts only gaseous moles (products − reactants).

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧪Applies to equilibria where gases appear in the equilibrium expression; solids and pure liquids are omitted.
  • 📌Partial pressure of i: pi = yiP (yi = mole fraction).
  • 📈Δn = Σν(g, products) − Σν(g, reactants); ignore pure solids and liquids in Δn.
  • 🌡️Kp and Kc are constant only at fixed T for a given balanced equation.
  • ⚖️If Δn = 0 then Kp = Kc (e.g., H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI).

4WHEN TO USE Kp VS Kc

  • Use Kp when equilibrium data are given as partial pressures (or via total pressure + mole fractions).
  • Use Kc when equilibrium composition is in molarity (mol L−1).
  • For the same reaction at the same T, Kp/Kc = (RT)Δn (gases only; consistent units required).

5COUNTING Δn CORRECTLY (EXAM FAVOURITE)

  • Δn = (sum of stoichiometric coefficients of gaseous products) − (sum for gaseous reactants).
  • Ignore solids and pure liquids in Δn and also in K expressions (their activity is constant).
  • Use the balanced equation as written; if equation is multiplied by n, Δn also multiplies by n.
  • Temperature must be in Kelvin while using (RT)Δn.

Compute Δn = 2 − (1+3) = −2.
Use Kp = Kc(RT)Δn = 0.50 × (RT)−2.

So (RT)−2 = 1/(24.93)2 = 1/621.5049 ≈ 0.001608.

✓  Kp ≈ 8.04 × 10−4

Compute Δn = (1+1) − 1 = 1.

  • Write Kp using partial pressures of each gas species.
  • Use ideal gas link p = cRT under the standard exam assumption of ideal behaviour.
  • Δn counts only gaseous species; omit solids and pure liquids from Δn and from K expressions.
  • If the balanced equation is reversed: new Kp = 1/(old Kp) and Δn changes sign.
  • If the equation is multiplied by n: new Kp = (old Kp)n and new Δn = nΔn.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Counting Δn using solids/liquids; Δn includes gaseous species only.
  • Using Celsius temperature in (RT)Δn instead of Kelvin.
  • Using R in atm units while pressure is taken in bar (unit inconsistency).
  • Swapping sign of Δn (products − reactants is correct).

HETEROGENEOUS EQUILIBRIA

1Definition & Introduction

  • Heterogeneous equilibrium is an equilibrium where reactants/products exist in more than one phase.
  • Equilibrium constants are written using activities; for a pure solid and a pure liquid, activity = 1 (standard state).
  • Hence, pure solids and pure liquids are omitted from Kc/Kp expressions; only gases/aqueous solutes appear.
  • Omission does not mean the solid/liquid is absent; it must be physically present to maintain equilibrium.
  • Units of Kc depend on the net power of concentration terms in its expression; Kp units depend on Δn (gases only) in Kp = Kc(RT)Δn. If the net power is zero, the constant is dimensionless.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧊Activity of a pure solid/pure liquid is 1 (standard state), independent of amount present.
  • 🌫️Only phases with variable composition (gas, aqueous solute) appear in equilibrium expressions.
  • ⚖️Example: CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO2(g) ⇒ Kp = pCO2.
  • 📏For gases: Kp = Kc(RT)Δn, where Δn counts gases only.
  • 🏷️If Δn = 1, typical units: Kc → (mol L−1) ; Kp → bar (if pressure in bar).

4WHY SOLIDS & LIQUIDS ARE OMITTED (ACTIVITY IDEA)

  • General form uses activities: K = Π(aproducts)ν ÷ Π(areactants)ν.
  • For a pure solid or pure liquid, a = 1 (standard state), independent of amount present.
  • So terms like [CaCO3] or [H2O(l)] are constants and merge into K; they are not written explicitly.
  • For gases, a ≈ pp°; for dilute solutions (idea level), ai ≈ [i]1 mol L−1.

5EQUILIBRIUM EXPRESSIONS + UNITS (EXAM-READY)

  • For CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO2(g): Kp = pCO2 and Kc = [CO2].
  • Δn in the Kp–Kc relation counts gaseous species only; solids/liquids ignored.
  • Units: if Δn = +1, Kp has pressure units; corresponding Kc has concentration units.
  • If the balanced equation is multiplied by n, new constant = (K)n (same rule holds in heterogeneous systems).

USING K AND Q

1Definition & Introduction

  • Reaction quotient Q has same form as K, but uses concentrations at any instant (not equilibrium).
  • Comparing Q with K predicts direction of net reaction until Q becomes K.
  • Magnitude of K predicts extent: very large K → products dominate; very small K → reactants dominate.
  • To find equilibrium concentrations, set up an ICE table, express equilibrium terms in x, substitute into K.
  • If a quadratic forms, choose only the chemically possible root (no negative concentrations; cannot exceed available reactants).

2Diagram

Direction test: compare Qc with Kc; system shifts until Q equals K.

3Key Concepts

  • 📈Kc > 103: equilibrium lies far to products; Kc < 10−3: lies far to reactants.
  • 🧭Direction rule: Qc < Kc → forward; Qc > Kc → reverse; Qc = Kc → at equilibrium.
  • 🎯“Q chases K”: the system shifts to make Q move toward K.
  • 🧮ICE method: stoichiometric coefficients control the “Change” row (±ax, ±bx …).
  • ✅Always verify by substituting equilibrium concentrations back into the Kc expression.

4EXTENT FROM MAGNITUDE OF K

  • Kc > 103: products predominate; equilibrium mixture has very little reactant left.
  • Kc < 10−3: reactants predominate; very little product forms at equilibrium.
  • 10−3 < Kc < 103: appreciable amounts of both sides present.

5ICE METHOD (COMPOSITION CALCULATION)

  • Write balanced equation; choose variable x as extent of forward reaction.
  • Fill ICE: Initial (given), Change (± coefficient×x), Equilibrium (Initial ± Change).
  • Substitute equilibrium concentrations into Kc (or Kp) expression; solve for x.
  • Quadratic? reject roots giving negative concentration or consuming more than initial amount.
  • Back-calculate each equilibrium concentration and verify: computed Qc must equal Kc.

GIBBS ENERGY AND K

1Definition & Introduction

  • Gibbs energy change relates driving force to composition: ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q.
  • At equilibrium: ΔG = 0 and Q = K, giving ΔG° = −RT ln K.
  • K is the equilibrium constant (dimensionless when written in activities; often treated so in exams).
  • Sign meaning: ΔG < 0 spontaneous forward; ΔG > 0 spontaneous reverse; ΔG = 0 at equilibrium.
  • ΔG° tells extent: ΔG° < 0 ⇒ K > 1 (products favoured); ΔG° > 0 ⇒ K < 1 (reactants favoured).
  • Temperature affects K because ΔG° is temperature-dependent, and the link ΔG° = −RT ln K contains T.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧭Q vs K decides net direction: Q < K ⇒ net forward; Q > K ⇒ net reverse; Q = K ⇒ equilibrium (Q moves toward K).
  • 🧊Equilibrium condition: ΔG = 0 but ΔG° need not be zero.
  • 📈Magnitude of K indicates extent, not reaction rate.
  • 🔁For reverse reaction: ΔG° changes sign, and K reverses (Krev = 1/K).
  • 🧮Using logs: ΔG° = −2.303 RT log K (base-10).

4WORKING: HOW ΔG, Q, K PREDICT DIRECTION

  • Compute Q with the same form as K using current concentrations/pressures.
  • Evaluate ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q at the given T.
  • If ΔG < 0, net forward lowers G; if ΔG > 0, net reverse lowers G.
  • As net reaction occurs, Q changes until Q = K and ΔG = 0.
  • At equilibrium: ΔG° = −RT ln K fixes K for that T.

5INTERPRETATION: WHAT K SAYS ABOUT EQUILIBRIUM MIXTURE

  • K > 1: products favoured; ΔG° must be negative.
  • K < 1: reactants favoured; ΔG° must be positive.
  • K = 1: ΔG° = 0; comparable amounts (not necessarily equal concentrations).
  • Large |ΔG°| gives very large/small K because ln K scales exponentially.
  • Units caveat: strict thermodynamics uses activities; school problems treat K as dimensionless.

  • Forgetting to convert ΔG° from kJ mol−1 to J mol−1.

  • Assuming ΔG° < 0 means reaction goes to completion; it only means K > 1.

LE CHATELIER PRINCIPLE (EQUILIBRIUM SHIFTS)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Le Chatelier: If a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts to counteract the disturbance and re-establish equilibrium.
  • A shift changes equilibrium composition, not the balanced equation.
  • After a disturbance, system moves until Q becomes equal to K again.
  • Changes in concentration/pressure/volume/inert gas/catalyst do not change K (at same T).
  • A temperature change shifts equilibrium and also changes the numerical value of K.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧪Concentration: add species → shift away; remove species → shift to replace it
  • 🎈Pressure/volume (gases): ↑P(↓V) → shift to fewer moles of gas; reverse for ↓P
  • 🟦Inert gas: constant V → no shift; constant P → acts like expansion (↓partial pressures)
  • 🌡️Temperature: treat heat as a reagent; endothermic forward: ↑T increases K; exothermic forward: ↑T decreases K
  • ⚡Catalyst: speeds both directions equally; no change in equilibrium position or K

4EFFECT OF CONCENTRATION (REACTION QUOTIENT VIEW)

  • For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD: adding reactant A increases the denominator of Q → Q<K → shift right.
  • Adding product increases numerator → Q>K → shift left.
  • Removing reactant decreases denominator → Q>K → shift left (to regenerate reactant).
  • Removing product decreases numerator → Q<K → shift right (to make more product).
  • Only equilibrium concentrations change; K stays same if T unchanged.

5PRESSURE/VOLUME & INERT GAS (GASEOUS EQUILIBRIA)

  • Pressure effect matters only if gaseous moles differ: Δngas ≠ 0.
  • ↑P (↓V) shifts to side with fewer moles of gas; ↓P shifts to more moles.
  • Adding inert gas at constant volume: partial pressures of reacting gases unchanged → no shift.
  • Adding inert gas at constant pressure: volume increases → partial pressures drop → shift to more gas moles.
  • Catalyst: reaches equilibrium faster; does not shift equilibrium; does not change K.

IONIC EQUILIBRIUM BASICS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Ionic equilibrium is equilibrium between ions and unionised species in aqueous solution.
  • Substances conducting electricity in aqueous solution are electrolytes; others are non-electrolytes.
  • In weak electrolytes, ionisation is partial and establishes dynamic equilibrium in water.
  • Strong electrolytes are (nearly) completely ionised; they behave as if ions are the only species.
  • This topic is the basis for later ideas: acids, bases, pH, buffers and solubility equilibria.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🔌Electrical conduction in solution occurs due to mobile ions, not neutral molecules.
  • 🧪Faraday classification: strong vs weak electrolytes based on extent of ionisation.
  • 🔁Dynamic equilibrium: forward ionisation rate = reverse association rate (net change zero).
  • 💧Water stabilises ions via hydration, enabling dissociation of ionic and polar substances.
  • 🧠Weak electrolyte strength is about equilibrium position, not about how fast it ionises.

4ELECTROLYTES, NON-ELECTROLYTES: WHAT EXAMS TEST

  • Electrolyte: produces ions in water; conducts due to ion mobility.
  • Non-electrolyte: dissolves without ion formation; solution does not conduct appreciably.
  • Electrolytes may be ionic (NaCl) or molecular that ionise (HCl, CH3COOH).
  • Conductance: strong electrolyte > weak electrolyte >> non-electrolyte (same conditions).

5STRONG vs WEAK ELECTROLYTES: IONISATION BEHAVIOUR

  • Strong: ~100% ionised; treat as only ions present in solution.
  • Weak: partial ionisation (often < 5%); equilibrium exists between ions and molecules.
  • For weak electrolyte: increasing dilution generally increases degree of ionisation.
  • Ionic equilibrium language: write reversible ionisation for weak, one-way dissociation for strong (at this level).

  • Faraday classification is about extent of ionisation, not about solubility alone.
  • Ionic equilibrium concepts underpin acids/bases, buffers, and solubility (later subtopics).

ACID–BASE CONCEPTS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Acid–base concepts classify acids/bases by what they produce, transfer, or donate/accept.
  • Arrhenius: acid gives H+(aq) in water; base gives OH−(aq) in water (aqueous only).
  • Brønsted–Lowry: acid = proton donor; base = proton acceptor (proton-transfer concept; broader than Arrhenius).
  • Conjugate pair: acid ⇌ conjugate base + H+; differs by exactly one proton.
  • Lewis: acid = electron-pair acceptor; base = electron-pair donor (most general at this level).
  • Water is amphoteric: can act as acid or base depending on the reactant.

2Diagram

Arrhenius ⊂ Brønsted–Lowry ⊂ Lewis; conjugate pairs and amphoteric water connect the ideas.

3Key Concepts

  • 💧In water, H+ does not exist as a free species; it is present as solvated/protonated water, commonly written as H3O+.
  • 🔁In Brønsted–Lowry, every acid has a conjugate base; every base has a conjugate acid.
  • ⚖️Strong acid ⇔ weak conjugate base; strong base ⇔ weak conjugate acid.
  • 🧲Lewis acids often have incomplete octet or vacant orbitals: BF3, AlCl3, metal ions.
  • 🧠NH3 is a base without OH−; explained by Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis, not Arrhenius.

4CORE EXAMPLES (WHAT EXAMS LOVE)

  • Arrhenius acid: HCl(aq) → H+(aq) + Cl−(aq); in water: HCl + H2O → H3O+ + Cl−.
  • Arrhenius base: NaOH(aq) → Na+(aq) + OH−(aq).
  • Brønsted–Lowry: NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4+ + OH− (NH3 base, H2O acid).
  • Lewis: BF3 + :NH3 → BF3:NH3 (coordinate bond by lone-pair donation).

5HOW TO IDENTIFY CONJUGATE PAIRS FAST

  • Write the Brønsted reaction; mark the species that loses H+ (acid) and that gains H+ (base).
  • Pair each reactant with its product after H+ transfer: acid ↔ conjugate base, base ↔ conjugate acid.
  • Conjugate pair differs by exactly one H; charge changes by ±1 accordingly.
  • Water: with acids it behaves as a base (forms H3O+); with bases it behaves as an acid (forms OH−).

  • Conjugate acid–base pair differs by exactly one H+; charges differ by one unit.
  • Water is amphoteric: acts as base with acids, acts as acid with bases.
  • Lewis concept covers non-protonic acids (BF3, AlCl3) and metal ions as acids.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing aqueous dissociation using bare H+ and not showing H3O+ when discussing proton transfer in water.
  • Calling Cl− the conjugate acid of HCl (it is the conjugate base).
  • Assuming a base must contain OH−; missing bases like NH3.
  • Thinking water is only a base; ignoring its acid role with NH3.

pH, Kw, Ka, Kb (IONIC EQUILIBRIUM)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Ionic product of water: Kw = [H3O+][OH−] for 2H2O ⇌ H3O+ + OH−.
  • In aqueous solution, we commonly write [H+] to mean [H3O+] (same concentration for calculations).
  • At 298 K, pure water has [H+] = [OH−] = 1.0 × 10−7 mol L−1, so Kw = 1.0 × 10−14.
  • pH expresses acidity on a log scale: pH = −log[H+], pOH = −log[OH−].
  • At 298 K: pH + pOH = pKw = 14; neutral pH = 7, acidic < 7, basic > 7.
  • Weak acid/base strength measured by Ka, Kb; larger K (smaller pK) ⇒ stronger (within same type: acid vs acid, base vs base).

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 💧Water is amphoteric: one H2O donates H+, another accepts → H3O+ + OH−.
  • 🔁Kw is constant at fixed T; if [H+] increases, [OH−] decreases so that [H+][OH−] stays constant.
  • 📉Log rule: change of 1 pH unit means 10× change in [H+].
  • 🧷For a conjugate acid–base pair: Ka×Kb = Kw; strong acid ⇔ weak conjugate base.
  • 🧩Polyprotic acids ionise stepwise: Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3 (successive H+ removal gets harder).
  • ⬇️Ostwald (weak electrolytes): on dilution, degree of ionisation α increases; for α << 1, α ≈ √(K/c).

4WEAK ACID/BASE: CONSTANTS + OSTWALD (EXAM WORKHORSE)

  • Weak acid: HX + H2O ⇌ H3O+ + X−; Ka = [H+][X−][HX].
  • If initial concentration = c, degree of ionisation = α: [H+] = cα, [X−] = cα, [HX] = c(1−α).
  • So Ka = cα²1−α; for weak acids α << 1 ⇒ Ka ≈ cα² ⇒ α ≈ √(Ka/c) and [H+] = cα ≈ √(Kac).
  • Weak base: B + H2O ⇌ BH+ + OH−; Kb = [BH+][OH−][B].
  • If x = [OH−] produced and x << c, then Kb ≈ x²c ⇒ x ≈ √(Kbc).

5POLYPROTIC ACIDS (STEPWISE Ka)

  • Stepwise dissociation: H2A ⇌ H+ + HA− (Ka1), then HA− ⇌ H+ + A2− (Ka2), etc.
  • Typically: Ka1 > Ka2 > Ka3 because removing H+ becomes harder as negative charge increases.
  • In pH calculations for polyprotic acids, the first dissociation often dominates unless Ka2 is comparable to Ka1.

Calculate pH of 0.020 M HCl at 298 K.
Solution:

2
A 0.10 M weak acid HA has Ka = 1.0 × 10−5 at 298 K. Find (i) [H+], (ii) pH, (iii) % ionisation.
Solution:
Let [H+] = [A−] = x, [HA] = 0.10 − x.
Ka = x²0.10 − x = 1.0 × 10−5.
Assume x << 0.10 ⇒ 0.10 − x ≈ 0.10.
Then x² ≈ (1.0 × 10−5)(0.10) = 1.0 × 10−6 ⇒ x = 1.0 × 10−3 mol L−1.
Check: x/0.10 = 0.01 = 1% (small), so approximation is valid.
pH = −log(1.0 × 10−3) = 3.00.
✓  [H+] = 1.0 × 10−3 mol L−1; pH = 3.00; % ionisation = 1.0%

10⭐Important Points

  • At fixed T, Kw is constant for any aqueous solution (pure water, acids, bases, salts).
  • pH + pOH = pKw; at 298 K use 14, but pKw changes with temperature.
  • Bigger Ka (smaller pKa) ⇒ stronger acid; bigger Kb (smaller pKb) ⇒ stronger base.
  • Conjugate pairs obey KaKb = Kw (so strength is inverse across conjugates).
  • Weak-electrolyte approximations like [H+] ≈ √(Kac) or α ≈ √(K/c) require α << 1; always check % ionisation.

11❌Common Mistakes

  • Using pH + pOH = 14 at temperatures other than 298 K without adjusting pKw.
  • Mixing up pKa and Ka: smaller pKa means larger Ka (stronger acid).
  • Applying weak-acid shortcut (x << c) without checking % ionisation afterward.
  • Forgetting log rule: pH change of 2 units means 100× change in [H+], not 2×.

BUFFERS AND SOLUBILITY

1Definition & Introduction

  • Buffer: solution that resists pH change on adding small acid/base; usually weak acid/base + its salt.
  • Common ion effect: added common ion shifts equilibrium to suppress ionisation/solubility (Le Chatelier).
  • Salt hydrolysis: ions of salts react with water to generate H+/OH−, making pH ≠ 7.
  • Ksp: equilibrium constant for dissolution of sparingly soluble salts in saturated solution.
  • Qsp vs Ksp: compares ionic product with Ksp to predict dissolution/precipitation.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧷Suppression of ionisation: for HA ⇌ H+ + A−, adding A− shifts left, lowers [H+].
  • ⚖️CH3COONa gives basic solution; NH4Cl gives acidic solution due to hydrolysis.
  • 🧾Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH depends on ratio of salt/acid (or BH+/B), not absolute dilution.
  • ☔Buffer best when [salt] ≈ [acid] (or [BH+] ≈ [B]); effective range ≈ pKa ± 1.
  • 🧱For MxXy(s): Ksp = [M]x[X]y; common ion lowers solubility S.
  • 🚦Precipitation criterion: if Qsp > Ksp → precipitate forms; if Qsp < Ksp → dissolves.

4SALT HYDROLYSIS → NATURE & pH

  • Strong acid + strong base salt: no hydrolysis; pH = 7 (e.g., NaCl).
  • Weak acid + strong base salt: anion hydrolyses → OH−; basic (e.g., CH3COONa).
  • Strong acid + weak base salt: cation hydrolyses → H+; acidic (e.g., NH4Cl).
  • Weak acid + weak base salt: pH = 7 + 12(pKa − pKb); independent of salt concentration.

5SOLUBILITY EQUILIBRIA: Ksp, COMMON ION, Qsp TEST

  • Write dissolution: MxXy(s) ⇌ xMp+ + yXq−; omit solid in expression.
  • If molar solubility = S, then [M] = xS, [X] = yS → Ksp = xxyySx+y.
  • Adding common ion increases one ion concentration, so to keep Ksp fixed, S decreases (precipitation favored).
  • Mixing solutions: compute Qsp from initial ion concentrations after dilution; compare with Ksp.

8Solved Examples

1
Calculate pH of an acidic buffer made by mixing 0.20 M CH3COOH and 0.10 M CH3COONa. Given pKa(CH3COOH) = 4.74.
Solution:
Use Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log[salt][acid].
Substitute: pH = 4.74 + log0.100.20 = 4.74 + log(0.5).
log(0.5) = log(1/2) = −log 2 = −0.301.
✓  pH = 4.74 − 0.301 = 4.44 (approx).
2
Will AgCl precipitate when 50.0 mL of 1.0×10−3 M AgNO3 is mixed with 50.0 mL of 2.0×10−3 M NaCl? Given Ksp(AgCl) = 1.8×10−10 at 298 K.
Solution:
After mixing equal volumes, each concentration halves due to dilution.
[Ag+]initial = 1.0×10−3 ÷ 2 = 5.0×10−4 M.
[Cl−]initial = 2.0×10−3 ÷ 2 = 1.0×10−3 M.
Compute ionic product: Qsp = [Ag+][Cl−] = (5.0×10−4)(1.0×10−3) = 5.0×10−7.
Compare: Qsp (5.0×10−7) >> Ksp (1.8×10−10) ⇒ precipitation occurs until Qsp = Ksp.
✓  Yes, AgCl precipitates (since Qsp > Ksp).

9⭐Important Points

  • Common ion effect decreases degree of ionisation of weak acids/bases and decreases solubility of salts.
  • Acidic buffer: weak acid + its salt; basic buffer: weak base + its salt; pH set by concentration ratio.
  • At 298 K: pH + pOH = 14; for basic buffer compute pOH first, then pH.
  • Salt hydrolysis decides pH: CH3COONa basic, NH4Cl acidic; NaCl neutral.
  • For weak acid–weak base salt: pH depends on pKa and pKb, not on salt concentration.

10❌Common Mistakes

  • Using Henderson–Hasselbalch with moles but forgetting volume change on mixing (ratio may change).
  • For basic buffer, writing pH = pKb + log([salt]/[base]) instead of pOH form, then converting.
  • Including solid concentration in Ksp expression or writing Ksp = [ions] without powers.
  • Doing precipitation test with initial concentrations before mixing/dilution.
