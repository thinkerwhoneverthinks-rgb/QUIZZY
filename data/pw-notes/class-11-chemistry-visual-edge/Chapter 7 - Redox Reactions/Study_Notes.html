UNIT 7 — REDOX REACTIONS

TOPIC1

CLASSICAL REDOX CONCEPT

1Definition & Introduction

  • Classical redox: oxidation = addition of O / electronegative element or removal of H / electropositive element; reduction is the reverse.
  • Oxidation (classical) makes a substance richer in an electronegative element (O, F, Cl, etc.) or poorer in an electropositive element (H, metal, etc.).
  • Reduction (classical) removes an electronegative element (O, halogen, etc.) or adds an electropositive element (H, metal, etc.).
  • Oxidation and reduction always occur together in the same overall reaction (hence redox).
  • This concept helps spot redox quickly in combustion, corrosion, and ore-to-metal type changes.

2Diagram

Classical definitions: oxidation/reduction are opposite transfers, but occur as a coupled pair.

3Key Concepts

  • 🟦Oxidation (classical): +O / +EN element or −H / −EP element.
  • 🟩Reduction (classical): −O / −EN element or +H / +EP element.
  • 🔁In one equation: one species is oxidised while another is reduced; changes are coupled.
  • 🔥Combination with O2 often signals oxidation of the other reactant (oxygen gets incorporated).
  • 🧪Decomposition of a metal oxide often shows reduction of the metal oxide (oxygen removed).

4HOW TO SPOT OXIDATION / REDUCTION (CLASSICAL QUICK CHECK)

  • If a species gains oxygen (forms an oxide): it is oxidised.
  • If a species loses oxygen (oxide → element/less-oxygen compound): it is reduced.
  • If a species gains hydrogen (hydrogenation): it is reduced.
  • If a species loses hydrogen (dehydrogenation): it is oxidised.
  • Addition of an electronegative element behaves like oxygen addition; addition of an electropositive element behaves like hydrogen addition.

5NCERT ANCHOR EXAMPLES (IDENTIFY BOTH CHANGES)

  • 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO: Mg is oxidised (oxygen added). The other part of the same reaction is the paired reduction change (oxygen combines with Mg).
  • 2HgO → 2Hg + O2: HgO is reduced (oxygen removed). Simultaneously, oxygen from HgO appears as O2, representing the paired oxidation change within the same compound.
  • Exam habit: in every equation, write one line: who gains O / loses H? and who loses O / gains H?

6OXIDATION vs REDUCTION (CLASSICAL CONCEPT)

Basis Oxidation Reduction
Oxygen / electronegative element Added to substance Removed from substance
Hydrogen / electropositive element Removed from substance Added to substance

7⭐Important Points

  • Classical oxidation/reduction is identified using oxygen/hydrogen (or EN/EP element) transfer.
  • Oxidation (classical) = +O / +EN element OR −H / −EP element.
  • Reduction (classical) = −O / −EN element OR +H / +EP element.
  • Oxidation and reduction cannot occur alone; always identify both in the same overall equation.
  • Note: a separate electronic definition (electron transfer) exists, but do not mix it if the question asks for the classical concept.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Saying only oxidation occurs in 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO; forgetting the paired reduction occurs simultaneously.
  • Mixing classical and electronic language in a classical-only question (e.g. writing only "loss of electrons").
  • Assuming reduction always means only "removal of oxygen"; ignoring "addition of hydrogen" / electropositive element.
  • Ignoring EN/EP generalisation: halogen addition can be treated like oxygen addition in the classical view.

9💡Memory Technique

“O-H Swap Rule (Classical)”
Oxidation: O in OR H out. Reduction: O out OR H in. Beside any equation: find one in and find one out (oxidation and reduction always together).
TOPIC2

ELECTRON TRANSFER CONCEPT (REDOX)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Redox (electronic concept): a reaction where electrons are transferred from one species to another.
  • Oxidation = loss of electron(s) by a species; its positive charge/oxidation number increases.
  • Reduction = gain of electron(s) by a species; its positive charge/oxidation number decreases.
  • Oxidation and reduction always occur together (electrons lost = electrons gained).
  • A redox equation can be split into oxidation half-reaction and reduction half-reaction and then added back.

2Diagram

Electron flow connects oxidation (e− release) to reduction (e− uptake); agents are defined by e− transfer.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧠OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).
  • 📤Oxidation half-reaction shows e− on products: electrons are released.
  • 📥Reduction half-reaction shows e− on reactants: electrons are consumed.
  • 🧪Oxidising agent: electron acceptor; it gets reduced.
  • 🧲Reducing agent: electron donor; it gets oxidised.
  • 🔁When adding halves: equalise electrons, add, then cancel e−.

4CORE HALF-REACTIONS (NCERT EXEMPLARS)

  • Oxidation half: 2Na(s) → 2Na+ + 2e− (Na loses e−).
  • Reduction half: Cl2(g) + 2e− → 2Cl− (Cl2 gains e−).
  • Adding halves (e− already equal): 2Na + Cl2 → 2NaCl.
  • Check: electrons cancel; atoms and overall charge balance in final equation.

5HOW TO IDENTIFY AGENTS QUICKLY

  • Species that appears as e− on product side in its half-reaction is being oxidised.
  • Species that appears as e− on reactant side in its half-reaction is being reduced.
  • Oxidising agent is the species that gets reduced (it pulls e−).
  • Reducing agent is the species that gets oxidised (it pushes e−).

6Oxidation vs Reduction vs Agents (Electron-Transfer View)

Basis Oxidation Reduction
Electron change Loses e− Gains e−
Half-reaction placement of e− e− on product side e− on reactant side
Charge/oxidation number trend Increases Decreases
Agent involved Reducing agent causes it Oxidising agent causes it
Agent definition Reducing agent = e− donor; gets oxidised Oxidising agent = e− acceptor; gets reduced

7⭐Important Points

  • OIL RIG is strictly about electrons, not oxygen/hydrogen.
  • In any redox reaction: total e− lost = total e− gained (charge conservation).
  • Half-reactions are a bookkeeping tool: add them only after equalising e−.
  • Oxidising agent and reducing agent are identified by what they do to electrons, not by names.
  • A species can be oxidised even without oxygen present (e.g., metals forming cations).

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Mixing up: "oxidising agent is oxidised" (wrong); it is reduced.
  • Writing electrons on the wrong side in half-reactions (oxidation: e− on RHS; reduction: e− on LHS).
  • Forgetting to cancel electrons while adding half-reactions.
  • Thinking oxidation always means adding oxygen; that is only the classical view.

9💡Memory Technique

“OIL RIG + Agent Trick”
OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (e−). Agent rule: OA Accepts e−, RA Releases e−. So: OA is Reduced, RA is Oxidised.
TOPIC3

COMPETITIVE ELECTRON TRANSFER (METAL DISPLACEMENT)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Competitive electron transfer is a displacement redox where two metals compete to lose/gain electrons in solution.
  • A metal strip M(s) is dipped in another metal salt containing Nn+(aq).
  • If reaction occurs: M is oxidised to Mm+, and Nn+ is reduced to N(s).
  • The metal that gets oxidised more easily is the stronger reducing agent (more reactive metal).
  • Observable changes (deposit, colour change) give a quick experimental activity order (e.g. Zn > Cu > Ag).

2Diagram

Displacement observations: Zn reduces Cu2+; Cu reduces Ag+ → reducing power Zn > Cu > Ag.

3Key Concepts

  • ⚙️General idea: M(s) + Nn+(aq) → Mm+(aq) + N(s) if M is stronger reductant
  • 🧪Metal deposited indicates which ion got reduced; the strip metal is the electron donor (reducing agent)
  • 🎯Colour changes track ion concentrations: Cu2+ is blue; fading means Cu2+ is being reduced/removed
  • 🔋This trend later matches electrochemical series: more negative E° ⇒ stronger reducing agent
  • ⚖️No visible change often means no feasible displacement or comparable tendencies (near equilibrium)

4CORE EXPERIMENTS (WHAT YOU SEE → WHAT YOU CONCLUDE)

  • Zn strip in Cu(NO3)2(aq): Cu coats Zn; blue colour decreases → Zn oxidises, Cu2+ reduces.
  • Net ionic: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s).
  • Cu strip in AgNO3(aq): Ag coats Cu; solution turns blue (Cu2+ forms).
  • Net ionic: Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) → Cu2+(aq) + 2Ag(s).
  • Co strip in NiSO4(aq): both Co2+ and Ni2+ persist → comparable tendencies; little/no net change.

5HOW TO BUILD THE REDUCING-POWER ORDER (EXAM METHOD)

  • If M displaces N from Nn+ solution, then M is more reactive and a stronger reducing agent.
  • From Zn|Cu2+ and Cu|Ag+ tests: Zn > Cu > Ag (reducing strength/activity).
  • Strong reductant: oxidises easily; its ions are comparatively weaker oxidants.
  • Do not confuse: oxidising agent is reduced (Cu2+, Ag+), reducing agent is oxidised (Zn, Cu).
  • Trend link: more negative E°(Mn+/M) ⇒ metal M is higher in activity series (stronger reductant).

6DISPLACEMENT TEST: REACTION OCCURS vs NO REACTION

Basis Reaction occurs (displacement) No reaction / very little change
Electron transfer M gives e− to Nn+ No favourable net e− transfer
What deposits? N(s) deposits on M strip No metallic coating (or negligible)
Reducing agent M(s) is stronger reductant than N(s) Reducing powers comparable or M weaker
Solution observation Ion colour may fade/appear (e.g. blue Cu2+) Colour largely unchanged
Conclusion Place M above N in activity series Cannot place clearly from this single test

7⭐Important Points

  • Displacement is a redox test for relative reducing strength of metals in aqueous salts.
  • Deposit on strip is the reduced metal; the strip metal is oxidised into solution.
  • Zn in Cu2+ gives Cu deposit + blue fades ⇒ Zn > Cu (reducing agents).
  • Cu in Ag+ gives Ag deposit + blue appears ⇒ Cu > Ag (reducing agents).
  • When no visible displacement occurs, do not force an order; metals may be close in reactivity or kinetics slow.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Calling the deposited metal the reducing agent (it is the reduced product, not the electron donor).
  • Thinking blue colour fades in CuSO4 because Zn2+ is blue (Zn2+ is colourless).
  • Writing wrong stoichiometry: forgetting 2Ag+ needed for Cu → Cu2+ + 2e−.
  • Assuming no reaction means impossible; sometimes reaction is slow due to oxide layer or concentration effects.

9💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: "ZCA" reducing power”
Remember the lab sequence: Zn coats with Cu, and Cu coats with Ag. So reducing strength order: Zn > Cu > Ag. Hook: "Zinc Can reduce Ag via Cu in between".

TOPIC4
Assigned charge idea to track oxidation/reduction even in covalent compounds

OXIDATION NUMBER BASICS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Oxidation number (oxidation state) is the assigned charge an atom would have if bonding electrons go to the more electronegative atom.
  • It assumes complete electron transfer in every bond; hence it is a book-keeping device, not the real charge in covalent molecules.
  • It helps detect oxidation/reduction by change in oxidation number, even when electron transfer is not explicit.
  • Increase in oxidation number ⇒ oxidation; decrease in oxidation number ⇒ reduction.
  • In many exam problems, the terms oxidation number and oxidation state are used interchangeably as this assigned value (not a measured atomic charge in covalent molecules).

2Diagram

Oxidation number is assigned by electronegativity; changes in it reveal redox.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧾Assign bonding pair to the more electronegative atom; then compute "as if ionic" charges.
  • 🔍Redox test: identify atoms whose oxidation numbers change between reactants and products.
  • 🧪Works for covalent species like CH4, CCl4, CO2 where no ions exist.
  • ➕More electronegative element usually gets a negative oxidation number in a bond.
  • ⚖️Sum rule: total oxidation numbers add to 0 (neutral) or to the ion charge (polyatomic ion).

4HOW TO ASSIGN OXIDATION NUMBER (CORE RULES)

  • Free element (H2, O2, Na, S8): oxidation number = 0.
  • Monoatomic ion: oxidation number = ionic charge (Na+ = +1, O2− = −2).
  • Common defaults: O = −2, H = +1, F = −1 (use these first in typical problems).
  • High-yield exceptions: O = −1 in peroxides (H2O2), O = −1/2 in superoxides (KO2), O is positive in OF2 (O = +2).
  • Hydrogen exception: H = −1 in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH2).

5COVALENT EXAMPLES (WHY IT IS A BOOK-KEEPING DEVICE)

  • In CH4: H is +1 (with non-metals), so C = −4 to make sum 0.
  • In CCl4: Cl is −1 (common halogen value), so C = +4 to make sum 0.
  • In CO2: O is −2 each, so C = +4; yet the molecule is covalent, not made of ions.
  • Same element (C) can have different oxidation numbers in different compounds: −4 to +4 here.
  • Redox identification idea: an element is oxidised if its oxidation number increases; it is reduced if its oxidation number decreases.

6OXIDATION NUMBER vs IONIC CHARGE (REAL)

Basis Oxidation number Ionic charge
Meaning Assigned (hypothetical) charge assuming full transfer Actual net charge on an ion
Where used Covalent + ionic compounds; redox tracking Only for ions (monoatomic/polyatomic)
Depends on Electronegativity-based assignment rules Electron count relative to neutral atom
Example C in CO2 is +4 (book-keeping) CO32− has charge −2 (real ion)

7⭐Important Points

  • Oxidation number is assigned by giving bonding electrons to the more electronegative atom.
  • It is a formalism; covalent molecules do not actually contain full ionic charges.
  • Oxidation = increase in oxidation number; reduction = decrease in oxidation number.
  • Apply fixed-rule values (and key exceptions) first; apply sum rule last to find unknown oxidation number.
  • Oxidation number method enables redox detection even when no e− are shown explicitly.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Treating oxidation number as the real charge on atoms inside covalent molecules.
  • Forgetting that free elements (O2, H2, Cl2) have oxidation number 0.
  • Ignoring exceptions: O in peroxides/superoxides/OF2; H in metal hydrides.
  • Applying the sum rule first without fixing standard values (H, O, F/halogens) appropriately.

9💡Memory Technique

“EN-TAKES: "More EN takes e−"”
Imagine each bond as a tug-of-war: the more electronegative atom "takes" both electrons. The electron-taker gets a more negative oxidation number; the other atom gets more positive. Then use the sum rule to finish the bookkeeping.
TOPIC5

OXIDATION NUMBER RULES

1Definition & Introduction

  • Oxidation number (O.N.) is the charge an atom would have if the bonding electron pair in each bond were assigned entirely to the more electronegative atom.
  • Assigned by fixed priority rules; use them as a book-keeping tool to track redox changes.
  • Rules 1–5 set fixed values for common atoms; apply the sum rule last to find unknown O.N.
  • O.N. may be fractional as an average when the same element exists in multiple whole-number oxidation states within one species.
  • For polyatomic ions, total O.N. sum equals the ionic charge (helps compute unknown element O.N.).

2Diagram

Use fixed-value rules first; apply the sum rule at the end to solve the unknown O.N.

3Key Concepts

  • 0️⃣Free element (uncombined): O.N. = 0 (H2, O2, Na, S8)
  • 🔋Monoatomic ion: O.N. = ionic charge (Mg2+ = +2, Cl− = −1)
  • 🟦Oxygen: usually −2; exceptions decide the whole question in peroxides/superoxides/OF2
  • 🟨Hydrogen: usually +1; in metal (ionic) hydrides H = −1
  • 🧊Fluorine: always −1; other halogens are often −1 in halides but can be + when bonded to oxygen
  • ➕Sum rule: total O.N. in neutral compound = 0; in polyatomic ion = ion charge

4RULES (IN PRIORITY ORDER)

  • Rule 1: Any free element: O.N. = 0 (Na, Mg, H2, O2, P4, S8).
  • Rule 2: Monoatomic ion: O.N. = ionic charge (Fe3+ = +3, S2− = −2).
  • Rule 3 (O): O is −2 usually; in peroxides O = −1; in superoxides O = −12.
  • O with F: In OF2, oxygen is +2; in O2F2, oxygen is +1.
  • Rule 4 (H): H is +1 usually; in metal/ionic hydrides (NaH, CaH2) H = −1.

5HOW TO ASSIGN O.N. QUICKLY (EXAM METHOD)

  • Write the species; identify if it is neutral or a polyatomic ion (note the charge).
  • Give fixed O.N. to obvious atoms: F, then O (check peroxide/superoxide/OF2), then H, then other halogens if applicable.
  • For remaining element x, use the sum rule and solve; keep track of atom counts.
  • If answer is fractional, report it as an average O.N. (structure contains integers).

6OXYGEN / HYDROGEN / HALOGENS: NORMAL vs EXCEPTIONS

Element Usually Key exceptions (most tested)
O −2 Peroxide: −1; Superoxide: −12; OF2: +2; O2F2: +1
H +1 Metal (ionic) hydrides: −1 (NaH, CaH2)
F, Cl/Br/I F: −1 always
Cl/Br/I: −1 in most halides (as X−)		 Cl/Br/I become + with oxygen (oxoacids/oxoanions); F never shows +

7⭐Important Points

  • Apply fixed-value rules first; use the sum rule last to find unknown O.N.
  • Peroxide vs superoxide: O22− gives O = −1 each; O2− gives O = −12 each.
  • In compounds with fluorine, assign F = −1 first; oxygen may become positive (OF2, O2F2).
  • Hydrogen becomes −1 only in metal/ionic hydrides; with nonmetals it stays +1.
  • Cl/Br/I can have positive O.N. in oxoacids/oxoanions, despite being −1 in many halides.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Assuming oxygen is always −2; missing peroxide/superoxide/OF2 exceptions.
  • Using the sum rule first and forcing wrong values for O or H.
  • Taking fractional O.N. as the literal state of every atom, not as an average.
  • Assigning Cl/Br/I as −1 even in oxo-species where they are positive.

9💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: F O H then SUM”
F first: Fluorine is always −1. O next: Oxygen is −2 except Peroxide −1, Superoxide −12, OF2 +2 (and O2F2 has O = +1). H next: Hydrogen is +1 except metal hydrides −1. Finish with SUM: total = 0 (neutral) or ion charge (polyatomic ion).
TOPIC6

STOCK NOTATION & FRACTIONAL OXIDATION NUMBER

1Definition & Introduction

  • Stock notation indicates a metal’s oxidation number as a Roman numeral in brackets, typically written in the compound name (and often shown with the metal in the formula for clarity).
  • Used when a metal shows variable oxidation states so the name/formula is unambiguous.
  • Fractional oxidation number is an average oxidation state calculated by rules; it is not the real state of every atom.
  • Fractional O.N. occurs when the same element exists in two or more integer oxidation states within the same species (structure-based).
  • In naming, Stock notation is placed in the compound name, e.g. iron(II) oxide, tin(IV) chloride.

3Key Concepts

  • 🔢Roman numerals: (I), (II), (III), (IV) → represent oxidation number of the metal cation
  • 🧲From formula: use common oxidation-number rules (O usually −2; halides usually −1) to determine the metal oxidation number (exceptions exist but are not the focus here)
  • 🧩Hg2Cl2 contains the dimeric cation Hg22+ so each Hg is +1 (Stock: mercury(I))
  • 📉Fractional O.N. is a bookkeeping result; real structures distribute oxidation numbers as integers on different atoms
  • 🧪Example pair: Fe(II)O vs Fe(III)2O3 are different compounds, not just different names

4STOCK NOTATION: WHAT TO WRITE

  • Write oxidation number of the metal in Roman numerals in brackets to specify its oxidation state (commonly shown in the name, e.g. iron(II) oxide).
  • Use when the metal forms more than one stable cation (Fe, Sn, Cu, Hg, Pb, etc.).
  • Examples: Sn(II)Cl2 vs Sn(IV)Cl4; Cu(I)I vs Cu(II)O.
  • Different numerals → different oxidation state → often different composition and properties.

5FRACTIONAL OXIDATION NUMBER: WHY IT APPEARS

  • Calculated using oxidation-number rules and the sum rule; may give a fraction for an element.
  • Interpretation: fraction = average oxidation state over all atoms of that element in the species.
  • Structural reality: different atoms of that element usually have integer oxidation numbers; the average can be fractional.
  • NCERT example: in C3O2, terminal C are +2 each and middle C is 0 → average C = 43.

7⭐Important Points

  • Always use Stock notation for metals with variable valency (Fe, Cu, Sn, Hg): name becomes unambiguous.
  • Roman numeral denotes oxidation number of the metal in that compound.
  • FeO and Fe2O3 are distinct compounds: Fe(II) vs Fe(III).
  • Hg2Cl2 contains Hg22+: each Hg is +1 → mercury(I) chloride.
  • Fractional O.N. means average; real oxidation states in a molecule/ion remain integers on specific atoms.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing "iron oxide" for both FeO and Fe2O3 without (II)/(III).
  • Assuming fractional O.N. implies electrons are shared in fractions by each atom.
  • Misreading Hg(I) as Hg+ only; forgetting Hg(I) commonly exists as Hg22+.
  • Putting Roman numeral on the anion (wrong): it belongs to the metal with variable O.N.

9💡Memory Technique

“STOCK = “STATE ON TAG””
Think: metal wears a tag “(II)” or “(III)” in the name/formula to show its oxidation state. If a fraction shows up, say: “Fraction = not for each atom; it’s only the average.”

TOPIC7

TYPES OF REDOX REACTIONS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Types of redox reactions are classified by the reactant–product pattern and oxidation-number changes.
  • A reaction is redox only if at least one species is oxidised and another is reduced (O.N. changes).
  • In combination and decomposition, redox usually needs an element in free state as reactant/product.
  • Displacement redox occurs when a more reactive element displaces another from its compound.
  • Disproportionation is a special redox where the same element is oxidised and reduced simultaneously.

2Diagram

Four exam-relevant types: identify by pattern, then confirm by oxidation-number change.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩Quick check: look for oxidation number change; free element has O.N. = 0.
  • 🔥Many common combustion reactions are combination redox (element + O2 → oxide).
  • 🧨Not all decompositions are redox; redox only if a product is an element (O.N. 0).
  • 🔁Displacement has 2 subtypes: metal displacement and non-metal displacement (H/halogens).
  • ⚖️Disproportionation needs an element capable of at least three oxidation states; F never disproportionates because it cannot show a positive oxidation state.

41) COMBINATION (A + B → C)

  • Redox only if A or B (or both) is an element in free state (O.N. = 0).
  • Often involves addition of O2; many combustion reactions fall in this category.
  • Example: C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g)
    O.N.: C: 0 → +4 (oxidised); O: 0 → −2 (reduced).

52) DECOMPOSITION (C → A + B)

  • Opposite of combination; redox only if at least one product is an element (O.N. = 0).
  • Example (redox): 2KClO3 → 2KCl + 3O2
    Cl: +5 → −1 (reduction); O: −2 → 0 (oxidation).
  • Counterexample (not redox): CaCO3 → CaO + CO2 (no O.N. change).

6At-a-glance classification (pattern + key condition)

Type General pattern Key condition to be redox Representative example
Combination A + B → C At least one reactant is a free element (O.N. 0) C + O2 → CO2
Decomposition C → A + B At least one product is a free element (O.N. 0) 2KClO3 → 2KCl + 3O2
Displacement X + YZ → XZ + Y X must be more reactive (better reductant/oxidant) than displaced species CuSO4 + Zn → ZnSO4 + Cu
Disproportionation A → A(oxidised) + A(reduced) Same element; intermediate O.N.; element must have at least 3 oxidation states 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2

7⭐Important Points

  • For combination/decomposition, first check whether any reactant/product is a free element (O.N. 0).
  • Decomposition is not always redox: CaCO3 → CaO + CO2 shows no O.N. change.
  • Displacement happens only if the free element is more reactive (metals: better reductant; halogens: stronger oxidant).
  • Halogen displacement trend: F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2 (oxidising power).
  • Disproportionation: same element acts as both oxidising and reducing agent in the same reaction.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Marking every decomposition as redox without checking oxidation numbers.
  • Confusing oxidising agent with oxidised species (oxidising agent itself gets reduced).
  • Forgetting free elements have oxidation number 0 (needed to spot combination/decomposition redox).
  • Assuming any halogen can displace any halide; ignoring the F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2 order.
TOPIC8

BALANCING REDOX EQUATIONS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Balancing a redox equation means conserving atoms and net charge using electron bookkeeping.
  • Core rule: total electrons lost = total electrons gained (total increase in oxidation number = total decrease).
  • Two standard methods: oxidation number method (overall) and half-reaction method (ionic, stepwise).
  • In acidic medium: use H+ and H2O to balance H and O; use e− to balance charge (in half-reaction method).
  • In basic medium: after acidic-style balancing, add OH− to both sides to remove H+ as H2O; cancel extra water.

3Key Concepts

  • 🔁Redox always has oxidation and reduction simultaneously; oxidant accepts e− (gets reduced), reductant donates e− (gets oxidised).
  • 🧮Oxidation number method: choose coefficients so Σ(ΔO.N. increase) = Σ(ΔO.N. decrease).
  • 🧩Half-reaction method: balance atoms (except O, H), then O with H2O, H with H+, and charge with e− in each half.
  • 🧪Basic medium: convert any H+ to H2O by adding equal OH− to both sides; then cancel extra H2O.
  • 🟣KMnO4 in acid reduces: MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O.

4OXIDATION NUMBER METHOD (MOLECULAR/OVERALL)

  • 1 Write correct formulas; include charges for ionic species (if present).
  • 2 Assign oxidation numbers; identify atoms whose oxidation number changes.
  • 3 Compute Δ(O.N.) per atom × number of atoms; choose coefficients so total increase = total decrease.
  • 4 Balance the overall ionic charge by adding H+ (acidic) or OH− (basic), as allowed by the medium.
  • 5 Balance H atoms by adding H2O; finally verify that all atoms and net charge match on both sides.

5HALF-REACTION (ION-ELECTRON) METHOD (BEST IN AQUEOUS IONIC)

  • 1 Write skeletal ionic equation; split into oxidation half and reduction half.
  • 2 In each half: balance all atoms except O and H.
  • 3 Balance O by adding H2O; balance H by adding H+ (acidic medium).
  • 4 Balance charge by adding e− to the more positive side; then equalise electrons, add halves and cancel e−.
  • 5 For basic medium: add OH− to both sides equal to any H+ present; convert H+ + OH− → H2O; cancel extra H2O; recheck atoms and charge.

7⭐Important Points

  • Always decide the medium first (acidic/basic/neutral); it controls whether H+ or OH− is allowed.
  • Half-reaction method: add e− only to balance charge, never to balance atoms.
  • In basic medium, do acidic-style balancing first, then remove H+ using OH− on both sides.
  • Final check must include both atom count and net ionic charge on each side.
  • For KMnO4: products depend on medium; in acid commonly Mn2+, in neutral/basic often MnO2.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Balancing O with O2 instead of H2O in aqueous ionic method.
  • Adding OH− to only one side while converting from acidic to basic.
  • Equalising electrons before correctly balancing O and H in each half reaction.
  • Forgetting to multiply Δ(O.N.) by number of atoms in the species (e.g. S in S2O32−).

REDOX TITRATIONS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Redox titration is a volumetric method where oxidant and reductant react stoichiometrically; end point is a colour change.
  • Equivalence point: moles of oxidant and reductant are exactly in stoichiometric ratio (electrons lost = electrons gained).
  • In calculations, use n-factor (electrons transferred per formula unit, in the stated medium) to connect stoichiometry to volumes.
  • End point detection may use self-indicator (titrant coloured), external indicator (added indicator), or starch–iodine complex.
  • Iodometric/iodimetric titrations often use Na2S2O3 to consume iodine; disappearance of the blue starch–iodine colour indicates the end point.

3Key Concepts

  • ⚖️Equivalence condition: nox×n-factorox = nred×n-factorred (same total electrons).
  • 🟣KMnO4 in acid is a self-indicator: first permanent pale pink persists ~30 s after equivalence.
  • 🔵K2Cr2O7 needs external indicator (diphenylamine): intense blue/violet just after equivalence.
  • 🧿Iodometric end point: iodine + starch gives deep blue; end point is blue disappears (fades to pale yellow/colourless) as I2 is reduced.
  • 🧾Key iodometric reactions: 2Cu2+ + 4I− → 2CuI(s) + I2; I2 + 2S2O32− → 2I− + S4O62−.

4EQUIVALENCE CONDITION (STOICHIOMETRY VIA)

  • Let n-factor = electrons exchanged per formula unit in the given reaction medium (acidic/basic/neutral).
  • At equivalence: total electrons lost by reductant = total electrons gained by oxidant.
  • Use equivalents: N1V1 = N2V2, where N = M×(n-factor) for the stated redox change in that medium.
  • Common n-factors (acidic): MnO4− → Mn2+ is 5; Cr2O72− → 2Cr3+ is 6; I2 → 2I− is 2.
  • For Fe2+ → Fe3+, n-factor = 1; for C2O42− → 2CO2, n-factor = 2.

5END POINT DETECTION: SELF, EXTERNAL & STARCH (IODOMETRY)

  • Self-indicator (KMnO4): before equivalence MnO4− is consumed; at end point slight excess gives pale pink.
  • Why acidic? KMnO4 shows clean reduction to Mn2+ in acid; medium affects products and n-factor.
  • External indicator (K2Cr2O7): dichromate is not a strong self-indicator; diphenylamine gives sharp blue/violet when slight excess oxidant appears.
  • Iodometric method: oxidant liberates I2 from I−; titrate I2 with thiosulphate until blue disappears.
  • Starch tip: add starch near end (pale yellow I2) to avoid strong adsorption and sluggish end point.

  • Adding starch at the start in iodometry, causing stubborn blue and slow/late end point.

LIMITS OF OXIDATION NUMBER

1Definition & Introduction

  • Oxidation number (O.N.) is the hypothetical charge an atom would have if bonding electrons were assigned completely to the more electronegative atom.
  • Hence O.N. is a formal book-keeping device: it assumes complete electron assignment even in covalent bonds.
  • In real covalent bonds, electrons are shared and the bond pair is only polarised, not fully transferred.
  • Modern bonding description: in many covalent redox processes, oxidation corresponds to a decrease in electron density around the atom(s) involved and reduction corresponds to an increase in electron density.
  • Therefore, O.N. tracks redox changes well, but it does not directly represent true atomic charges in molecules.

2Diagram

O.N. is a counting tool; in covalent bonds, redox is more realistically framed as electron-density decrease/increase rather than full charge transfer.

3Key Concepts

  • ⚠️Core limitation: O.N. treats every bond as 100% ionic for counting purposes.
  • 🧲Covalent reality: electron density shifts toward the more electronegative atom (bond polarity).
  • 🔁Redox in covalent chemistry need not involve fully separated ions; charge transfer is often partial and best viewed via density shift.
  • 🧩Fractional O.N. values indicate an average, not fractional electrons on each atom.
  • 🎯Exam lens: identify oxidation/reduction by O.N. change, but interpret bonding via electron density.

4WHY OXIDATION NUMBER IS ONLY AN APPROXIMATION

  • Definition uses a hypothetical ionic limit: assign shared pair entirely to the more electronegative atom.
  • Real covalent bonds show partial charge (δ+ / δ−), not full integer ionic charges.
  • O.N. depends on electronegativity-based assignment; it is not a directly measurable atomic charge.
  • Resonance/delocalisation can spread electron density over multiple atoms, but O.N. still forces integer counting rules.
  • So O.N. is best treated as book-keeping for balancing and identifying redox changes.

5ELECTRON-DENSITY REFRAMING (MODERN VIEW)

  • Oxidation: electron density around the atom(s) involved decreases (often on bonding to more electronegative atoms).
  • Reduction: electron density around the atom(s) involved increases (often on bonding to less electronegative atoms).
  • This viewpoint avoids imagining unrealistically complete electron transfer in covalent bonds.
  • In problems, O.N. change remains the fastest and standard method to identify oxidation/reduction.

7⭐Important Points

  • O.N. definition itself embeds the complete electron assignment assumption.
  • In covalent bonds, oxidation/reduction is better described as electron density decrease/increase around the atoms involved.
  • Do not equate oxidation number with true atomic charge; it is a formal assignment.
  • Fractional oxidation numbers are averages; they indicate mixed oxidation states in the structure.
  • Despite limitations, O.N. change remains the standard exam criterion for identifying redox.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Claiming oxidation number gives the actual charge on an atom in a covalent molecule.
  • Thinking fractional oxidation numbers mean fractional electrons or physically fractional charges on each atom.
  • Using OIL RIG lines without noting that, in covalent systems, the realistic picture is often density shift rather than full transfer.
  • Not specifying which atom(s) are considered when saying oxidation/reduction; electron density change is atom-specific.

ELECTRODE PROCESSES AND STANDARD ELECTRODE POTENTIAL (E°)

1Definition & Introduction

  • In a galvanic (voltaic) cell, a redox reaction is split into two half-cells so electrons flow through an external wire.
  • Oxidation occurs at the anode; reduction occurs at the cathode (galvanic cell).
  • A redox couple is written oxidised/reduced, e.g. Zn2+/Zn, Cu2+/Cu.
  • Standard electrode potential E° is electrode potential at unit activities: ~1 M solutes, ~1 bar gases (older texts: 1 atm), 298 K; measured vs SHE (0.00 V).
  • Sign of E° indicates reduction tendency relative to H+/H2: positive = easier reduction (stronger oxidant), negative = harder reduction (stronger reductant).
  • In a Daniell cell, Zn is anode (Zn → Zn2+ + 2e−), Cu is cathode (Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu); electrons go anode → cathode.

2Diagram

Daniell cell: Zn anode, Cu cathode; electron flow anode → cathode; salt bridge ions maintain neutrality.

3Key Concepts

  • 🧪Half-cell notation: Zn(s)|Zn2+(aq) || Cu2+(aq)|Cu(s)
  • 🔁Salt bridge completes internal circuit; anions → anode, cations → cathode to maintain neutrality
  • ⚡Electron flow is anode → cathode; conventional current is opposite
  • 📏Cell emf under standard conditions: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
  • 📚Electrochemical series (usual increasing E° order): oxidising power increases, reducing power decreases

4DANIELL CELL: ELECTRODE PROCESSES

  • Anode (oxidation): Zn(s) → Zn2+(aq) + 2e−; Zn dissolves, [Zn2+] increases.
  • Cathode (reduction): Cu2+(aq) + 2e− → Cu(s); Cu deposits, blue colour fades.
  • External circuit carries electrons; a voltmeter measures potential difference between electrodes.
  • Salt bridge contains inert electrolyte (e.g. KCl/KNO3/NH4NO3) in gel; prevents mixing but allows ion migration.

5STANDARD ELECTRODE POTENTIAL AND ELECTROCHEMICAL SERIES

  • SHE: Pt | H2(g, 1 bar) | H+(aq, 1 M); E°(H+/H2) = 0.00 V by convention.
  • More positive E° (usual increasing-E° table order) ⇒ stronger oxidising agent; more negative E° ⇒ stronger reducing agent.
  • For spontaneous galvanic cell: choose higher E° as cathode (reduction), lower E° as anode (oxidation).
  • Example values: E°(Cu2+/Cu) = +0.34 V; E°(Zn2+/Zn) = −0.76 V.

8⭐Important Points

  • Electrode potentials listed in series are reduction potentials; oxidation potential = −(reduction potential).
  • For galvanic cells: anode is negative, cathode is positive; electrons flow anode → cathode.
  • Salt bridge: anions migrate to anode compartment; cations migrate to cathode compartment to keep charge balance.
  • Standard conditions: solutes ~1 M, gases ~1 bar (often written 1 atm in older problems), T = 298 K.
  • In the usual increasing-E° electrochemical series order: higher E° ⇒ stronger oxidising agent; lower E° ⇒ stronger reducing agent.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Using E°cell = E°anode − E°cathode (sign reversed).
  • Choosing anode/cathode by metal reactivity without checking given E° values.
  • Thinking salt bridge sends electrons; it sends ions only (internal circuit).
  • Forgetting that tabulated E° values are reduction potentials, not oxidation potentials.
