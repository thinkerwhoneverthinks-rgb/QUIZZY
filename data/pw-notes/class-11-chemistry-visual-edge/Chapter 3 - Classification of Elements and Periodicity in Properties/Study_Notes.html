TOPIC1

NEED FOR CLASSIFICATION (ELEMENTS)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Classification = systematic grouping of elements so that similar properties are studied together, avoiding isolated study of each element.
  • As discoveries increased (e.g., 31 known in 1800; 63 by 1865; 114+ today), a framework became essential to manage the growing data.
  • Similar chemical behaviour repeats at regular intervals (periodicity), enabling meaningful grouping.
  • Periodic classification rationalises known chemical facts by linking properties to atomic number and electronic configuration.
  • It also predicts likely properties/compounds of elements from their position in the periodic table.

2Key Concepts

  • 🗂️Systematic grouping prevents memorising isolated facts for 100+ elements and countless compounds.
  • 🔁Periodicity: properties recur when elements are arranged in increasing atomic number.
  • 👨‍🔬Families/groups: same group ⇒ similar valence-shell configuration ⇒ similar chemistry.
  • 🔮Position-based prediction: common valency/oxidation states, typical oxides/hydrides/halides, and reactivity trends can be inferred.
  • 🧠Rationalisation: trends (size, metallic character, reactivity) become explainable instead of descriptive.

3WHY CLASSIFY ELEMENTS?

  • Studying each element separately is inefficient due to huge number of compounds and reactions.
  • Classification provides a common language to describe behaviour (group, period, block).
  • Similarities in properties become easier to spot (e.g., alkali metals form similar types of salts).
  • Lets chemistry be learnt as patterns rather than lists of disconnected facts.

4HOW CLASSIFICATION HELPS (EXAM ANGLE)

  • Places elements so similar valence-shell configuration falls in the same group.
  • Helps predict common oxidation states and formulas of common compounds (oxides, hydrides, halides).
  • Explains trends across a period and down a group (size, metallic/non-metallic character, reactivity).
  • Gives a framework to compare an unfamiliar element with its neighbours (periodic trends).

6Solved Q&A

1
Explain: Why did classification of elements become essential in the 19th century?
Answer:
The number of known elements increased (e.g., 31 in 1800; 63 by 1865), so chemical information expanded rapidly.
Each element forms many compounds; studying them one-by-one is inefficient and confusing.
Grouping elements by recurring similarities created a compact framework to organise facts and compare behaviour.
✓  Because the growing number of elements and their compounds demanded a systematic framework to organise and compare recurring properties.
2

A: Elements in the same group show similar chemical properties.
R: They have similar valence-shell electronic configuration.
Answer:
Chemical properties mainly depend on valence electrons involved in bonding and ion formation.
In a group, the valence-shell electron pattern is similar (only the principal quantum number changes down the group).
Therefore elements of a group show similar typical valency, similar compound types, and related reactivity.
✓  Both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A.

7⭐Important Points

  • Core need: manage and summarise chemical behaviour of 100+ elements into patterns.
  • Classification is based on recurring similarities in physical and chemical properties.
  • Modern periodic table is the most effective classification: elements arranged by atomic number.
  • Same group ⇒ similar valence-shell configuration ⇒ similar chemistry (family concept).
  • Big payoff: organise known facts, predict likely properties, and rationalise trends.

8❌Common Mistakes

  • Treating classification as mere memorisation of the table rather than a tool to infer properties.
  • Saying periodicity is based on atomic mass (confusing Mendeleev with modern periodic law).
  • Assuming all properties are identical within a group; they are similar with gradual variation.
  • Forgetting the key link: similar properties arise mainly from valence electronic configuration.

GENESIS OF PERIODIC CLASSIFICATION

1Definition & Introduction

  • Genesis of periodic classification is the historical development of arranging elements to reveal repeating (periodic) properties.
  • Driven by rapid growth in known elements: about 31 (around 1800) → about 63 (around 1865) → 114+ today.
  • Early attempts correlated atomic weight with similar chemical properties and recurring patterns.
  • Mendeleev introduced a workable table with predictions and gaps for undiscovered elements.
  • Moseley proved atomic number is fundamental, giving the modern basis of periodicity.

2Diagram

  • Shift in basis: Atomic weight patterns → Predictive table → Atomic number law
  • Explained by periodic repetition of valence electronic configuration
  • Timeline of key milestones leading to Modern Periodic Law (atomic number basis).

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩Core idea: properties repeat at regular intervals when elements are arranged systematically.
  • ⚖️19th century basis was mostly atomic weight; modern basis is atomic number (Z).
  • 🔮A strong classification must predict new elements and correct placements, not just fit known data.
  • 🧾Key contributors: Döbereiner, de Chancourtois, Newlands, Lothar Meyer, Mendeleev, Moseley.
  • 🧠Modern explanation of periodicity: periodic repetition of electronic configuration with increasing Z.

4TIMELINE: KEY CONTRIBUTORS & IDEAS

  • 1817–1829 Döbereiner: triads of 3 similar elements; middle shows intermediate properties.
  • Triad rule: middle atomic weight ≈ average of other two (worked for few sets only).

5WHY MENDELEEV & MOSELEY WERE TURNING POINTS

  • Mendeleev’s law: properties periodic in atomic weight (historical), but had mass-based anomalies.

  • Claiming Newlands’ law works for all elements; it breaks after Ca.
  • Confusing de Chancourtois’ helix with Newlands’ octaves (both are pre-Mendeleev but different models).
  • Thinking Mendeleev strictly followed increasing atomic weight without exceptions.

DÖBEREINER'S TRIADS

1Definition & Introduction

  • Döbereiner's triads are groups of three elements with similar properties and related atomic masses.
  • In a triad, the atomic mass of the middle element is approximately the arithmetic mean of the other two.
  • The properties of the middle element are generally intermediate between the first and third elements.
  • Proposed during 1817–1829; among the earliest links between atomic mass and chemical properties.
  • Worked for only a few sets; limited applicability led many to dismiss it as coincidence.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🧩Triad criterion: similar chemistry + mass relation (middle ≈ mean of extremes).
  • ⚖️Example idea: Li, Na, K show similar chemistry; Na has atomic mass close to the average of Li and K.
  • 🌟Property pattern: middle often shows intermediate trend (e.g., reactivity, density) within the triad.
  • 🕜Historical importance: first serious step towards the Periodic Law idea.
  • 🚫Limitation: could not classify most known elements; many did not fit into triads.

4HOW A TRIAD IS IDENTIFIED (EXAM-USEFUL CHECKLIST)

  • Pick three elements with similar chemical behaviour (often same modern group).
  • Arrange them by increasing atomic mass: light → middle → heavy.
  • Check the mass relation: middle atomic mass is close to the mean of first and third.
  • Check property gradation: the middle shows intermediate trend (e.g., reactivity).

5STANDARD TRIADS TO REMEMBER (WITH COMMONLY USED ATOMIC MASSES)

  • Alkali metals: Li (7), Na (23), K (39) → Na is close to the mean of Li and K.
  • Alkaline earth metals: Ca (40), Sr (88), Ba (137) → Sr is close to the mean of Ca and Ba (using rounded masses).
  • Halogens: Cl (35.5), Br (80), I (127) → Br is close to the mean of Cl and I.
  • Do not overclaim: “approximately” is key; small deviations are acceptable.

7Solved Q&A

1
Given Li, Na, K. Explain why they are treated as a Döbereiner triad.
Answer:
They show similar chemical properties: all are alkali metals, typically form 1+ ions and react with water.
Their atomic masses increase from Li to Na to K, so they can be arranged in increasing mass order.
In this set, the middle element (Na) has an atomic mass that is close to the average of the other two, matching Döbereiner’s empirical rule.
Na also shows intermediate properties between Li and K in several trends (as observed historically).
✓  Li–Na–K fits both triad ideas: similar chemistry and the middle element has atomic mass approximately equal to the mean of the other two, with intermediate properties.
2

Answer:
Assertion is true for Döbereiner triads: property gradation was observed within each triad.
Reason is also true: the key triad pattern is middle atomic mass ≈ mean of extremes.
At Döbereiner level, the mass relation was used as an empirical indicator associated with intermediate properties.
Modern causation is based on atomic number and electronic configuration, not atomic mass alone.
✓  Both Assertion and Reason are true; the Reason supports the assertion empirically in triads, but it is not a modern causal explanation.

8⭐Important Points

  • Time period: 1817–1829; among the first systematic classifications.
  • Triad rule: middle atomic mass ≈ mean of the other two; “approximately” is crucial.
  • Middle element shows intermediate properties (often reactivity, density, etc.).
  • Must-know examples: Li–Na–K, Ca–Sr–Ba, Cl–Br–I.
  • Historical role: seed idea for periodicity, but applicability was very limited.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing “equal to mean” instead of “approximately” equal; triads allow small deviations.
  • Treating triads as a complete periodic table; it worked for only a few element sets.
  • Confusing modern periodic law basis (atomic number) with Döbereiner’s basis (atomic mass).
  • Using mass relation alone without checking similar properties.

NEWLANDS' LAW OF OCTAVES

1Definition & Introduction

  • Newlands (1865): On arranging elements in increasing atomic weight, every 8th element resembles the 1st in properties.
  • Analogy to music: the 8th note is a repetition of the 1st note (an octave).
  • Newlands placed elements in rows of 7; the next element (8th) starts a new row and lies under the 1st element to compare similarity.
  • It was an early systematic recognition of periodic recurrence (periodicity) of chemical properties.
  • The law showed good similarity patterns mainly only up to calcium (Ca); it failed for heavier elements.

2Diagram

Newlands wrote 7 elements per row; the 8th starts the next row and is compared with the 1st element above it.
 

3Key Concepts

  • 📈Arrangement criterion: increasing atomic weight (not atomic number).
  • 8️⃣Core pattern: element 8 resembles element 1, element 9 resembles element 2, etc.
  • 🧱Layout idea: 7 elements per row; the next (8th) begins the next row so similar ones align vertically.
  • ✅Classic example: Li shows similarity with Na when ordered by increasing atomic weight.
  • ⚠️Limitation: the octave pattern worked well mainly only up to Ca; beyond this it became inconsistent.

4WORKING (HOW NEWLANDS ARRANGED ELEMENTS)

  • Write elements in order of increasing atomic weight.
  • Place them in horizontal rows of 7 elements.
  • Compare element 1 with 8, 2 with 9, etc. → similarity expected.
  • Example: Li (1st) and Na (8th) fall in the same column.

5MERITS (WHY IT IS IMPORTANT)

  • Gave an early, clear statement of periodicity (recurrence of properties).
  • Used a simple, testable numerical pattern to compare elements systematically.
  • Highlighted that chemical properties are not random; they show regular repetition.

7Solved Q&A

1
State Newlands' Law of Octaves and illustrate it using the Li → Na example.
Answer:
Law: Arrange elements by increasing atomic weight; every 8th element resembles the 1st in properties.
Example order: Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F are the 1st to 7th elements in the sequence.
The next element, Na, is the 8th and is placed under Li in Newlands' rows-of-7 scheme.
Conclusion: Li and Na should show similar chemical properties (both are alkali metals).
✓  Newlands proposed that in increasing atomic-weight order, every 8th element repeats similarity; Li and Na form a classic octave example.
2
Give two reasons why Newlands' Law of Octaves was not universally accepted.
Answer:
It worked well mainly only up to Ca; after that the “every 8th” similarity failed for many elements.
It left no gaps for undiscovered elements, so later elements could not be fitted smoothly into the strict octave pattern.
✓  Limited validity (mainly up to Ca) and inability to accommodate new/heavier elements in a strict octave sequence.

8⭐Important Points

  • Proposed by J. A. R. Newlands (1865).
  • Elements were arranged by increasing atomic weight.
  • Key idea: every 8th element resembles the 1st (octave analogy).
  • He wrote elements in rows of 7 so the 8th starts the next row and aligns vertically with the 1st.
  • Worked well mainly up to Ca; failed for heavier elements.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing “every 7th element is similar” instead of every 8th.
  • Saying Newlands used atomic number; he used atomic weight.
  • Claiming the octave pattern works for all elements.
  • Forgetting that Newlands used 7 per row so the 8th starts the next row.

MENDELEEV'S PERIODIC LAW

1Definition & Introduction

  • Mendeleev's Periodic Law (1869): properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic weights.
  • Elements were arranged in increasing atomic weight into periods (rows) and groups (columns).
  • Elements with similar chemical properties (similar valency/compound formulas) were kept in the same group.
  • Mendeleev left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties using periodic trends.
  • Some elements were placed out of strict atomic-weight order to preserve similarity of properties.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 📏Basis: atomic weight ordering + property similarity (valency, oxide/chloride formulas).
  • 🧩Structure: periods show repeating trends; groups contain chemically similar elements.
  • ⛳Bold step: left gaps ("eka-" elements) instead of forcing wrong fits.
  • 🔮Predictive success: forecast eka-aluminium → Ga (E2O3, ECl3) and eka-silicon → Ge (EO2, ECl4).
  • ⚠️Anomalies: I before Te (and others later explained by atomic number).

4HOW MENDELEEV BUILT THE TABLE (EXAM VIEW)

  • Placed elements so that valency and formulas of oxides/hydrides/chlorides repeated periodically.
  • Vertical columns = groups (family resemblance); horizontal rows = periods (repeating trends).
  • Used property priority: if weight order conflicted with properties, properties decided placement.
  • Corrected doubtful atomic weights of some elements using periodicity as a check.

5PREDICTIONS + ANOMALIES (WHAT TO QUOTE)

  • Eka-aluminium prediction matched gallium: density predicted 5.9 g/cm³, found 5.94 g/cm³.
  • Eka-silicon prediction matched germanium: oxide EO2, chloride ECl4 like Si.
  • Anomaly example: iodine placed before tellurium to keep I with halogens (similar properties).
  • Reason anomalies existed: atomic weights are not the fundamental driver of periodicity (atomic number is).

7Solved Q&A

1
Why did Mendeleev leave gaps in his periodic table instead of filling them with known elements?
Answer:
If a known element did not match the group properties (valency, compound formulas), Mendeleev did not force-fit it.
He assumed an undiscovered element existed there and kept a gap to preserve periodic similarity.
This allowed prediction of properties (approximate atomic weight, density, oxide/chloride formulas), later verified.
✓  He prioritised chemical similarity and left gaps for undiscovered elements, enabling successful predictions that validated his table.
2
Explain the placement of iodine before tellurium in Mendeleev's table, even though iodine has slightly lower atomic weight.
Answer:
Iodine shows halogen properties (forms I−, HI; typical Group 17 chemistry).
Tellurium shows chalcogen properties (forms TeO2, H2Te; typical Group 16 chemistry).
To keep similar elements in the same group, Mendeleev placed I with halogens and Te with chalcogens, breaking strict weight order.
✓  Because chemical properties were given priority over atomic weight, I was kept with halogens and Te with chalcogens.

8⭐Important Points

  • Exact statement to quote: properties are a periodic function of atomic weights (not atomic number).
  • Mendeleev’s key innovation: left gaps and predicted properties of missing elements (eka-elements).
  • Predictive evidence: eka-Al → Ga and eka-Si → Ge, including correct oxide/chloride formulas.
  • He sometimes reversed atomic-weight order (I/Te) to maintain chemical similarity in groups.
  • Mendeleev’s table worked for most known elements of his time but had anomalies due to mass-based basis.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Writing Mendeleev’s law as periodic function of atomic number (that is Modern Periodic Law).
  • Thinking Mendeleev always followed increasing atomic weight with no exceptions.
  • Not connecting predictions to specific examples: eka-Al = Ga, eka-Si = Ge.
  • Stating I/Te placement was an error, without mentioning it preserved group properties.

MODERN PERIODIC LAW (MOSELEY: ATOMIC NUMBER BASIS)

1Definition & Introduction

  • Modern Periodic Law: Physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers (Z).
  • Moseley (1913) used characteristic X-rays to show that for a given X-ray series, √ν vs Z is a straight line (while plotting √ν vs atomic mass is not).
  • Atomic number equals nuclear charge (= number of protons) and fixes the number of electrons in a neutral atom.
  • Periodicity arises from periodic repetition of valence-shell electronic configuration with increasing Z.
  • This law resolves Mendeleev’s mass-order anomalies: Ar–K, Co–Ni, Te–I.

2Diagram

Characteristic X-ray frequency depends on nuclear charge; hence atomic number is the true periodic basis.

3Key Concepts

  • 📈Moseley’s evidence: for a fixed X-ray series, √ν increases linearly with Z
  • ⚛️Z is fundamental: governs electron count → electronic configuration → chemistry
  • 🧩Mass anomalies vanish because ordering is by Z, not by atomic weight
  • 🔁Group similarity: same valence configuration repeats at specific Z intervals

4MOSELEY’S X-RAY EVIDENCE (WHAT HE PLOTTED & WHAT IT MEANT)

  • Elements bombarded with fast electrons emit characteristic X-rays (K, L series).
  • For a fixed series, √ν varies linearly with Z (often written as √ν ∝ (Z − screening)).
  • Plotting √ν vs Z gives linearity; plotting vs atomic mass does not.
  • Therefore atomic number (nuclear charge) is the fundamental property behind periodicity.

5HOW MODERN PERIODIC LAW FIXES MENDELEEV’S ANOMALIES

  • Ar–K: Ar mass 39.9 > K mass 39.1, but Z(Ar)=18 and Z(K)=19 → noble gas before alkali metal.
  • Co–Ni: Co mass 58.93 > Ni mass 58.69, but Z(Co)=27 and Z(Ni)=28 → Co before Ni matches properties.
  • Te–I: Te mass 127.6 > I mass 126.9, but Z(Te)=52 and Z(I)=53 → chalcogen before halogen.
  • Reason for periodicity: as Z increases, valence-shell configuration repeats (e.g., ns1 for Group 1).

1
Why did Mendeleev place iodine after tellurium even though iodine has a lower atomic mass?
Answer:
Mendeleev prioritised similarity of chemical properties (grouping) over strict mass order.

2

Answer:
A straight-line relation shows a direct dependence of characteristic X-ray frequency on nuclear charge.
Nuclear charge is determined by atomic number (Z), not by atomic mass.
Therefore periodic classification should be based on increasing Z.
✓  Atomic number (Z) is the fundamental basis of periodicity, not atomic mass.

8⭐Important Points

  • Modern Periodic Law: properties are periodic functions of atomic number (Z).
  • Moseley’s evidence: for a given X-ray series, √ν vs Z is linear.
  • Modern law fixes mass-order anomalies: Ar before K, Co before Ni, Te before I.
  • Atomic number determines electron count → electronic configuration → periodic repetition of properties.
  • In the modern periodic table, elements are arranged in increasing Z; groups reflect similar valence configuration.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Stating Modern Periodic Law as periodicity with atomic weight (that is Mendeleev’s law).
  • Saying Moseley proved periodicity by mass; he correlated X-ray frequency with Z (nuclear charge).
  • Explaining anomalies using mass arguments instead of atomic number/electronic configuration.
  • Mixing up the anomaly pairs (write: Ar–K, Co–Ni, Te–I).

LONG FORM PERIODIC TABLE

1Definition & Introduction

  • Long form periodic table arranges elements in increasing atomic number (Z) into 7 periods and 18 groups.
  • Elements in the same group have similar valence-shell electronic configuration → similar chemistry.
  • Period number equals the highest principal quantum number (n) occupied in the ground-state configuration.
  • Period lengths arise from the number of orbitals filled as per Aufbau sequence and subshell capacities.
  • IUPAC uses group numbers 1–18 (replaces old IA–VIIIA/IB–VIIIB/0).

2Key Concepts

  • 📌7 periods (rows) show filling of a new valence shell; each period ends at a noble gas.
  • 🏛️18 groups (columns) cluster elements with the same valence-shell electronic configuration pattern (s-, p- and for transition elements, (n−1)d involvement).
  • 🔢IUPAC groups: 1–2 (s-block), 3–12 (d-block), 13–18 (p-block); f-block shown separately.
  • 🧠Period lengths: 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32 (7th is incomplete but capacity is 32).
  • ➡️Orbital filling causes 4th period to include 3d and 6th period to include 4f before p fills.
  • ⚠️He is 1s2 (s-like) but placed in Group 18 due to completely filled valence shell.

3PERIODS: LENGTHS & ORBITALS FILLED

  • Period 1 (n=1): 2 elements; fills 1s.
  • Period 2 (n=2): 8 elements; fills 2s then 2p.
  • Period 3 (n=3): 8 elements; fills 3s then 3p (3d starts after 4s).
  • Period 4 (n=4): 18 elements; fills 4s → 3d → 4p.
  • Period 5 (n=5): 18 elements; fills 5s → 4d → 5p.

4GROUPS (IUPAC 1–18) & VALENCE CONFIGURATION PATTERNS

  • Group 1: ns1 (alkali metals); Group 2: ns2 (alkaline earth).
  • Groups 13–18: ns2np1 to ns2np6 (p-block).
  • Groups 3–12: (n−1)d filling; general valence pattern (n−1)d1–10ns0–2.
  • f-block: (n−2)f filling; shown separately to keep table compact (lanthanoids/actinoids).
  • Down a group: n changes, but valence-electron pattern stays same → similar reactivity.

  • Long form periodic table is strictly by increasing atomic number (Z), not atomic mass.
  • 7 periods, 18 groups; group numbers are IUPAC 1–18 only.

  • Period lengths: 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32 (7th incomplete but capacity 32).

IUPAC NAMING FOR SUPERHEAVY ELEMENTS

1Definition & Introduction

  • A temporary, systematic IUPAC system to name newly synthesised superheavy elements (generally Z ≥ 100) before an official permanent name is ratified.
  • Atomic number digits are replaced by fixed numerical roots and joined in order.
  • The name ends with the suffix -ium (systematic name).
  • The temporary systematic symbol is a three-letter symbol made from the first letters of the roots (first letter capital, next two lowercase).
  • Used to avoid disputes; later replaced by an IUPAC-approved permanent name/symbol.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🔢Digits → roots: 0 nil, 1 un, 2 bi, 3 tri, 4 quad, 5 pent, 6 hex, 7 sept, 8 oct, 9 enn
  • 🧩Name rule: root(1st digit)+root(2nd)+root(3rd)+...+ium
  • 🏷️Symbol rule (temporary systematic): first letters of each root in order (capital + 2 lowercase)
  • ⚖️Temporary names are systematic; official names are ratified later and may honor place/scientist

4NUMERICAL ROOTS (MUST MEMORISE)

  • 0 nil (n), 1 un (u), 2 bi (b), 3 tri (t), 4 quad (q)
  • 5 pent (p), 6 hex (h), 7 sept (s), 8 oct (o), 9 enn (e)
  • Write roots strictly in the same order as digits of Z (left to right).

5ALGORITHM (EXAM-FRIENDLY STEPS)

  • Step 1: Split Z into digits (e.g., 120 → 1, 2, 0).
  • Step 2: Replace each digit by its root (1→un, 2→bi, 0→nil).
  • Step 3: Join roots + add suffix ium → Unbinilium.
  • Step 4: Temporary symbol = initials of roots → Ubn for un-bi-nil.
  • Reminder: This is a temporary systematic IUPAC name; later a permanent name is adopted.

ELECTRONIC CONFIGURATION AND POSITION

1Definition & Introduction

  • Block (s/p/d/f) of an element is decided by the subshell into which the differentiating (last added) electron enters.
  • Aufbau principle: electrons occupy lower-energy orbitals first; hence the filling sequence controls periods and blocks.
  • Period number = highest principal quantum number (n) present in the ground-state electronic configuration (usually the ns of that period).
  • Elements in the same group have the same valence-shell pattern for s- and p-block (same outer electronic configuration).
  • d-Block corresponds to filling of (n−1)d along with ns; f-Block corresponds to filling of (n−2)f (inner transition).

2Diagram


 

3Key Concepts

  • 🧠Find the differentiating electron → decide block → decide period (highest n) → decide group from valence pattern.
  • 🧱Blocks: s (ns1–2), p (ns2np1–6), d ((n−1)d with ns), f ((n−2)f with ns).
  • ⬇️Down a group: same outer configuration type; n increases (e.g., ns1, ns2np5).
  • 🔁Across a period: electrons are added to orbitals of the same highest n (ns then np), with d/f insertions in longer periods.
  • ⚠️Key order fact: 4s fills before 3d, but within period 4, 3d fills before 4p.

4AUFBAU ORDER: THE EXAM-RELEVANT PART

  • Energy order (core sequence): 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p.
  • Reason for long periods: insertion of (n−1)d and (n−2)f between ns and np of the same period.
  • Period 4: K starts 4s; then 3d fills (Sc→Zn); only then 4p fills (Ga→Kr).
  • Period 6: after 6s, 4f fills (lanthanoids), then 5d, then 6p.

5GROUPWISE CONFIGURATION (MAIN GROUP) + d/f SERIES PLACEMENT

  • Representative (s,p) groups: same outer configuration type across group (only n changes).
  • Group 1: ns1; Group 2: ns2.
  • Group 13→18: ns2np1–6 respectively; noble gases end a period (ns2np6, except He).
  • d-Block: differentiating electron in (n−1)d; general configuration: (n−1)d1–10ns0–2.
  • f-Block placed separately to keep table compact: 4f series Ce(58)→Lu(71); 5f series Th(90)→Lr(103).

  • Period number is given by the highest principal quantum number (n) present in the ground-state electronic configuration.

BLOCKS AND ELEMENT TYPES

1Definition & Introduction

  • Block = set of elements in which the last (differentiating) electron enters the same subshell (s/p/d/f).
  • Block fixes the valence-shell configuration pattern → similar chemical behaviour within families/groups.
  • s + p together are representative (main-group) elements; d are transition; f are inner-transition.
  • Helium has 1s2 (s-subshell filled) but is placed in Group 18 due to noble-gas-like inertness (filled valence shell).
  • Hydrogen has 1s1; it shows resemblance to Group 1 (forms H+) and Group 17 (forms H−), so it is shown separately.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 🎯Block = subshell of differentiating electron: s, p, d, f.
  • 🧪s-block: Groups 1–2; valence ns1 or ns2; highly electropositive metals.
  • 🧷p-block: Groups 13–18; valence ns2np1 to ns2np6; includes metals, non-metals, metalloids.
  • 🧲d-block: Groups 3–12; general (n−1)d1–d10 ns0–ns2; variable oxidation states, coloured ions, catalysis.
  • 💡Zn, Cd, Hg are d-block but not typical transition elements (d10, no unpaired d-electrons in common ions).
  • 🧱f-block: lanthanoids & actinoids; (n−2)f1–f14(n−1)d0–d1ns2; properties within a series are very similar.

4BLOCK-WISE ELECTRONIC CONFIGURATION (EXAM READY)

  • s-block: outer configuration ns1 (Group 1) or ns2 (Group 2).
  • p-block: outer configuration ns2np1 to ns2np6; ends with noble gases (np6, except He).
  • d-block: general configuration (n−1)d1–d10ns0–ns2; differentiating electron enters (n−1)d.
  • f-block: general configuration (n−2)f1–f14(n−1)d0–d1ns2; differentiating electron enters (n−2)f.
  • Representative elements = s + p block; chemistry dominated by valence-shell electrons.

5TYPICAL PROPERTIES + SPECIAL CASES

  • s-block: low ionization enthalpy; form M+/M2+; compounds mostly ionic (Li, Be show more covalency).
  • p-block: widest variety; includes metals, non-metals, metalloids; many covalent compounds.
  • d-block: variable oxidation states, complex formation, paramagnetism, coloured ions, catalytic activity.
  • Zn, Cd, Hg: d-block but not typical transition since d10 → usually fixed +2, no coloured/paramagnetic ions commonly.
  • f-block: lanthanoid contraction; actinoids show more variable oxidation states and radioactivity.

PERIODIC TRENDS AND REACTIVITY

1Definition & Introduction

  • Periodic trends are regular variations in properties with atomic number due to changing effective nuclear charge and shielding.
  • Across a period: atomic size ↓, ionization enthalpy (IE) ↑, electronegativity (EN) ↑, electron gain enthalpy (ΔegH) generally becomes more negative (up to halogens), metallic character ↓.
  • Down a group: atomic size ↑, IE ↓, EN ↓, metallic character ↑; ΔegH often becomes less negative (not strictly regular).
  • Ionic size: cation smaller than atom; anion larger than atom; isoelectronic size depends mainly on nuclear charge.
  • Reactivity across a period: extremes are most reactive (alkali metals as reducing agents, halogens as oxidizing agents); noble gases least reactive.
  • Oxides across a period: basic → amphoteric/less basic → acidic; linked to metallic to non-metallic character.

2Diagram

3Key Concepts

  • 📏Covalent radius = half of X–X single-bond length; metallic radius = half of nearest-neighbour distance.
  • 🧲Across a period, Zeff ↑ dominates → size ↓ and IE/EN ↑.
  • ➕Ionization enthalpy (IE): X(g) → X+(g)+e−; IE2 > IE1 always.
  • ➖Electron gain enthalpy (ΔegH): X(g)+e−→X−(g); more negative value means greater tendency to form anions.
  • ⚡Electronegativity (Pauling): relative attraction for shared pair; F is highest (about 4.0 on Pauling scale).
  • 🧩Second-period anomalies: small size, high EN, no d-orbitals → maximum covalency 4 and strong pπ–pπ bonding.

4ATOMIC & IONIC RADIUS (EXAM PATTERNS)

  • Covalent radius: ½ × bond length in X–X (single bond).
  • Metallic radius: ½ × nearest-neighbour distance in metallic lattice.
  • Across period: radius decreases (Zeff ↑, same shell).
  • Down group: radius increases (new shell + shielding ↑).
  • Cation smaller than atom (less e−, same nuclear charge).

5IE, ELECTRON GAIN ENTHALPY & ELECTRONEGATIVITY (WITH ANOMALIES)

  • Ionization enthalpy (IE): energy to remove the most loosely held electron from X(g).
  • General trends: across period IE ↑; down group IE ↓.
  • Key anomalies: Be > B (2s more penetrating than 2p); N > O (half-filled 2p3 stability).
  • Electron gain enthalpy (ΔegH): enthalpy change on adding e−; more negative value → easier anion formation.
  • Halogen anomaly: F has less negative ΔegH than Cl (compact 2p electron–electron repulsion).

  • Do not compare noble-gas radii with covalent radii; use van der Waals radii conceptually.
  • IE2 ≫ IE1 when IE1 produces a noble-gas configuration (e.g., Na → Na+).
  • Across a period, ΔegH generally becomes more negative up to halogens; noble gases have positive ΔegH.
  • Oxidation states in p-block: maximum positive oxidation state is often close to the group number (13→+3, 14→+4, 15→+5, 16→+6, 17→+7).
  • Second-period elements show maximum covalency 4 (only 2s,2p available); heavier congeners can exceed 4.

9❌Common Mistakes

  • Saying F has the most negative ΔegH among halogens (it is Cl).
  • Forgetting: cation always smaller and anion always larger than parent atom.
  • Using atomic number trend but ignoring anomalies: Be>B and N>O in IE.
  • Mixing up electron gain enthalpy (kJ mol−1) with electronegativity (relative scale).

11📌Quick Recap

Across a period: radius ↓, ionization enthalpy ↑, electronegativity ↑, electron gain enthalpy (ΔegH) generally becomes more negative (up to halogens), metallic character ↓.Down a group: radius ↑, IE ↓, EN ↓; metallic character ↑; ΔegH often becomes less negative (trend not strictly regular).Covalent radius = ½ × (X–X single bond length); metallic radius = ½ × (nearest-neighbour distance in metal).Cation smaller; anion larger; isoelectronic series: higher nuclear charge Z → smaller radius.IE anomalies to remember: Be > B, N > O.ΔegH anomalies to remember: F less negative than Cl; O less negative than S.Oxides across a period: basic → amphoteric/less basic → acidic (overall increase in non-metallic character).
