Unit 5: Human Physiology (Systems Biology Perspective)

From “parts” to “systems”

Reductionist approach: studying life by breaking it into smaller parts (molecules, reactions, cell-free systems), leading to heavy use of physico-chemical concepts and techniques. Surviving tissue models and cell-free systems were widely used and produced an explosion of knowledge → major growth of molecular biology. Molecular physiology became almost synonymous with biochemistry and biophysics.
⚠ Don’t overcommit to one lens: studying only the whole organism OR only molecules can miss key explanations.
Modern understanding: neither a purely organismic approach nor a purely reductionist molecular approach alone fully explains living phenomena.
Think: knowing every instrument ≠ automatically understanding the “music” — interactions create the full effect.

Systems Biology & Emergence

Systems biology: explains living phenomena as emergent properties produced by interactions among components of a system. Emergent properties: new properties/behaviours that arise from interactions among parts and are not predictable by studying parts in isolation. Emergent properties can arise from regulatory networks spanning:

Biographical Note: Alfonso Corti (1822–1888)

Who he was & what he discovered

Alfonso Corti (Italian anatomist, born 1822) initially studied the cardiovascular systems of reptiles, then focused on the mammalian auditory system. In 1851, he described a structure on the basilar membrane of the cochlea containing hair cells that convert sound vibrations into nerve impulses: the organ of Corti. Died in 1888.
Sound vibrations
→
Hair cells (organ of Corti)
→
Nerve impulses
The organ of Corti is basically the ear’s “translator”: vibration signals → electrical messages for the brain.

Chapter 14: Breathing and Exchange of Gases

Why organisms need breathing (respiration)

O2 is used to indirectly break down simple molecules like glucose, amino acids, fatty acids → to obtain energy for activities. These catabolic reactions release CO2 (harmful) → must be removed.
Breathing (commonly called respiration here) = exchange of O2 from atmosphere with CO2 produced by cells.
O2 supply must be continuous
→
CO2 release must be continuous
Hands on chest = the up–down movement is due to breathing movements changing thoracic volume → driving air flow.

14.1 Respiratory Organs (across animal groups)

Respiratory mechanisms vary mainly based on:
  • Habitat (aquatic vs terrestrial)
  • Level of organisation (simple body plan vs complex organ systems)
Major respiratory structures + the animal groups using them:
Body surface diffusionsponges, coelenterates, flatworms (simple diffusion over entire body surface)
Moist cuticleearthworms (gas exchange through cuticle)
Tracheal tubesinsects (tube network transports atmospheric air within body)
Gillsaquatic arthropods & molluscs; also fishes (branchial respiration)
Lungsterrestrial forms; amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals (pulmonary respiration)
Moist skinfrogs also use skin (cutaneous respiration)
Don’t mix terms:
  • Branchial respiration = gills
  • Pulmonary respiration = lungs
  • Cutaneous respiration = skin (not “lungs through skin”)

Common exam slip: “bronchi are exchange surfaces.” Exchange occurs at alveoli (and their ducts); bronchi/bronchioles mainly conduct air (up to terminal bronchioles).

Change in thoracic cavity volume
→
Change in lung (pulmonary) cavity volume
Essential because we cannot directly alter pulmonary volume; lung volume changes happen via thoracic volume.

Steps of respiration (full sequence)

Respiration steps (in order):
  1. Breathing / pulmonary ventilation: atmospheric air drawn in; CO2-rich alveolar air released out.
  2. Diffusion of gases (O2, CO2) across the alveolar membrane.
  3. Transport of gases by the blood.
  4. Diffusion of O2 and CO2 between blood and tissues.
  5. Utilisation of O2 by cells for catabolic reactions + release of CO2 (cellular respiration).
Exam trap: “breathing = respiration.” Here, breathing is commonly called respiration, but mechanistically respiration includes ventilation + diffusion + transport + tissue diffusion + cellular utilisation.

14.2 Mechanism of Breathing (pressure gradients and muscles)

Two stages of breathing:
Inspirationatmospheric air is drawn in
Expirationalveolar air is released out
Air moves in/out due to a pressure gradient between lungs and atmosphere.
Rule for airflow (pressure gradient):
  • Inspiration when intra-pulmonary pressure < atmospheric pressure (negative pressure relative to atmosphere)
  • Expiration when intra-pulmonary pressure > atmospheric pressure
Muscles generating pressure gradients:
Diaphragmprimary muscle; changes thoracic volume
External intercostalsbetween ribs; aid inspiration
Internal intercostalsbetween ribs; involved in generating gradients (specialised set)
Initiation of inspiration (as stated):
  • Diaphragm contracts → increases thoracic chamber volume in the antero-posterior axis
  • External intercostal muscles contract → lift up ribs → further increases thoracic volume

Mechanism of Breathing (Inspiration & Expiration)

Figure 14.2: Mechanism of breathing showing : (a) inspiration (b) expiration
Breathing (pulmonary ventilation) happens because of pressure differences made by changes in thoracic (chest) volume. When thoracic volume changes, pulmonary (lung) volume changes too → this alters intra‑pulmonary pressure relative to atmospheric pressure → airflow occurs.
Think: Air = “moves down a pressure slope”. Your chest changes size first, then lung pressure changes, then air follows.

Inspiration (breathing in)

Diaphragm contracts
→
Thoracic volume increases
→
Pulmonary volume increases
→

→
Air moves into lungs
  • Diaphragm contraction increases thoracic volume.
  • Inter-costal muscles contract, raising the sternum, increasing thoracic chamber volume in the dorso‑ventral axis.
  • Air enters because intra‑pulmonary pressure becomes less than atmospheric pressure.

Expiration (breathing out)

Diaphragm & inter-costal muscles relax
→
Diaphragm & sternum return to normal position
→
Thoracic & pulmonary volumes decrease
→
Intra‑pulmonary pressure rises slightly above atmospheric
→
Air is expelled
Key exam logic: Air always moves from higher pressure to lower pressure.
  • Inspiration: intra‑pulmonary pressure < atmospheric
  • Expiration: intra‑pulmonary pressure > atmospheric (even if only slightly)

Forceful breathing & breathing rate

  • Force of inspiration/expiration can be increased using additional abdominal muscles.
  • Normal breathing rate: about 12–16 breaths/minute in a healthy human (average).

Spirometer (clinical estimation of air volumes)

A spirometer estimates the volume of air involved in breathing movements and helps in clinical assessment of pulmonary functions.
Do not confuse thoracic volume change (chest cavity) with pulmonary volume change (lungs). Thoracic change drives pulmonary change, which then changes intra‑pulmonary pressure.

Respiratory Volumes and Capacities

Respiratory volumes are basic measurable air volumes. By adding specific volumes, we obtain pulmonary capacities used in clinical diagnosis.

Respiratory Volumes (basic components)

Tidal Volume (TV)Air inspired/expired in normal respiration; ~500 mL
Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV)Extra air inspired by forcible inspiration; ~2500–3000 mL
Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV)Extra air expired by forcible expiration; ~1000–1100 mL
Residual Volume (RV)Air remaining after forcible expiration; ~1100–1200 mL
Minute air moved in quiet breathing: TV ≈ 500 mL. With ~12–16 breaths/min, a healthy man can inspire/expire about 6000–8000 mL of air per minute.

Pulmonary Capacities (derived by adding volumes)

Inspiratory Capacity (IC) = TV + IRV Total air a person can inspire after a normal expiration.
Expiratory Capacity (EC) = TV + ERV Total air a person can expire after a normal inspiration.
Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) = ERV + RV Air remaining in lungs after a normal expiration.
Vital Capacity (VC) = ERV + TV + IRV Maximum air that can be breathed in after a forced expiration, OR maximum air that can be breathed out after a forced inspiration.
Total Lung Capacity (TLC) = RV + ERV + TV + IRV = VC + RV Total air accommodated in lungs at the end of a forced inspiration.
Exam trap: VC does NOT include RV. TLC includes RV (because lungs never completely empty).

Exchange of Gases

Figure 14.3: Diagrammatic representation of exchange of gases at the alveolus and the body tissues with blood and transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide
Alveoli are the primary sites of gas exchange. Gas exchange also occurs between blood and tissues. O2 and CO2 move by simple diffusion, mainly due to a pressure/concentration gradient.

Factors affecting diffusion of O2 and CO2

  • Pressure/concentration gradient (major driver)
  • Solubility of gases
  • Thickness of the diffusion membranes

Partial pressure (pO2, pCO2)

Partial pressure = pressure contributed by an individual gas in a mixture of gases.
  • Oxygen: pO2
  • Carbon dioxide: pCO2
The values below establish the diffusion gradients across alveoli ↔ blood and blood ↔ tissues.
  • Atmospheric air: pO2 = 159 mm Hg; pCO2 = 0.3 mm Hg
  • Alveoli: pO2 = 104 mm Hg; pCO2 = 40 mm Hg
  • Blood (deoxygenated): pO2 = 40 mm Hg; pCO2 = 45 mm Hg
  • Blood (oxygenated): pO2 = 95 mm Hg; pCO2 = 40 mm Hg
  • Tissues: pO2 = 40 mm Hg; pCO2 = 45 mm Hg

Blood (40)

Tissues (40)
CO2: Tissues (45)
→
Blood (45→40)
→
Alveoli (40)

Why CO2 diffuses efficiently despite smaller gradient

CO2 solubility is 20–25 times higher than O2. So, for the same partial pressure difference, more CO2 diffuses across the membrane than O2.

Diffusion membrane (structure and thickness)

Figure 14.4: A Diagram of a section of an alveolus with a pulmonary capillary.
The diffusion membrane (alveolar air ↔ blood) has three major layers:
Alveolar epitheliumThin squamous epithelium of alveoli
Capillary endotheliumEndothelium of alveolar capillaries
Basement substanceThin basement membrane supporting alveolar epithelium + basement membrane around endothelial cells
Total thickness is much less than a millimetre → diffusion is highly favourable for:
  • O2: alveoli → tissues
  • CO2: tissues → alveoli
Always state diffusion directions with both steps:
  • O2: alveoli → blood → tissues
  • CO2: tissues → blood → alveoli

Transport of Gases

Blood transports O2 and CO2. Transport occurs via RBCs and plasma in specific proportions.

Overall transport proportions (must-know values)

O2 via RBCs~97% transported by RBCs (as Hb-bound)
O2 via plasma~3% dissolved in plasma
CO2 via RBCs~20–25% transported by RBCs
CO2 as bicarbonate~70% carried as bicarbonate
CO2 dissolved~7% dissolved in plasma

Transport of Oxygen

Haemoglobin (Hb) is a red, iron-containing pigment in RBCs. O2 binds reversibly with Hb to form oxyhaemoglobin.
  • Carrying capacity: each Hb molecule can carry a maximum of 4 O2 molecules.
  • Hb–O2 binding is primarily related to pO2.
  • Other factors that interfere with binding: pCO2, H+ concentration, and temperature.
Think “Hb is a reversible taxi”: it loads O2 where pO2 is high (lungs) and drops it where pO2 is low (tissues).

Oxygen dissociation curve (sigmoid curve)

Figure 14.5: Oxygen dissociation curve
Plotting % saturation of Hb with O2 against pO2 gives a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve called the oxygen dissociation curve. It is used to study how factors like pCO2 and H+ affect Hb–O2 binding.
Alveoli (favour binding/formation of oxyhaemoglobin): high pO2, low pCO2, lower H+ concentration, lower temperature. Tissues (favour dissociation/release of O2): low pO2, high pCO2, high H+ concentration, higher temperature.

Under normal physiological conditions, every 100 mL of oxygenated blood can deliver about 5 mL of O2 to tissues.

Transport of Carbon dioxide

CO2 is transported in three forms: (i) dissolved in plasma, (ii) as bicarbonate (major share), and (iii) bound to haemoglobin as carbamino‑haemoglobin (about 20–25%).
  • CO2 binding to Hb (carbamino‑haemoglobin) is related to pCO2.
  • pO2 is a major factor affecting this binding.
  • Tissues: high pCO2 + low pO2 → more CO2 binding.
  • Alveoli: low pCO2 + high pO2 → CO2 dissociation.
Write the condition pairs exactly in exams: tissues = high pCO2, low pO2 (binding); alveoli = low pCO2, high pO2 (dissociation).

CO2 Transport: Release at Alveoli & Role of Carbonic Anhydrase

Carbamino-haemoglobin = CO2 bound to haemoglobin (formed mainly in tissues; CO2 is delivered/unloaded at alveoli). Carbonic anhydrase (CA) = enzyme present in very high concentration in RBCs; minute quantities also occur in plasma. It catalyses the interconversion between CO2 and bicarbonate, in both directions.
Carbonic anhydrase reaction (reversible): CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 (catalysed by carbonic anhydrase) H2CO3 ⇌ HCO3− + H+

At Tissue Site (High pCO2)

Because catabolism produces CO2, partial pressure of CO2 (pCO2) is high in tissues.
High tissue pCO2
→
CO2 diffuses into blood (plasma + RBC)
→
CA drives formation of HCO3− + H+
  • Net effect: CO2 becomes “trapped” largely as bicarbonate (HCO3−) for transport in blood.

At Alveolar Site (Low pCO2)

At the alveoli, pCO2 is low, so the same reversible reactions proceed in the opposite direction.
Low alveolar pCO2
→
HCO3− + H+ recombine
→
H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O
→
CO2 diffuses into alveoli and is exhaled
Key transport idea: CO2 produced at tissues is converted mainly to bicarbonate for transport; at alveoli it is reconverted to CO2 and eliminated.
Quantitative fact (exam use): Every 100 mL of deoxygenated blood delivers approximately 4 mL of CO2 to the alveoli.
Do not state carbonic anhydrase is only in plasma: it is in very high concentration in RBCs and only minute amounts in plasma.

Regulation of Respiration

Humans can maintain and modify respiratory rhythm according to tissue demands primarily through a neural system.

Respiratory Rhythm Centre (Medulla)

Respiratory rhythm centre = specialised centre in the medulla that is primarily responsible for regulation of breathing rhythm.

Pneumotaxic Centre (Pons)

Pneumotaxic centre = centre in the pons that can moderate the function of the respiratory rhythm centre.
Effect: Neural signals from the pneumotaxic centre can reduce the duration of inspiration and thereby alter respiratory rate.

Chemosensitive Area (Adjacent to Rhythm Centre)

A chemosensitive area located adjacent to the rhythm centre is highly sensitive to:
  • CO2
  • H+ (hydrogen ions)
↑ CO2 and/or ↑ H+
→
Chemosensitive area activated
→
Signals rhythm centre
→
Breathing adjusted to eliminate CO2/H+

Peripheral Receptors: Aortic Arch & Carotid Artery

Receptors in the aortic arch and carotid artery can detect changes in:
  • CO2 concentration
  • H+ concentration
They send necessary signals to the rhythm centre for remedial actions.
Role of oxygen: Oxygen has a quite insignificant role in the regulation of respiratory rhythm (compared with CO2/H+).
Exam pitfall: writing “breathing is regulated mainly by O2 levels.” In humans, regulation is dominated by CO2 and H+.

Disorders of Respiratory System

Asthma

Asthma = difficulty in breathing causing wheezing due to inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles.
⚠ Don’t mix it up: asthma is mainly about inflamed/narrowed airways (bronchi/bronchioles), not damaged alveoli.
Think of the airways like a “squeezed” straw → air struggles to move → you hear wheezing.

Emphysema

Emphysema = chronic disorder where alveolar walls are damaged, causing a decrease in respiratory surface area.
Major cause: cigarette smoking.
Imagine popping the tiny “bubble walls” of alveoli → fewer separate bubbles = less surface area for gas exchange.

Occupational Respiratory Disorders

In some industries (especially grinding or stone-breaking), heavy dust production may overwhelm the body’s defence mechanisms.
Long-term dust exposure
→
Inflammation
→
Fibrosis (proliferation of fibrous tissue)
→
Serious lung damage
Prevention: workers should wear protective masks.
⚠ Key idea: it’s the long-term exposure that drives fibrosis and lasting damage.

Chapter Wrap-Up: What You Must Be Able to State (from the Summary)

Overall Purpose & Steps in Respiration

Cells use O2 for metabolism to release energy and produce CO2 (harmful if accumulated). Animals evolved mechanisms for:
  • Transport of O2 to cells
  • Removal of CO2 from cells
Humans have a well-developed respiratory system: two lungs + associated air passages.

Alveoli ↔ Blood exchangeO2 into blood; CO2 into alveoli
Transport by bloodGases carried throughout body
Blood ↔ Tissues exchangeO2 to tissues; CO2 to blood
Cellular respirationCells utilise O2

Mechanism of Inspiration/Expiration & Spirometry

Inspiration and expiration occur by creating pressure gradients between atmosphere and alveoli using specialised muscles:
  • Intercostal muscles
  • Diaphragm
Air volumes can be measured using a spirometer; these measurements are of clinical significance.
Pressure gradient = “air moves from higher pressure to lower pressure” (like air rushing out of a squeezed balloon).

Diffusion of Gases: Determinants

Exchange of O2 and CO2 at alveoli and tissues occurs by diffusion. Rate of diffusion depends on:
  • Partial pressure gradients of O2 (pO2) and CO2 (pCO2)
  • Solubility of the gases
  • Thickness of the diffusion surface
These factors favour diffusion of:
  • O2: alveoli → deoxygenated blood; oxygenated blood → tissues
  • CO2: tissues → alveoli (opposite direction)
⚠ Common slip: the direction is set by partial pressure gradients, not “where there is more oxygen in general.”

Transport of O2

Oxygen is transported mainly as oxyhaemoglobin.
  • At alveoli (high pO2): O2 binds haemoglobin.
  • At tissues (low pO2, high pCO2 and high H+): O2 dissociates from haemoglobin.
