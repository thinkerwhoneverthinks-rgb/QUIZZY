Melvin Calvin and the Pathway of Carbon Assimilation

Melvin Calvin (born Minnesota, April 1911) earned a Ph.D. in Chemistry from the University of Minnesota and served as Professor of Chemistry at the University of California, Berkeley.
Historical context: After World War II (post Hiroshima–Nagasaki), concern about harmful effects of radioactivity led Calvin and co-workers to use radioactivity for beneficial scientific research.
Key experimental strategy: Calvin (with J.A. Bassham) traced photosynthetic reactions that form sugars and other substances from CO2, water, and minerals by labelling CO2 with 14C. This enabled mapping the pathway of carbon assimilation in photosynthesis.

→

→

→
Map carbon assimilation pathway
Calvin’s proposal (principle): Plants convert light energy → chemical energy by transferring an electron in an organised array of pigment molecules and other substances.
Nobel Prize: Awarded to Calvin in 1961 for mapping the pathway of carbon assimilation (the Calvin pathway/C3 cycle). Current relevance: Principles established are used in research on renewable resources for energy/materials and solar energy research.

11.1 What Do We Know? (Prior Knowledge + Simple Evidence)

From earlier experiments, we know that chlorophyll (green pigment of leaf), light, and CO2 are required for photosynthesis.

Experiment 1: Variegated leaf / Partially covered leaf (Evidence for chlorophyll + light requirement)

Setup idea (as commonly done in earlier classes):
  • Use a variegated leaf (green and non-green areas), or cover part of a leaf with black paper to block light.
  • Expose to light for some time.
  • Test the leaf for starch (product stored after photosynthesis).
Leaf with green &/or uncovered parts
→
Expose to light
→
Test for starch
→
Starch only where green + light present
Conclusion (reasoning): Photosynthesis occurs only in the green parts (chlorophyll-containing tissues) and only where light reaches. Therefore:
  • Chlorophyll is necessary (non-green areas do not form starch)
  • Light is necessary (covered regions do not form starch)
Exam tip: When asked “what does the starch test show?”, state the logic: starch presence indicates photosynthetic carbohydrate formation in that region under given conditions.

Experiment 2: KOH in test tube (Evidence for CO2 requirement)

Setup:
  • Enclose one part of a leaf in a test tube containing KOH-soaked cotton.
  • The other half remains exposed to normal air.
  • Place the setup in light for some time.
  • Test both parts later for starch.
Role of KOH: KOH absorbs CO2 from the air inside the tube, creating a CO2-deficient environment around the enclosed part of the leaf.
Observation: Exposed part tests positive for starch; enclosed part (with KOH) tests negative. Conclusion (how it is drawn): The key variable changed is availability of CO2. Since only the part receiving CO2 forms starch, CO2 is required for photosynthesis.
Common mistake: Writing “KOH provides CO2” is incorrect. KOH removes CO2 by absorption.

Priestley’s question: How do air and living/burning processes affect each other?

Historical milestones & overall concept of photosynthesis

Julius von Sachs (c. 1854): evidence for glucose formation and storage

Sachs provided evidence that when plants grow they produce glucose, and that glucose is usually stored as starch.
He also showed that the green substance (chlorophyll) is located in special bodies inside plant cells (later named chloroplasts), and that green parts of plants are the site of glucose formation.

T.W. Engelmann: action spectrum and link to chlorophyll absorption

Engelmann illuminated a green alga (Cladophora) with different colours of light and used aerobic bacteria to detect where O2 was produced (because these bacteria gather where oxygen is highest).
White light
→
Prism splits into spectrum
→
Different wavelengths fall on alga
→
Bacteria accumulate where O2 evolves most
  • Observation: Bacteria accumulated mainly in blue and red regions.
  • Inference: Maximum photosynthesis (O2 evolution) occurs under blue and red light.
  • Outcome: This produced an early action spectrum of photosynthesis, which resembles the absorption spectra of chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b.
Absorption spectrum = wavelengths a pigment absorbs strongly. Action spectrum = wavelengths most effective in driving photosynthesis (measured via O2 evolution/CO2 fixation). They are related but not perfectly identical.

Key features known by mid-19th century

  • Plants use light energy.
  • They make carbohydrates from CO2 and H2O.
  • In oxygen-evolving organisms, O2 is released.

Empirical equation (general carbohydrate unit)

CO2 + H2O ⟶ [CH2O] + O2 (in presence of Light)
[CH2O] denotes a generic carbohydrate unit (e.g., represents carbohydrate composition; glucose is a six-carbon sugar built from such units).

Cornelius van Niel: generalized photosynthesis and source of oxygen

Van Niel (based on studies of purple and green bacteria) proposed that photosynthesis is essentially a light-dependent redox process: hydrogen from a suitable oxidisable compound reduces CO2 to carbohydrate.
CO2 + 2H2A ⟶ [CH2O] + H2O + 2A (in presence of Light)
  • H2A = hydrogen donor (electron donor).
  • A = oxidised product after donation of hydrogen/electrons.
In green plants: H2O is the hydrogen donor and is oxidised to O2. In some bacteria: photosynthesis occurs without releasing oxygen (anoxygenic photosynthesis).
Green plantsH2O donor; oxidation product = O2
Purple/green sulphur bacteriaH2S donor; oxidation product = sulphur/sulphate (not O2)
Key inferenceO2 in plants comes from H2O, not CO2
Van Niel’s inference that oxygen evolved by green plants comes from water (not CO2) was later confirmed using radioisotopic techniques.

Overall balanced equation for photosynthesis (glucose as product)

6CO2 + 12H2O ⟶ C6H12O6 + 6H2O + 6O2 (in presence of Light)
  • This equation emphasises: (i) glucose (C6H12O6) is formed,
  • (ii) O2 released originates from H2O (proved by radioisotopes), and
  • (iii) photosynthesis is multistep, not a single reaction.
Exam trap: Do not state “oxygen comes from CO2”. Correct: oxygen released comes from water.
Analogy: Think of H2O as the “electron wallet” that pays electrons to reduce CO2 into sugar; the “spent” remainder becomes O2.

Where does photosynthesis take place?

Sites in the plant

Photosynthesis occurs in green leaves and also in other green parts of the plant (i.e., any chlorophyll-containing tissues).
Figure 11.2: Diagrammatic representation of an electron micrograph of a section of chloroplast
Leaf mesophyll cells contain a large number of chloroplasts. Chloroplasts often align along mesophyll cell walls so as to receive an optimum quantity of incident light.
The text highlights that chloroplast orientation can change with light conditions: sometimes their flat surfaces align parallel to the wall, sometimes they become perpendicular to incident light—interpret this as a light-optimising/photoprotective adjustment conceptually.

Chloroplast structure relevant to photosynthesis (division of labour)

Within a chloroplast is a membranous system and a fluid matrix, showing a clear division of labour between light capture/energy conversion and sugar synthesis.
GranaStacks/regions of thylakoid membranes (part of membranous system)
Stroma lamellaeMembrane connections between grana
StromaMatrix where enzymatic sugar-synthesis reactions occur
  • Membrane system (grana + lamellae): traps light energy; synthesises ATP and NADPH.
  • Stroma: enzymatic reactions synthesise sugar, which can form starch.

Light reactions vs dark reactions (carbon reactions)

Light reactions (photochemical reactions): directly driven by light; occur in the membrane system; produce ATP and NADPH while capturing light energy. Dark reactions (carbon reactions): not directly driven by light, but depend on ATP and NADPH made in light reactions; occur in the stroma; synthesize sugars.
Common misconception: “Dark reactions happen only in darkness.” Incorrect. They are called “dark” only because they are not directly light-driven; they remain functionally light-dependent via ATP and NADPH.

The most abundant plant pigment (hinted in the text) is chlorophyll a, which is also the chief pigment directly associated with photosynthesis.

Accessory Pigments & Action Spectrum Link

Key idea from the graphs (action spectrum + absorption spectrum): Most photosynthesis occurs in the blue and red regions of visible light, though some occurs at other visible wavelengths.
Accessory pigments are thylakoid pigments other than chlorophyll a (e.g., chlorophyll b, xanthophylls, carotenoids) that:
  • Absorb light and transfer absorbed energy to chlorophyll a
  • Broaden the usable wavelength range of incoming light (increase photosynthetic efficiency)
  • Protect chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation
Analogy: Chlorophyll a is the “main worker” at the reaction centre, while accessory pigments are “extra antenna panels” that capture more kinds of light and pass the energy inward—also acting like “sunscreen” to prevent damage.

11.5 What is Light Reaction (Photochemical Phase)?

Light reactions / photochemical phase include:
  • Light absorption
  • Water splitting (photolysis)
  • Oxygen release
  • Formation of high-energy intermediates: ATP and NADPH
Several protein complexes participate in these steps.

Photosystems and Light Harvesting Complex (LHC)

Pigments are organised into two discrete photochemical light-harvesting complexes (LHC) within:
Photosystem II (PS II)Discovered second? No—named by discovery sequence; functions first in Z-scheme
Photosystem I (PS I)Discovered first; functions after PS II in non-cyclic flow
Important naming note: PS I and PS II are named in the sequence of their discovery, not the sequence in which they function during the light reaction.
LHC (antenna complex): Made of hundreds of pigment molecules bound to proteins. The pigments (other than one special chlorophyll a) form the antenna, absorbing different wavelengths and funnelling energy to the reaction centre.
Figure 11.4: The light harvesting complex
Reaction centre: A single chlorophyll a molecule in each photosystem that performs photochemistry (primary charge separation). Reaction centres differ between PS I and PS II.
Reaction-centre chlorophyll a types:
P700PS I reaction centre; absorption peak at 700 nm
P680PS II reaction centre; absorption maxima at 680 nm
Exam trap: “All pigments form antenna except one chlorophyll a” → that one is the reaction centre. Also, P700 = PS I and P680 = PS II.
Figure 11.5: Z scheme of light reaction

Electron flow from PS II to PS I to NADP+

In PS II: Reaction centre chlorophyll a (P680) absorbs 680 nm red light → electrons get excited (jump to higher energy level, farther from nucleus) → captured by a primary electron acceptor → passed to an electron transport system (ETS) containing cytochromes.
“Downhill” movement: As electrons move along ETS, movement is downhill on the redox potential scale (energetically favourable transfer through carriers). Electrons are not used up; they are passed on through carriers and ultimately delivered to pigments of PS I.
In PS I: Reaction centre chlorophyll a (P700) absorbs 700 nm red light → electrons become excited and are transferred to an acceptor with greater redox potential → then electrons move downhill again to NADP+.
Reduction of NADP+: Addition of electrons reduces NADP+ to NADPH + H+. Exam wording: “NADP+ is reduced to NADPH + H+.”

Z-scheme (why it is called so)

Z scheme: The entire electron transfer path—starting at PS II (excitation to acceptor), down ETS to PS I, re-excitation, transfer to another acceptor, and finally downhill to NADP+—is called the Z scheme because of its characteristic Z-shaped appearance when carriers are arranged on a redox potential scale.
PS II (P680) excited e−
→
ETS (cytochromes; downhill)
→
PS I (P700) excited e−
→
NADP+ reduced to NADPH + H+

11.6.2 Cyclic and Non-cyclic Photophosphorylation

ATP as energy currency: Organisms extract energy from oxidisable substances and store it as bond energy. ATP carries this energy in its chemical bonds.
ATP = the “charged battery” of the cell (energy stored in bonds, ready to be used).
Phosphorylation: The process through which ATP is synthesised by cells (in mitochondria and chloroplasts).
Photophosphorylation (definition + equation form): Synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate (iP) in the presence of light. ADP + iP ⟶ ATP (in light)

Non-cyclic photophosphorylation

Non-cyclic photophosphorylation: Occurs when both photosystems work in series—first PS II, then PS I—connected by an electron transport chain (the Z scheme).
PS II

Products of non-cyclic flow: Both ATP and NADPH + H+ are synthesised.

Cyclic photophosphorylation

Cyclic photophosphorylation: Occurs when only PS I is functional. The excited electron is circulated within the photosystem and returns back to the PS I complex via the electron transport chain (does not reduce NADP+).

11.6.3 Chemiosmotic Hypothesis (ATP Synthesis in Chloroplast)

Chemiosmotic hypothesis: Explains ATP synthesis in chloroplasts by linking it to a proton (H+) gradient across a membrane. As in respiration, ATP synthesis is linked to development of a proton gradient across a membrane; here the membrane is the thylakoid membrane.
Key difference from mitochondria:
  • Photosynthesis: proton accumulation is towards the inside of thylakoid membrane = lumen
  • Respiration: protons accumulate in the intermembrane space when electrons move through the ETS

How the proton gradient is generated (three causes)

Proton gradient across thylakoid membrane develops due to processes during electron activation + transport:
  1. Water splitting on inner side: Photolysis occurs on the inner side of thylakoid membrane → H+ produced accumulates in the lumen.
  2. Proton translocation coupled to electron transport: As electrons move, protons are transported across the membrane because the primary electron acceptor (towards outer side) passes the electron to an H carrier (not directly to an electron carrier). This H carrier:
    • removes a proton from stroma while carrying an electron
    • releases the proton into the lumen when it transfers the electron to an electron carrier on the inner side
  3. NADP+ reduction consumes stromal protons: NADP reductase is located on the stroma side. Reduction of NADP+ to NADPH + H+ requires protons, which are removed from the stroma.
Net result inside chloroplast:
  • Stroma: protons decrease
  • Lumen: protons accumulate
  • Forms a proton gradient across thylakoid membrane + causes a measurable decrease in lumen pH
Figure 11.7: ATP synthesis through chemiosmosis

How the gradient drives ATP synthesis

Why the proton gradient matters: Breakdown of the gradient leads to ATP synthesis. How it breaks down: Protons move back across the thylakoid membrane to the stroma through a transmembrane channel (ATP synthase complex implied by the figure/text continuation).
H⁺ builds up in lumen
→
H⁺ flows through membrane channel
→
ATP synthesis in stroma
Must state direction correctly in exams: H+ accumulates in the lumen (inside thylakoid), then flows back to the stroma through a membrane channel to drive ATP synthesis.

Chemiosmosis & ATP Synthase (in chloroplast thylakoids)

ATP synthase (chloroplast) is a membrane enzyme complex that synthesises ATP using the energy stored in a proton (H+) gradient across the thylakoid membrane.

What chemiosmosis requires (chloroplast context)

Chemiosmosis requires four essentials: a membrane, a proton pump, a proton gradient, and ATP synthase.
  • Membrane: thylakoid membrane (separates stroma from thylakoid lumen).
  • Proton pump: uses energy to pump H+ across the membrane.
  • Proton gradient: high concentration of protons in the thylakoid lumen.
  • ATP synthase: provides a channel for H+ diffusion back across membrane; energy released activates ATP synthase to catalyse ATP formation.
Analogy: The thylakoid membrane is like a dam wall; H+ are water stored behind it (in the lumen). ATP synthase is the turbine: when H+ flow back, energy is captured to “generate” ATP.
Link to biosynthesis: ATP formed by chemiosmosis and NADPH produced by electron movement are used immediately in reactions in the stroma that fix CO2 and synthesise sugars.
Exam tip: State the direction + location clearly: H+ are pumped to build a high concentration in the thylakoid lumen, then diffuse back through ATP synthase toward the stroma, driving ATP synthesis at CF1.

Where are ATP and NADPH used? (Biosynthetic phase / “dark reaction” issue)

Products of the light reaction: ATP, NADPH, and O2.
  • O2 diffuses out of the chloroplast.
  • ATP and NADPH are used to drive the biosynthetic phase that produces sugars (food).
The biosynthetic phase does not directly depend on light, but it does depend on:
  • ATP (from light reaction)
  • NADPH (from light reaction)
  • CO2 and H2O
Evidence that it is not truly “dark”: Immediately after light becomes unavailable, biosynthesis continues for some time (using stored ATP/NADPH), then stops. When light is restored, synthesis starts again.
Common exam trap: Calling it “dark reaction” can mislead. It is light-independent (doesn’t require light directly) but indirectly light-dependent because it needs ATP and NADPH from light reactions.
Overall idea of biosynthesis (as presented): CO2 is combined with H2O to produce carbohydrates: (CH2O)n (sugars).

Discovery of first CO2-fixation product & pathway types (C3 vs C4)

Calvin’s 14C experiments and the Calvin cycle

Scientists aimed to identify the first product formed when CO2 is fixed. After World War II, Melvin Calvin used radioactive 14C in algal photosynthesis studies and found:
First identified CO2 fixation product (in Calvin’s work): a 3-carbon organic acid called 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA).

Two main types of CO2 assimilation: C3 and C4 pathways

Further experiments across many plants showed that not all plants first form PGA. A second group forms a different first stable product:
C3 pathwayFirst stable CO2-fixation product is a C3 acid: 3-PGA (PGA)
C4 pathwayFirst stable CO2-fixation product is a C4 acid: oxaloacetic acid (OAA)
OAA (oxaloacetic acid) is a 4-carbon organic acid identified as the first stable CO2-fixation product in C4 plants.
These groups (C3 vs C4) also show other associated characteristics (to be discussed later in the chapter).

The Primary Acceptor of CO2 (Calvin cycle)

Scientists asked: If the first stable product is a 3-carbon compound (PGA), then what size must the CO2 acceptor be?
Primary acceptor of CO2 in the Calvin cycle: a 5-carbon ketose sugar called ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP).
Conceptual pitfall (historical and exam-relevant reasoning): Many scientists assumed the acceptor would be a 2-carbon compound (because adding CO2 might “seem” to make 3C PGA). They searched for years before discovering the true acceptor was 5-carbon RuBP.

The Calvin Cycle (occurrence and stages)

Figure 11.8: Sucrose, starch
Universality: The Calvin pathway occurs in all photosynthetic plants—it operates whether a plant is C3 or C4 (or other). (C4 plants still run the Calvin cycle; they additionally have CO2-concentrating steps.)
Calvin and co-workers showed the pathway operates in a cyclic manner because RuBP is regenerated.

Three stages of the Calvin cycle

1. Carboxylation
→
2. Reduction
→
3. Regeneration

1) Carboxylation

Carboxylation: fixation of CO2 into a stable organic intermediate. It is the most crucial step where CO2 is utilised for carboxylation of RuBP.
CO2 + RuBP
→
(RuBP carboxylase)
→
2 × 3-PGA
The enzyme also shows oxygenation activity; hence it is more accurately called RuBP carboxylase-oxygenase (RuBisCO).

2) Reduction

Reduction stage: a series of reactions leading to formation of glucose (carbohydrate) using ATP and NADPH produced in the light reactions.
Energy requirement per CO2 fixed (reduction stage):
  • 2 ATP for phosphorylation
  • 2 NADPH for reduction
Stoichiometry for glucose formation: Fixation of 6 CO2 molecules and 6 turns of the cycle are required to form 1 glucose molecule.

3) Regeneration

Regeneration: re-formation of the CO2 acceptor molecule RuBP, essential for the cycle to continue uninterrupted.
ATP requirement (regeneration stage): regeneration steps require 1 ATP for phosphorylation to form RuBP.
Exam precision: Don’t just list stages—state what happens and what is consumed: carboxylation makes two 3-PGA; reduction uses ATP + NADPH; regeneration uses ATP to reform RuBP.

Calvin Cycle: Energy Requirement & Overall Balance

Energy cost per CO2 entering the Calvin cycle:
For every 1 CO2 fixed, the Calvin cycle requires 3 ATP and 2 NADPH.
Why cyclic photophosphorylation occurs:
Cyclic phosphorylation is believed to occur to compensate for the ATP:NADPH demand mismatch between the light reactions and the Calvin cycle (dark reaction).
➡ The Calvin cycle needs proportionately more ATP than NADPH.
For glucose synthesis via Calvin pathway:
  • 1 glucose requires 6 turns of the Calvin cycle (= fixation of 6 CO2)
ATP required = 6 × 3 = 18 ATP
NADPH required = 6 × 2 = 12 NADPH
Exam trap: Do not write “1 turn makes 1 glucose.”
The text explicitly states: 6 turns → 1 glucose.
Calvin cycle input–output summary (for 1 glucose):
IN: 6 CO2 + 18 ATP + 12 NADPH
→
OUT: 1 glucose + 18 ADP + 12 NADP
  • Interpretation: CO2 is reduced to carbohydrate using energy (ATP) and reducing power (NADPH); ATP becomes ADP and NADPH becomes NADP.

The C4 Pathway (Hatch and Slack Pathway)

Core idea:
C4 plants form a 4-carbon compound (oxaloacetic acid, OAA) as the first CO2-fixation product, but they still use the C3 pathway (Calvin cycle) as the main biosynthetic pathway for sugar formation.

How C4 plants differ from C3 plants (features)

C4 plants are adapted to dry tropical regions and show specific functional/anatomical specialisations.
Special leaf anatomy“Kranz” anatomy with prominent bundle sheath
Higher temperature toleranceperform well at higher temperatures
High-light responseshow response to high light intensities
No photorespirationphotorespiration absent due to CO2 concentrating mechanism

Kranz anatomy (bundle sheath specialisation)

Bundle sheath cells: Particularly large cells surrounding the vascular bundles in C4 leaves. Kranz anatomy: Leaf anatomy in which bundle sheath cells are arranged like a wreath around vascular bundles (Kranz = “wreath”).
  • Bundle sheath may form several layers around vascular bundles.
  • Cells have a large number of chloroplasts.
  • They have thick walls impervious to gaseous exchange.
  • No intercellular spaces (helps limit gas diffusion/leakage).
Observation-based identification:
Cut a vertical section of a leaf and look for a prominent bundle sheath “wreath” around vascular bundles → helps identify many C4 plants (e.g., maize, sorghum).
Do not confuse: “Bundle sheath present” is especially prominent/diagnostic in C4 leaves (Kranz).
Exam focus = the distinctive structure and role in C4 photosynthesis.

Steps of the Hatch and Slack Pathway (C4 cycle)

The Hatch and Slack pathway is a cyclic process involving coordinated roles of mesophyll cells and bundle sheath cells.

Photorespiration

First step of Calvin cycle (carboxylation by RuBisCO)

Normal carboxylation reaction: RuBP combines with CO2 to form two molecules of 3PGA, catalysed by RuBisCO.
RuBisCO carboxylase reaction: RuBP + CO2 —(RuBisCO)→ 2 × 3PGA
RuBisCO = most abundant enzyme in the world + unusual active site binds both CO2 and O2 (“carboxylase-oxygenase”). Binding is competitive and depends on relative concentrations of CO2 and O2.
  • Competitive binding: CO2 and O2 compete for RuBisCO active site.
  • Affinity note from text: RuBisCO has a much greater affinity for CO2 when the CO2:O2 is nearly equal.
  • Key determinant: relative concentration of CO2 vs O2 decides which binds.

What is photorespiration and what does it produce?

Photorespiration: In C3 plants, some O2 binds RuBisCO, decreasing CO2 fixation. RuBP then reacts with O2 producing one phosphoglycerate and one phosphoglycolate (2-carbon). This route is called the photorespiratory pathway.
O2 binds RuBisCO (in C3)
→
Less CO2 fixation
→
RuBP + O2
→
Phosphoglycerate + Phosphoglycolate (2C)
  • Consequence: CO2 fixation decreases because RuBP is diverted away from making 2 × 3PGA.
  • Products when O2 binds: Phosphoglycerate + Phosphoglycolate (2C).
  • In photorespiration there is no synthesis of sugars.
  • There is no synthesis of ATP.
  • There is no synthesis of NADPH.
  • It results in release of CO2 with utilisation of ATP (energy loss).
  • Biological function of photorespiration is not known yet (as per text).
Exam wording: Photorespiration consumes ATP and releases CO2 but does not produce ATP/NADPH/sugars.

Why photorespiration is absent in C4 plants

Reason: C4 plants have a CO2-concentrating mechanism that increases CO2 concentration at the RuBisCO site in bundle sheath cells.
C4 acids move to bundle sheath
→
Decarboxylation releases CO2
→
High intracellular CO2 near RuBisCO
→
RuBisCO mostly carboxylase (oxygenase minimized)
Outcomes in C4 plants: Lack of photorespiration explains better productivity/yields and contributes to higher temperature tolerance.

Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

Rate of photosynthesis determines plant productivity and hence yield (including crop yield). It is controlled by internal (plant) and external (environmental) factors that act simultaneously.

Internal factors depend on genetic predisposition and the growth of the plant, so different species/varieties can show different photosynthetic capacities even under the same environment.

Blackman’s (1905) Law of Limiting Factors

Statement: If a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, its rate is determined by the factor that is nearest to its minimal value; i.e., the factor that directly affects the process if its quantity is changed.
Exam meaning: When many factors act together, usually one factor is limiting at a time. The observed rate at any moment is controlled by the factor present at sub-optimal level.
  • Illustrative example (given): Even with a green leaf and optimal light and CO₂, photosynthesis may not occur if temperature is very low. Raising temperature to optimal restores photosynthesis → therefore temperature was the limiting factor.
Common exam trap: Don’t claim “light is always limiting” or “CO₂ is always limiting.” Limitation depends on which factor is closest to its minimum under the given conditions (Blackman’s law).

Light as a Factor (11.10.1)

Figure 11.10: Light intensity
While discussing light, distinguish among:
  • Light quality (wavelength/colour)
  • Light intensity (amount of light)
  • Duration of exposure (photoperiod/length of illumination)

Effect of Light Intensity on CO₂ Fixation (Figure 11.10)

  • At low light intensity, there is a linear relationship between incident light and CO₂ fixation rate.
  • At higher light intensity, the rate plateaus because other factors become limiting.
  • Light saturation occurs at about 10% of full sunlight → in nature, light is rarely limiting except in shade or dense forests.
  • Beyond an optimum point, very high light can cause breakdown of chlorophyll → decreased photosynthesis.
Analogy: Increasing light is like opening a water tap into a pipe: initially flow rises, but soon the narrowest part (CO₂ supply/enzymes/temperature) limits flow; forcing more can “damage the system” (chlorophyll breakdown).
Exam tip: “Light saturation at ~10% sunlight” is a favourite point—use it to justify why CO₂/temperature/water often limit photosynthesis more than light in open habitats.

Carbon Dioxide Concentration (11.10.2)

CO₂ is the major limiting factor for photosynthesis under many natural conditions because atmospheric CO₂ is low.
  • Atmospheric CO₂ is only about 0.03–0.04%.
  • Increasing CO₂ up to about 0.05% increases CO₂ fixation rate.
  • Beyond this, high CO₂ may become damaging over longer periods.

Differential Response of C3 vs C4 Plants to CO₂

  • At low light, neither C3 nor C4 shows much response to high CO₂ (because light is limiting).
  • At high light, both C3 and C4 show increased photosynthesis with increased CO₂.
  • C4 plants reach CO₂ saturation at about 360 µL L⁻¹.
  • C3 plants respond to increased CO₂ and saturate only beyond 450 µL L⁻¹.
  • Therefore, current CO₂ availability is limiting for C3 plants more than for C4 plants.

Applied Use: CO₂ Enrichment in Greenhouses

  • Greenhouse practice: C3 crops such as tomato and bell pepper are grown in CO₂-enriched atmospheres to increase photosynthesis → higher productivity/yields.
Common mistake: Writing that “C4 plants always increase continuously with CO₂.” In fact, they show earlier saturation (~360 µL L⁻¹) compared with C3.

Temperature (11.10.3)

Temperature affects photosynthesis mainly through its control of enzyme-mediated reactions.
  • Dark reactions (carbon fixation/Calvin cycle etc.) are enzymatic → strongly temperature-controlled.
  • Light reactions are also temperature-sensitive but to a much lesser extent.
  • C4 plants respond better to higher temperatures → higher photosynthetic rates at higher temperature.
  • C3 plants have a lower temperature optimum.
  • Optimum temperature depends on habitat adaptation: tropical plants have a higher temperature optimum than temperate plants.
Temperature change
→
Enzyme activity in dark reactions changes
→
Overall photosynthetic rate changes
Exam phrasing: Say “dark reactions are enzymatic and temperature-controlled” rather than “dark reactions need darkness.” (They are light-independent, not necessarily occurring only at night.)

Water (11.10.4)

Although water is a reactant in the light reaction, water availability affects photosynthesis mainly indirectly through its effects on the plant.
  • Water stress causes stomatal closure → reduced CO₂ entry → reduced CO₂ availability for fixation.
  • Water stress causes leaf wilting → reduced leaf surface area and reduced metabolic activity.
Water stress
→
Stomata close
→
CO₂ availability drops
→
Photosynthesis decreases
Don’t oversimplify: Water limits photosynthesis not merely because it is “used up” in photolysis, but because it controls CO₂ supply (stomata) and leaf function.

Chapter Summary (Consolidation of Key Points)

  • Photosynthesis enables green plants to make food: CO₂ enters via stomata and is used to form carbohydrates (mainly glucose and starch).
  • Occurs in green parts, mainly leaves. Mesophyll cells contain many chloroplasts responsible for CO₂ fixation.
  • In chloroplasts: membranes are sites of light reaction; stroma is site of chemosynthetic pathway (carbon-fixing reactions).
  • Two stages: Light reaction and carbon-fixing reactions.
Think: Leaves = “solar panels” + “sugar factory” → CO₂ in (stomata), sugar stored as starch.

Carbon Fixation (Calvin Cycle) and Photorespiration

  • CO₂ is added by RuBisCO to RuBP (5-carbon compound) → forms 2 molecules of 3-carbon PGA.
  • PGA is converted to sugar through the Calvin cycle, and RuBP is regenerated.
  • ATP and NADPH (from light reaction) are utilised in the Calvin cycle.
  • RuBisCO also catalyses a wasteful oxygenation reaction in C3 plants → photorespiration.
