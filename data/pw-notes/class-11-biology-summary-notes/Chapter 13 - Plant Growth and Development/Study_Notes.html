13.1 Growth

Definition and essential features of growth

Growth = an irreversible and permanent increase in size of:
  • an organ, or its parts, or
  • even an individual cell.
Growth is generally accompanied by metabolic processes (both anabolic and catabolic) and occurs at the expense of energy.
  • Leaf expansion is an example of true growth because it is irreversible and permanent and is linked with metabolism.
Swelling of a piece of wood in water—this is a size increase but is classically considered not true growth because it is mainly due to water absorption (imbibition) rather than metabolic, protoplasmic increase.
Common mistake: Writing “any increase in size is growth.” In exams, emphasise: irreversible + permanent + metabolic/energy-linked. Mention why swelling of wood differs (primarily physical imbibition).

13.1.1 Plant growth generally is indeterminate (open form of growth)

Plant growth is unique because plants retain the capacity for unlimited growth throughout life. This is due to the presence of meristems at specific locations.
  • Meristematic cells have the capacity to divide and self-perpetuate.
  • Their products soon lose the capacity to divide and form the plant body.
Open form of growth = growth in which new cells are always added to the plant body due to continuous meristem activity.
Exam angle: If asked “What if the meristem ceases to divide?”, the logical consequence is that addition of new cells stops, so further growth of that region/axis would halt.

Meristems and types of growth: primary vs secondary

Key meristems previously studied:
Root apical meristemResponsible for primary growth; elongation along the axis
Shoot apical meristemResponsible for primary growth; elongation along the axis
Vascular cambiumLateral meristem; increases girth (secondary growth)
Cork cambiumLateral meristem; increases girth (secondary growth)
  • Primary growth: mainly due to root apical and shoot apical meristems; contributes to elongation along the plant axis.
  • Secondary growth: occurs in dicotyledonous plants and gymnosperms due to lateral meristems (vascular cambium and cork cambium) that appear later in life; increases the girth of the organs where active.

13.1.2 Growth is measurable

At the cellular level, growth is mainly due to an increase in the amount of protoplasm. Because protoplasm increase is difficult to measure directly, growth is measured using parameters approximately proportional to it.
Common growth measurement parameters:
Fresh weightIncrease in fresh weight
Dry weightIncrease in dry weight
LengthIncrease in length
AreaIncrease in area
VolumeIncrease in volume
Cell numberIncrease in cell number
Illustrative extremes (must remember):
  • A single maize root apical meristem can produce > 17,500 new cells per hour → growth expressed as increase in cell number.
  • Cells in a watermelon may increase in size up to 3,50,000 times → growth expressed as increase in cell size.
Choose the measurement based on the structure:
  • Pollen tube: growth measured in terms of length.
  • Dorsiventral leaf: growth reflected as increase in surface area.
Exam tip: When asked “How is growth measured?”, list multiple parameters and add one context example (e.g., pollen tube length; leaf area; meristem cell number).

13.1.3 Phases of growth (root tip as model)

The period of growth is divided into three phases:
Meristematic phase
→
Elongation phase
→
Maturation phase
Meristematic phase (at root/shoot apex):
  • Cells are constantly dividing.
  • Rich in protoplasm.
  • Have large conspicuous nuclei.
  • Cell walls are primary, thin, and cellulosic.
  • Abundant plasmodesmatal connections.
Elongation phase (just proximal to meristematic zone; away from tip):
  • Increased vacuolation
  • Cell enlargement
  • New cell wall deposition
Maturation phase (further away from apex; proximal to elongation zone):
  • Cells attain maximal size.
  • Wall thickening occurs.
  • Protoplasmic modifications occur.
  • Most differentiated tissues/cell types studied earlier belong to this phase.
Common confusion: “Proximal” here means just next, away from the tip when moving from the apex toward older regions.

13.1.4 Growth rates

Growth rate = increased growth per unit time. Therefore, rate of growth can be expressed mathematically (details to follow in later text), and an organism/organ can produce more cells in a variety of ways.

Plant Growth: Growth Rate Patterns (Arithmetic vs Geometrical)

Growth rate may increase in two main patterns:
Arithmetic growthconstant increase per unit time; linear curve
Geometrical growthincrease proportional to current size; exponential then sigmoid (S-curve) in nature

Arithmetic Growth

Definition: After mitotic division, only one daughter cell continues to divide; the other differentiates and matures.
Example idea: A root elongating at a constant rate shows arithmetic growth; length increases by the same amount each unit time.
Graph: Plot of organ length vs time gives a linear (straight-line) curve.
Arithmetic growth equation: Lt = L0 + rt
  • Lt = length at time t
  • L0 = length at time 0
  • r = growth rate / elongation per unit time
Exam trap: Arithmetic growth is not “slow”; it is defined by constant addition per unit time and a linear graph, not by how big the organ is.

Geometrical Growth

Definition: After mitotic division, both progeny cells retain the ability to divide, so the number/size increases proportionally—initially slow, then rapid.
Typical phases when plotted against time:
Lag phaseinitial growth slow
Log/Exponential phasegrowth increases rapidly at an exponential rate
Stationary phasewith limited nutrients, growth slows and plateaus
Lag (slow start)
→
Log/Exponential (rapid)
→
Stationary (plateau)
Graph in natural conditions: Plotting growth parameter vs time typically gives a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve. Significance: A sigmoid curve is characteristic of living organisms growing in a natural environment and is typical for cells, tissues, and plant organs.
Exponential (geometrical) growth equation: W1 = W0 ert
  • W1 = final size (weight/height/number, etc.)
  • W0 = initial size at the beginning of the period
  • r = growth rate (relative growth rate)
  • t = time of growth
  • e = base of natural logarithms
Relative growth rate (r) as an efficiency index: In exponential growth, r measures the ability of the plant to produce new plant material and is referred to as the efficiency index. Implication: Final size W1 depends on initial size W0; larger starting size can yield larger final size even with the same r and t.
Exam trap: “Geometrical growth” is not automatically an S-curve; the pure mathematical form is exponential, but in real systems nutrient limitation makes the overall curve sigmoid due to a stationary phase.

Absolute Growth Rate vs Relative Growth Rate

Quantitative comparisons between growth of living systems can be made in two ways:
Absolute growth rate (AGR)total growth per unit time
Relative growth rate (RGR)growth per unit time on a common basis (e.g., per unit initial size)
How to interpret the leaf example (A vs B):
  • Leaves A and B start at different sizes but each increases area by 5 cm² in the same time (to form A1 and B1).
  • Absolute growth rate: same for both, because the absolute increase (5 cm²) per time is the same.
  • Relative growth rate: higher in the leaf that was smaller initially, because the same 5 cm² represents a larger fraction of its initial area.
Quick exam check: If two systems gain the same amount, the one that started smaller has the higher RGR.

Conditions for Growth (Plant Growth Requirements)

Growth requires both resources and appropriate environmental conditions.

Essential Resources: Water, Oxygen, Nutrients

Water
  • Plant cells grow in size mainly by cell enlargement, which requires water.
  • Turgidity provides the force for extension growth.
  • Plant growth and development are closely linked to the water status of the plant.
  • Water acts as a medium for enzymatic activities needed for growth.
Oxygen
  • Required for releasing metabolic energy essential for growth activities.
Nutrients (macro- and micro-essential elements)
  • Needed for synthesis of protoplasm.
  • Also act as a source of energy.

Environmental Conditions: Temperature, Light, Gravity

  • Each plant has an optimum temperature range best suited for growth.
  • Deviation from this optimum range may be detrimental to survival.
  • Environmental signals like light and gravity affect specific phases/stages of growth.
Exam tip: When asked “conditions for growth,” include both (1) resources (water/oxygen/nutrients) and (2) signals/conditions (temperature, light, gravity).

Differentiation, Dedifferentiation and Redifferentiation

Differentiation

Differentiation: Cells derived from root apical meristem, shoot apical meristem, and cambium mature and become structurally specialised to perform specific functions.
What changes during differentiation? Cells undergo few to major structural changes in:
  • Cell wall
  • Protoplasm
  • Example: Tracheary element differentiation
    • Cell loses protoplasm.
    • Develops strong, elastic, lignocellulosic secondary cell wall.
    • Functional outcome: can conduct water over long distances even under extreme tension.
Link structure to function: In answers, explicitly connect anatomical features (e.g., lignified secondary wall, loss of protoplasm) to their function (water conduction, strength under tension).

Dedifferentiation

Dedifferentiation: Living differentiated cells that have lost the capacity to divide may regain the capacity for division under certain conditions.
Examples: Formation of meristems from fully differentiated parenchyma cells:
Interfascicular cambiumformed from differentiated parenchyma between vascular bundles
Cork cambiumformed from differentiated parenchyma to produce protective tissues

Redifferentiation

Redifferentiation: Cells produced by dedifferentiated meristems/tissues may again lose the capacity to divide and mature to perform specific functions.
Differentiated cell (non-dividing)
→
Dedifferentiation (regains division; meristem formed)
→
Redifferentiation (specialises again)
Wording to use in exams: Dedifferentiation = regain division; Redifferentiation = specialise again after division.

Open (Indeterminate) Nature of Differentiation in Plants

Like growth, plant differentiation is described as open because cells/tissues arising from the same meristem can attain different mature structures.
Position-dependent differentiation: Final structure at maturity is influenced by the location of the cell within the organ.
  • Root example:
    • Cells positioned away from the root apical meristem differentiate into root-cap cells.
    • Cells pushed to the periphery mature into epidermis.

Plasticity and Heterophylly

Plasticity: Ability of plants to follow different developmental pathways in response to:
  • Environment
  • Life stages/phases
This results in formation of different kinds of structures.
Think of plants as “adjustable blueprints”: same plant, different leaf shapes or structures depending on where/when/how it grows.

Heterophylly as an Example of Plasticity

Heterophylly: Presence of different leaf forms on the same plant due to developmental stage or environment; it is a classic example of plasticity.
Types/examples mentioned:
Juvenile vs mature leaf formscotton, coriander, larkspur: juvenile leaves differ in shape from mature leaves
Environmental heterophyllybuttercup: leaves formed in air differ from leaves formed in water
How to frame answers: State “heterophylly” then explicitly connect it to “plasticity” and specify whether it is due to phase of life (juvenile vs mature) or environment (air vs water).

Development, Growth and Differentiation (Linkage & Control)

Development in plants is the sum of growth + differentiation. Therefore, growth, differentiation and development are closely related life events.

Factors Controlling Development

Plant development (both growth and differentiation) is controlled by:
Intrinsic factorsInternal control: genetic + chemical regulators
Extrinsic factorsEnvironmental control: external conditions
  • Intrinsic factors include:
    • Intracellular (genetic) factors
    • Intercellular chemical factors (e.g., plant growth regulators)
  • Extrinsic factors include: light, temperature, water, oxygen, nutrition etc.

Plant Growth Regulators (PGRs)

13.4.1 Characteristics of PGRs

Plant Growth Regulators (PGRs) are small, simple molecules with diverse chemical composition that regulate plant growth and development. They are also called plant growth substances, plant hormones, or phytohormones in literature.
Chemical diversity of PGRs (must know examples):
Indole compoundsIAA (indole-3-acetic acid)
Adenine derivativesKinetin (N6-furfurylamino purine)
Carotenoid derivativesABA (abscisic acid)
TerpenesGA3 (gibberellic acid)
GasesEthylene (C2H4)

Functional Classification of PGRs

PGRs are broadly divided into two groups based on function in the living plant body.
Growth promotersPromote division, enlargement, pattern formation, tropic growth, flowering, fruiting, seed formation
Growth inhibitors / stress-response PGRsWound/stress responses; dormancy, abscission, growth inhibition
  • Plant growth promoters: Auxins, Gibberellins, Cytokinins
  • Growth inhibitor/stress-related group: Abscisic acid (ABA)
  • Ethylene: can fit either group, but is largely an inhibitor of growth activities.
Exam trap: Do not force ethylene strictly into “promoter” or “inhibitor” only. Text point: it could fit either group but is largely inhibitory.

13.4.2 Discovery of Plant Growth Regulators (Accidental Discoveries)

Key theme: The discovery of each of the five major PGR groups was accidental.

Discovery of Auxin (Phototropism Experiments)

Darwin and Francis Darwin observed that coleoptiles of canary grass bend towards unilateral light (phototropism). Conclusion from experiments: the tip of the coleoptile is the site of a transmittable influence that causes bending of the whole coleoptile.
Unilateral light
→
Tip perceives stimulus
→
Signal transmitted
→
Coleoptile bends to light
  • F.W. Went isolated auxin from tips of coleoptiles of oat seedlings.
Think of the coleoptile tip as a “sensor + signal sender”: the bending happens elsewhere, but the command originates at the tip.

Discovery of Gibberellins (Bakanae Disease)

Bakanae (“foolish seedling”) disease of rice seedlings is caused by the fungus Gibberella fujikuroi.
  • E. Kurosawa (1926): rice seedlings showed disease symptoms when treated with sterile filtrates of the fungus.
  • The active substances were later identified as gibberellic acid (a gibberellin).

Discovery of Cytokinins (Callus Proliferation Requirement)

F. Skoog and co-workers: Callus (mass of undifferentiated cells) from internodal segments of tobacco stems proliferated only when, besides auxins, the nutrient medium contained certain supplements.
  • Required supplements (in addition to auxins): vascular tissue extracts, yeast extract, coconut milk, or DNA
  • Miller et al. (1955) identified and crystallised the cytokinesis-promoting substance, named kinetin.

Discovery of Abscisic Acid (ABA)

  • Mid-1960s: three independent studies purified/characterised inhibitors named inhibitor-B, abscission II, and dormin.
  • Later, all three were proven chemically identical and named abscisic acid (ABA).

Discovery of Ethylene (Volatile Ripening Factor)

  • H.H. Cousins (1910) confirmed release of a volatile substance from ripened oranges that hastened ripening of stored unripened bananas.
  • This volatile substance was identified as ethylene, a gaseous PGR.
Exam focus: Link each PGR to its discovery cue: phototropism (auxin), bakanae (GA), tobacco callus + DNA/coconut milk (cytokinin), inhibitor-B/abscission II/dormin (ABA), volatile ripening gas (ethylene).

13.4.3 Physiological Effects of Plant Growth Regulators

13.4.3.1 Auxins

Auxins (Greek auxein = “to grow”) were first isolated from human urine. The term auxin applies to IAA (indole-3-acetic acid) and other natural/synthetic compounds with similar growth-regulating properties. Auxins are generally produced at growing apices of stems and roots, from where they migrate to regions of action.
Examples of auxins (must distinguish natural vs synthetic):
Natural auxinsIAA, IBA (indole butyric acid)
Synthetic auxinsNAA (naphthalene acetic acid), 2,4-D (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid)
Think: Auxin = “growth signal” made at the tips → travels to where the plant needs shaping (roots, buds, fruits).
Agricultural/horticultural uses and effects of auxins:
  • Root initiation in stem cuttings (used widely in plant propagation).
  • Promote flowering (example: pineapple).
  • Fruit/leaf drop (abscission control):
    • Prevent fruit and leaf drop at early stages
    • Promote abscission of older mature leaves and fruits

Apical Dominance (Auxin-mediated)

In most higher plants, the growing apical bud inhibits the growth of lateral (axillary) buds. This is called apical dominance.
Apical bud present
→
Lateral buds suppressed
Decapitation effect: Removal of shoot tips (decapitation) usually results in the growth of lateral buds (branching).
Decapitation (shoot tip removed)
→
Lateral buds grow
→
Branching
  • Applications: used in tea plantations and hedge-making to promote branching after tip removal.
Write clearly in exams: apical bud present → lateral buds suppressed; apical bud removed → lateral buds grow into branches.

Additional Effects/Uses of Auxins

ParthenocarpyInduction (fruit development without fertilisation), e.g., tomato
Herbicidal use2,4-D kills dicotyledonous weeds but does not affect mature monocotyledonous plants → weed-free lawns
Xylem differentiationControls xylem differentiation
Cell division supportHelps in cell division
Common mix-up: 2,4-D is used to kill dicots, while mature monocots are largely unaffected (hence lawns).

13.4.3.2 Gibberellins

Gibberellins (GAs) are promotory PGRs. Over 100 gibberellins are reported from diverse organisms (including fungi and higher plants). They are denoted as GA1, GA2, GA3, etc. Gibberellic acid (GA3) was among the first discovered and is the most intensively studied. All GAs are acidic.
  • GAs produce a wide range of physiological responses.
  • Axis/internode elongation is a key, exploited effect.
Applications/physiological effects of gibberellins:
Grape stalk elongationIncrease length of grape stalks (via increased axis length)
Fruit shape improvementCause fruits like apple to elongate and improve shape
Delay senescenceFruits remain on trees longer → extended market period
Malting (brewing)GA3 speeds up malting process
Sugarcane yield boostIncreases stem length → yield ↑ by as much as 20 tonnes per acre
Conifers: early seedsSpraying juvenile conifers hastens maturity → early seed production
Bolting promotionPromotes bolting in beet, cabbages, and many rosette habit plants
Bolting = internode elongation just prior to flowering (not merely “flowering”).

13.4.3.3 Cytokinins

Cytokinins have specific effects on cytokinesis (cell division). They were discovered as kinetin, a modified adenine (a purine), obtained from autoclaved herring sperm DNA.
Kinetin is historically key but does not occur naturally in plants.
Search for natural cytokinins:
  • Isolation of zeatin from corn-kernels and coconut milk.
  • After zeatin: several naturally occurring cytokinins and some synthetic compounds with cell division-promoting activity were identified.
Do not write “kinetin is a natural plant hormone.” Text states clearly: kinetin does not occur naturally in plants; zeatin is a natural cytokinin.

Plant Growth Regulators (PGRs): Cytokinins (continuation)

Cytokinins are plant growth regulators synthesised mainly in regions of rapid cell division (e.g., root apices, developing shoot buds, young fruits). They primarily promote cell division and influence several aspects of shoot development.

Sites of synthesis (where made most)

Root apicesMajor site of cytokinin synthesis
Developing shoot budsActive growth regions
Young fruitsRapid cell division zones

Physiological effects (what they do)

  • Promote formation of new leaves
  • Promote development of chloroplasts in leaves
  • Promote lateral shoot growth
  • Promote adventitious shoot formation
  • Overcome apical dominance (reduce suppression of lateral buds by the apical bud)
  • Promote nutrient mobilisation → helps in delay of leaf senescence
Exam link: “Cytokinins overcome apical dominance” + “delay senescence” are repeatedly tested applications/MCQ stems.
Common confusion: Cytokinins are strongly linked with shoot/lateral bud activation and delay of senescence; do not mix this up with ABA (stress/inhibitor) or ethylene (senescence/abscission/ripening).

Ethylene

Ethylene = a simple gaseous plant growth regulator (PGR). Produced in high amounts by senescing tissues + ripening fruits. Major roles: senescence, abscission, fruit ripening (and more).

Synthesis (where/when produced more)

Senescing tissueshigh ethylene output during ageing
Ripening fruitsmajor source during ripening

Effects on seedling growth (triple-response type features)

Triple response (seedlings) = classic ethylene effect set.
  • Causes horizontal growth of seedlings
  • Causes swelling of the axis
  • Causes apical hook formation in dicot seedlings

Senescence and abscission

  • Promotes senescence
  • Promotes abscission of plant organs, especially leaves and flowers

Fruit ripening and respiratory climactic

Ethylene ↑
→
Fruit ripening ↑
→
Respiration rate ↑
  • Ethylene is highly effective in fruit ripening
  • Enhances respiration rate during fruit ripening
Respiratory climactic (climacteric rise) = the rise in respiration rate occurring during ripening of fruits under ethylene influence.
Exam tip: The term asked is exactly “respiratory climactic” (as written here). Define as: rise in respiration during ripening.

Breaking dormancy and promoting germination/sprouting

  • Breaks seed dormancy and bud dormancy
  • Initiates germination in peanut seeds
  • Promotes sprouting of potato tubers

Deep water rice adaptation (internode/petiole elongation)

Ethylene promotes rapid internode/petiole elongation in deep water rice plants so leaves/upper shoot stay above water → survival in flooding.

Root growth and root hairs

  • Promotes root growth
  • Promotes root hair formation → increases absorption surface
Real-life analogy: Flooded rice + ethylene = a “rapid lift system” (internodes elongate fast so photosynthetic parts remain above water).

Agricultural/horticultural uses of ethylene

  • Used to initiate flowering and synchronise fruit-set in pineapples
  • Induces flowering in mango
  • Among the most widely used PGRs in agriculture (because it regulates many processes)

Ethephon: widely used source of ethylene

Ethephon = most widely used compound as a source of ethylene. In aqueous solution it is readily absorbed + transported, then releases ethylene slowly.
Ripening boostHastens fruit ripening in tomatoes + apples
Thinning toolAccelerates abscission → thinning of cotton, cherry, walnut
Yield via sex expressionPromotes female flowers in cucumbers → yield ↑
Don’t write: “ethylene is applied directly” in most field situations. In exams, mention ethephon as the practical source because it releases ethylene slowly.

Abscisic Acid (ABA)

Abscisic acid (ABA) was discovered for roles in abscission + dormancy. It acts as a general plant growth inhibitor and an inhibitor of plant metabolism, strongly linked with stress responses.

Core actions: inhibition, dormancy, and germination

  • Acts as a general growth inhibitor
  • Acts as an inhibitor of plant metabolism
  • Inhibits seed germination

Stomatal closure and stress tolerance (“stress hormone”)

  • Stimulates closure of stomata
  • Increases tolerance to various kinds of stresses
Why ABA is called the stress hormone: promotes stomatal closure + increases stress tolerance.

Seed development, maturation, dormancy, and desiccation tolerance

  • Plays an important role in seed development
  • Important in seed maturation
  • Induces/maintains seed dormancy
  • By inducing dormancy, helps seeds withstand desiccation and other unfavourable conditions

Antagonism with gibberellins (GAs)

In most situations, ABA antagonises gibberellins (GAs) — ABA generally inhibits processes that GAs promote (esp. around germination/dormancy).
Exam trap: “Which PGR induces immediate stomatal closure?” → ABA (not ethylene/cytokinin/GA/auxin).

Interaction and Integration of PGRs in Plant Growth and Development

PGR roles across growth, differentiation, and development

In any and every phase of plant growth, differentiation, and development, one or more PGRs play roles.

Types of relationships among PGRs

ComplementaryPGRs support each other’s effects in a process
Antagonisticone PGR opposes another (e.g., ABA vs GA)
Individualistica PGR acts alone as a dominant regulator in a context
Synergisticcombined effect is greater/critical together

Events controlled by interactions of multiple PGRs

  • Dormancy in seeds/buds
  • Abscission
  • Senescence
  • Apical dominance

Intrinsic vs extrinsic control of growth and development

PGRs provide only one kind of intrinsic control. Plant growth & development are controlled by:
  • Genomic control
  • Intrinsic control via PGRs
  • Extrinsic factors (environmental factors)

Extrinsic factors act via PGRs

Many extrinsic (environmental) factors like temperature and light control growth & development via PGRs.
  • Vernalisation
  • Flowering
  • Dormancy
  • Seed germination
  • Plant movements
Exam phrasing to use: “Extrinsic factors (light/temperature) often influence development through changes in PGR levels/actions.”

Growth = an irreversible increase expressed as parameters like size, area, length, height, volume, cell number, etc., and it includes an increase in protoplasmic material.
