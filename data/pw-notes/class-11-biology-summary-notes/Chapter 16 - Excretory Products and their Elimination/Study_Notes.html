Ch-16 : Excretory Products and their Elimination

INTRODUCTION

Excretory products are substances that accumulate in animals due to metabolic activities and/or excess ingestion and therefore must be removed totally or partially to maintain internal balance.
Nitrogenous wastesAmmonia, urea, uric acid (major forms)
Respiratory wasteCarbon dioxide (CO₂)
WaterExcess water must be eliminated/regulated
IonsNa⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, phosphate, sulphate, etc.
Relative toxicity & water requirement (key exam logic):
  • Ammonia = most toxic → needs large amount of water for elimination.
  • Uric acid = least toxic → eliminated with minimum water loss.
  • Terrestrial adaptation favored less toxic wastes (urea/uric acid) for water conservation.

Ammonotelism, ureotelism, uricotelism

  • Ammonotelism: excretion of nitrogenous waste mainly as ammonia.
  • Ureotelism: excretion mainly as urea.
  • Uricotelism: excretion mainly as uric acid.
Ammonotelic animalsMany bony fishes, aquatic amphibians, aquatic insects
Ureotelic animalsMammals, many terrestrial amphibians, marine fishes
Uricotelic animalsReptiles, birds, land snails, insects

Exam trap: Don’t just memorise animal groups—link each to the reason (toxicity and water availability). Also note: insects appear in both lists because many aquatic insects are ammonotelic, while (terrestrial) insects are typically uricotelic.

Excretory structures across the animal kingdom

A survey of animals shows wide variety of excretory structures: most invertebrates have simple tubular forms, while vertebrates possess complex tubular organs called kidneys.
Protonephridia / flame cellsPlatyhelminthes (Planaria), rotifers, some annelids, Amphioxus
NephridiaEarthworms and other annelids
Malpighian tubulesMost insects (incl. cockroaches)
Antennal / green glandsCrustaceans (e.g., prawns)

Functions of major invertebrate excretory organs

  • Protonephridia (flame cells): primarily concerned with ionic and fluid volume regulation (i.e., osmoregulation).
  • Nephridia: remove nitrogenous wastes and maintain fluid and ionic balance.
  • Malpighian tubules: removal of nitrogenous wastes and osmoregulation.
  • Antennal/green glands: perform excretory function in crustaceans (e.g., prawns).
Write examples exactly as given: Planaria for flame cells; cockroach for Malpighian tubules; prawn for green glands; earthworm for nephridia; Amphioxus also has protonephridia.

16.1 Human excretory system

Human excretory (urinary) system consists of the following parts:
Kidneys (pair)Main excretory organs; form urine
Ureters (pair)Carry urine from kidneys to bladder
Urinary bladderStores urine temporarily
UrethraConducts urine to outside

Kidney: location, external features, and internal regions

  • Shape/colour: reddish brown, bean-shaped.
  • Position: between last thoracic and third lumbar vertebra, close to the dorsal inner wall of abdominal cavity.
  • Adult kidney size: 10–12 cm long; 5–7 cm wide; 2–3 cm thick.
  • Average weight: 120–170 g.
Hilum: notch towards the centre of the inner concave surface of the kidney; ureter, blood vessels and nerves enter through it. Renal pelvis: broad funnel-shaped space present inner to hilum. Calyces: projections from renal pelvis (singular: calyx). Capsule: tough outer covering (outer layer) of kidney.
Two main internal zones:
  • Cortex (outer zone)
  • Medulla (inner zone)
Medullary pyramids: medulla is divided into conical masses that project into calyces. Renal columns (Columns of Bertini): extensions of cortex in between medullary pyramids.
Think “funnel + cups”: renal pelvis = the funnel space, calyces = cup-like projections receiving urine from pyramids.
Common confusion: renal pelvis is a cavity/space, whereas calyces are its projections; Columns of Bertini are cortex tissue extending between pyramids (not part of pelvis).

Nephron: functional unit of kidney

Each kidney contains nearly one million complex tubular structures called nephrons—the functional units of the kidney.
Part 1: GlomerulusTuft of capillaries
Part 2: Renal tubuleTubular portion that processes filtrate

Glomerulus and Bowman’s capsule (Malpighian body / renal corpuscle)

Glomerulus: tuft of capillaries formed by the afferent arteriole (a fine branch of the renal artery). Efferent arteriole: carries blood away from the glomerulus. Bowman’s capsule: double-walled cup-like structure that encloses the glomerulus; it is the beginning of the renal tubule. Malpighian body / renal corpuscle: glomerulus + Bowman’s capsule.

Renal pelvis (via medullary pyramids into calyces)
  • Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT): highly coiled network after Bowman’s capsule.
  • Henle’s loop: hairpin-shaped; has descending limb and ascending limb.
  • Distal convoluted tubule (DCT): another highly coiled region; continuation of ascending limb.
  • Collecting duct: straight tube receiving DCTs of many nephrons; many collecting ducts converge and open into renal pelvis through medullary pyramids in the calyces.

Cortical vs juxtamedullary nephrons + regional location

Location of nephron parts:
  • Cortex: Malpighian corpuscle, PCT, DCT.
  • Medulla: Henle’s loop dips into medulla.
Cortical nephronsMajority; Henle’s loop short; extends very little into medulla
Juxtamedullary nephronsSome; Henle’s loop very long; runs deep into medulla

Peritubular capillaries and vasa recta

Peritubular capillaries: fine capillary network formed by the efferent arteriole around the renal tubule. Vasa recta: a minute vessel of the peritubular network that runs parallel to Henle’s loop forming a U-shaped arrangement.
Vasa recta in nephron types: vasa recta is absent or highly reduced in cortical nephrons.

16.2 Urine formation (intro + glomerular filtration)

Urine formation involves three main processes occurring in different parts of the nephron:
Glomerular filtrationFiltration of blood in glomerulus
ReabsorptionUseful substances taken back (covered later in chapter)
SecretionAdditional wastes added to filtrate (covered later in chapter)

Glomerular filtration: rate, layers, and ultrafiltration concept

  • First step in urine formation = filtration of blood by glomerulus = glomerular filtration.
  • On average, kidneys filter 1100–1200 mL of blood per minute.
  • This is roughly 1/5th of the blood pumped out by each ventricle of the heart per minute.
Driving force: glomerular capillary blood pressure causes filtration. Filtration occurs through 3 layers:
  1. Endothelium of glomerular blood vessels
  2. Basement membrane between the two epithelial layers
  3. Epithelium of Bowman’s capsule
Podocytes: epithelial cells of Bowman’s capsule arranged intricately to leave minute spaces called filtration slits / slit pores.
What passes into Bowman’s capsule?
  • Almost all plasma constituents except proteins pass into the lumen of Bowman’s capsule.
  • Because filtration is very fine and protein-free, it is termed ultra filtration.
“Ultra-filtration” = super-fine filter: small solutes go through, proteins stay behind.
High-yield phrasing: glomerular filtration is through 3 layers and podocytes create filtration slits; filtrate is essentially plasma minus proteins → hence ultrafiltration.

Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR)

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) = the volume of filtrate formed by kidneys per minute.
Normal GFR (healthy individual) ≈ 125 mL/min ≈ 180 L/day.
Kidneys regulate GFR using built-in mechanisms; a key mechanism involves the juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA).

Reabsorption (why it is necessary; active vs passive)

Reabsorption = movement of useful substances from the filtrate back into blood through renal tubules.
Daily filtrate ≈ 180 L/day but urine released ≈ 1.5 L/day ⇒ ~99% of filtrate must be reabsorbed by renal tubules.
Active reabsorptionGlucose, amino acids, Na+ (examples given)
Passive reabsorption/transportNitrogenous wastes (as stated: absorbed by passive transport)
Water reabsorption (early nephron)Occurs passively in initial segments
Use the text’s wording in answers: it states nitrogenous wastes are absorbed by passive transport. Even if you know broader physiology, reproduce the given classification for exam alignment.

Tubular Secretion (role in homeostasis)

Tubular secretion = addition of substances from tubular cells into the filtrate during urine formation.
  • Substances secreted include: H+, K+, ammonia (NH3).
  • Importance: helps maintain ionic balance and acid–base balance of body fluids.
Urine formation is not just filtration. It involves: filtration + selective reabsorption + secretion.

Function of the Tubules

Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT)

PCT lining: simple cuboidal brush border epithelium—brush border increases surface area for reabsorption.
Major reabsorption site: PCT reabsorbs:
  • Nearly all essential nutrients
  • 70–80% of electrolytes and water
  • pH and ionic balance role: PCT selectively secretes H+ and NH3 into filtrate.
  • PCT also absorbs HCO3– from filtrate (helps acid–base balance).

Henle’s Loop

Key idea: Though reabsorption is minimum in the ascending limb, Henle’s loop is crucial for maintaining high osmolarity of medullary interstitial fluid.
Descending limbPermeable to water; almost impermeable to electrolytes → filtrate becomes concentrated
Ascending limbImpermeable to water; electrolytes transported actively/passively → filtrate becomes diluted
  • As filtrate goes down the descending limb: water leaves → filtrate concentrates.
  • As concentrated filtrate goes up the ascending limb: electrolytes leave to medullary fluid (no water follows) → filtrate dilutes.
Don’t swap limb properties: descending = water-permeable; ascending = water-impermeable.

Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT)

DCT performs conditional reabsorption of Na+ and water (depends on body needs/hormones).
  • Reabsorbs HCO3–
  • Selectively secretes H+, K+, and NH3
  • Maintains pH and sodium–potassium balance in blood

Collecting Duct

Collecting duct extends from the cortex to the inner medulla.
  • Reabsorbs large amounts of water → helps produce concentrated urine.
  • Allows passage of small amounts of urea into medullary interstitium → maintains osmolarity.
  • Helps maintain pH and ionic balance via selective secretion of H+ and K+.
Why urea “leak” matters: letting some urea enter the medulla acts like adding solute to the surrounding tissue, helping the kidney draw water out later and concentrate urine.

Mechanism of Concentration of the Filtrate

Counter Current System: Henle’s Loop and Vasa Recta

Mammals can produce concentrated urine due mainly to the special arrangement of:
  • Henle’s loop
  • Vasa recta
Counter current pattern:
  • Filtrate flow in the two limbs of Henle’s loop is in opposite directions → forms a counter current.
  • Blood flow through the two limbs of vasa recta is also counter current.
  • Proximity of Henle’s loop and vasa recta + counter current flow maintains an increasing osmolarity toward inner medulla.
Osmolarity gradient: ~300 mOsmol L–1 (cortex) → ~1200 mOsmol L–1 (inner medulla).

How the Gradient is Generated and Maintained (NaCl and Urea cycling)

The medullary osmotic gradient is mainly due to NaCl and urea.
NaCl pathwayascending limb (Henle) transports NaCl; exchanged with descending limb (vasa recta); returned to interstitium by ascending portion (vasa recta)
Urea pathwaysmall amounts enter thin segment of ascending limb (Henle); transported back to interstitium by collecting tubule
Counter current mechanism = the above transport of substances facilitated by the special arrangement of Henle’s loop and vasa recta that maintains the medullary concentration gradient.
Counter current arrangement
→
Medullary interstitial gradient maintained
→
Water leaves collecting tubule easily
→
Filtrate becomes concentrated (urine)
Capacity statement: Human kidneys can produce urine nearly 4× more concentrated than the initial filtrate formed.

Regulation of Kidney Function

Overall Control: Hormonal Feedback Sources

Kidney function is monitored and regulated by hormonal feedback mechanisms involving:

ADH (Vasopressin) and Osmoreceptor Feedback

Osmoreceptors respond to changes in:
  • Blood volume
  • Body fluid volume
  • Ionic concentration
Excessive fluid loss
→
Osmoreceptors activated
→
Hypothalamus stimulated
→
ADH released from neurohypophysis
  • ADH (vasopressin) facilitates water reabsorption from latter parts of tubule → prevents diuresis (excess urine production).
  • Negative feedback: increased body fluid volume switches off osmoreceptors → ADH suppressed.
  • ADH also affects kidney function via vasoconstrictory effects on blood vessels → ↑ blood pressure → ↑ glomerular blood flow → ↑ GFR.
Be precise: ADH is released from the neurohypophysis (posterior pituitary), but the stimulus pathway begins with osmoreceptors → hypothalamus.

Renin–Angiotensin Mechanism (JGA-mediated)

Trigger conditions: A fall in any of the following can activate JG cells:
  • Glomerular blood flow
  • Glomerular blood pressure
  • GFR
↓ glomerular flow/pressure/GFR
→
Renin released (JG cells)
→
Angiotensinogen → Angiotensin I → Angiotensin II
  • Angiotensin II is a powerful vasoconstrictor → increases glomerular blood pressure → increases GFR.
  • Angiotensin II activates adrenal cortex → releases aldosterone.
  • Aldosterone causes reabsorption of Na+ and water from distal parts of tubule → increases blood pressure and GFR.
This integrated pathway is called the Renin–Angiotensin mechanism.

Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF): Check on RA Mechanism

Stimulus: Increased blood flow to the atria of the heart → release of Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF).
  • ANF causes vasodilation (dilation of blood vessels) → decreases blood pressure.
  • Therefore, ANF acts as a check on the renin–angiotensin mechanism.
Compare roles: Renin–angiotensin tends to raise BP/GFR; ANF tends to lower BP (opposes RA system).

Micturition (Urine Release) & Urine Characteristics

Micturition = the process of release (voiding) of urine from the urinary bladder. Micturition reflex = the neural mechanism that causes micturition (reflex control leading to bladder emptying).
Mechanism described: neural signals initiate:
  • Contraction of smooth muscles of the urinary bladder
  • Simultaneous relaxation of the urethral sphincter
  • → results in release of urine
Normal urine output (adult): about 1–1.5 litres/day. Typical urine properties: light yellow, watery fluid, slightly acidic (about pH 6.0), with a characteristic odour. Urea excretion (average): about 25–30 g/day.
Clinical relevance: urine characteristics change in different conditions; urine analysis aids diagnosis of metabolic disorders and kidney malfunction.

Role of Other Organs in Excretion

Besides kidneys, lungs, liver, and skin also eliminate excretory wastes.

Lungs

  • Remove large amounts of CO2 (approximately 200 mL/min).
  • Also remove significant quantities of water daily (via expired air).

Liver

The liver secretes bile containing several substances that are eliminated from the body.
Bilirubinbile pigment excreted via bile
Biliverdinbile pigment excreted via bile
Cholesteroleliminated through bile
Degraded steroid hormonesexcreted via bile
Vitaminssome eliminated via bile
Drugsmany eliminated via bile
Most bile-carried substances ultimately pass out along with digestive wastes (i.e., via the intestine).
Think of liver + gut as a “waste exit route”: liver pushes specific wastes into bile, and the intestine carries them out with faeces.

Skin (Sweat glands & Sebaceous glands)

Skin contributes to excretion through secretions of sweat glands and sebaceous glands.
Sweat (from sweat glands): watery fluid containing NaCl, small amounts of urea, lactic acid, etc.
  • Primary function: cooling effect (thermoregulation).
  • Also helps: removal of some wastes (e.g., salts, traces of nitrogenous waste).
Sebum (from sebaceous glands): eliminates substances like sterols, hydrocarbons, and waxes. Function of sebum: provides a protective oily covering for the skin.
Additional route: small amounts of nitrogenous wastes can also be eliminated through saliva.

Disorders of the Excretory System

Uremia

Uremia = accumulation of urea in blood due to kidney malfunction.
Uremia is highly harmful and may lead to kidney failure.

Hemodialysis (Artificial Kidney)

Hemodialysis = removal of urea (nitrogenous wastes) from blood using an artificial kidney in uremic patients.
Blood drained from a convenient artery
→
Add anticoagulant (heparin)
→
Pump into dialysing unit (artificial kidney)
Coiled cellophane tube in dialysing fluid
→
Wastes diffuse out (concentration gradient)
→
Cleared blood returned via vein + anti-heparin
Dialysing unit structure: contains a coiled cellophane tube surrounded by dialysing fluid. Dialysing fluid composition: same as plasma except nitrogenous wastes.
Principle: the porous cellophane membrane allows passage of molecules based on concentration gradient. Because nitrogenous wastes are absent in the dialysing fluid, they move from blood → dialysing fluid, thereby clearing the blood.
Don’t forget the two pharmacological steps in dialysis answers: heparin is added before dialysis (to prevent clotting) and anti-heparin is added after dialysis before returning blood.
This method is described as a boon for thousands of uremic patients.

Kidney Transplantation

Kidney transplantation = ultimate method to correct acute renal failures (kidney failure) by transplanting a functioning kidney.
  • Kidney from a donor is transplanted to the host.
  • Preferably a close relative is chosen to minimise rejection by the host immune system.
  • Modern clinical procedures have increased the success rate.

Renal Calculi

Renal calculi = stone/insoluble mass of crystallised salts (e.g., oxalates) formed within the kidney.

Glomerulonephritis

Glomerulonephritis = inflammation of glomeruli of the kidney.

Chapter Wrap-up

  • Urine storage & release: urine is stored in the bladder until a voluntary signal from the CNS allows release via urethra (micturition), executed through the micturition reflex.
  • Concentration mechanism: DCT and collecting duct can concentrate filtrate about four times (from 300 mOsmol L–1 to 1200 mOsmol L–1), conserving water.
  • Counter current mechanism: operates between limbs of Henle’s loop and vasa recta; filtrate concentrates down the descending limb and dilutes in the ascending limb; electrolytes and urea are retained in interstitium.
  • Other excretory routes: lungs (CO2, water), liver (bile pigments and other substances), skin (sweat/sebum), saliva (small nitrogenous wastes).
  • Disorders & management: uremia (urea accumulation) → hemodialysis; ultimate correction of kidney failure → transplantation; renal calculi; glomerulonephritis.
