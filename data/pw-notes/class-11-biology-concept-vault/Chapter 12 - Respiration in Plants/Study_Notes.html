RESPIRATION IN PLANTS

Respiration in Plants

Respiration (cellular respiration) = the breaking of C–C bonds of complex compounds through oxidation within cells, releasing a considerable amount of energy in a step-wise, enzyme-controlled manner, with energy trapped as ATP.
All living organisms require energy to carry out life activities such as:
  • Absorption
  • Transport
  • Movement
  • Reproduction
  • Even breathing itself

Source of energy for life processes: oxidation of “food”

All energy for life processes is obtained by oxidation of macromolecules called “food”.
Green plants and cyanobacteria make their own food by photosynthesis: they trap light energy and convert it into chemical energy stored in carbohydrate bonds (e.g., glucose, sucrose, starch).
Exam trap: Even in green plants, not all cells photosynthesise. Only cells with chloroplasts (usually in superficial layers) photosynthesise; non-green parts still need food for respiration.
Photosynthesis (chloroplasts)
→
Carbohydrates formed & stored
→
Food translocated to non-green parts
→
Respiration (cytoplasm + mitochondria)
Animals are heterotrophic (depend on plants):
Herbivoresobtain food directly from plants
Carnivoresobtain food indirectly via herbivores
Saprophytes (e.g., fungi)depend on dead & decaying matter
Ultimate source: All food respired for life processes ultimately comes from photosynthesis.

What the chapter focuses on (context for exam)

This portion introduces that the chapter concerns:
  • Mechanism of breakdown of food inside cells to release energy (cellular respiration)
  • Trapping of released energy for ATP synthesis
Sites in eukaryotes:
  • Photosynthesis occurs in chloroplasts.
  • Breakdown of complex molecules to yield energy occurs in cytoplasm and mitochondria.

Respiratory substrates

Respiratory substrates = compounds that are oxidised during respiration.
Usually, carbohydrates are oxidised for energy release; however, in some plants under certain conditions, other substrates can be used:
Carbohydratesusual substrate
Proteinscan be used in some plants under conditions
Fatscan be used in some plants under conditions
Organic acidscan be used in some plants under conditions

How energy is captured: step-wise oxidation and ATP

During oxidation in cells, energy is not released in one burst as free heat. Instead:
  • Energy is released in a series of slow, step-wise reactions.
  • Steps are controlled by enzymes.
  • Energy is trapped as chemical energy in the form of ATP.
ATP is the energy currency of the cell: energy released by oxidation during respiration is used to synthesise ATP, and ATP is broken down whenever/wherever energy is needed.
Respiration provides:
  • ATP for energy-requiring processes
  • Carbon skeletons (intermediates) used as precursors for biosynthesis of other cell molecules

12.1 Do Plants Breathe?

Plants require O2 for respiration and release CO2 → so they must ensure O2 availability and CO2 removal.

Gas exchange structures in plants

Plants lack specialised respiratory organs (unlike animals) but exchange gases using:
Stomatapores mainly on leaves for gas exchange
Lenticelsopenings in stems (especially woody) for gas exchange

Why plants can manage without specialised respiratory organs

Plants can “get along” without respiratory organs due to three main reasons:
1. Local self-sufficiencyeach plant part meets its own gas-exchange needs; little gas transport between parts
2. Lower demandroots, stems, leaves respire at far lower rates than animals; large gas exchange mainly during photosynthesis
3. Short diffusion distancesmost living cells lie close to the surface; diffusion path is not great even in bulky plants
During photosynthesis, large volumes of gases are exchanged, and each leaf is adapted to meet its own needs. Also, in photosynthesising cells, O2 availability is not a problem because O2 is released within the cell.

Gas exchange in thick stems/roots: living vs dead tissues

Even in thick, woody stems and roots, diffusion remains feasible because:
  • In stems, living cells occur in thin layers inside and beneath the bark.
  • Stems have lenticels that allow gas exchange.
  • Cells in the interior are largely dead and provide mechanical support.
  • Most plant cells have at least some surface area in contact with air.
  • Loose packing of parenchyma cells in leaves, stems, roots creates an interconnected network of air spaces facilitating diffusion.
Analogy: A plant is like a building with many small windows (stomata/lenticels) and lots of internal corridors (air spaces in parenchyma). You don’t need one main “door” (lungs) because air can reach nearby rooms by short diffusion routes.

Complete combustion of glucose vs respiration strategy

Complete combustion of glucose yields CO2 and H2O, releasing most energy as heat:
Complete combustion (overall): C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + Energy (mostly as heat)
Key strategy in cellular respiration: Cells do not oxidise glucose in one step. They oxidise it in several small steps so that energy released in certain steps is just enough to be coupled to ATP synthesis instead of being lost as heat.
One-step oxidation
→
Energy lost mostly as heat (less usable)
Step-wise enzyme reactions
→
Energy conserved by ATP formation

Aerobic vs anaerobic situations: oxygen availability

Respiration generally uses oxygen and releases carbon dioxide, water and energy. However, some cells/organisms live where oxygen may not be available.
Even today, many organisms are adapted to anaerobic conditions. Historically, it is believed the first cells lived in an atmosphere lacking oxygen.
Types of anaerobes mentioned:
Facultative anaerobescan live with or without O2
Obligate anaerobesrequire anaerobic conditions; cannot tolerate/use O2
Universal point: All living organisms retain enzymatic machinery to partially oxidise glucose without oxygen.
Glycolysis = breakdown of glucose to pyruvic acid (occurs without requiring oxygen).

12.2 Glycolysis

Meaning and naming

Glycolysis term origin:
  • glycos (Greek) = sugar
  • lysis (Greek) = splitting

EMP pathway (Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas)

The scheme of glycolysis was given by Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof, and J. Parnas; therefore glycolysis is often called the EMP pathway.

Where it occurs and who has it

  • Occurs in the cytoplasm.
  • Present in all living organisms.
  • In anaerobic organisms, it is the only process in respiration (as stated here).

Overall outcome of glycolysis (as introduced here)

In glycolysis, glucose undergoes partial oxidation to form two molecules of pyruvic acid.

Source of glucose for glycolysis in plants

In plants, the glucose entering glycolysis is derived from:
  • Sucrose (the end product of photosynthesis)
  • Storage carbohydrates (text continues beyond this portion)
Exam focus from this slice: Do not write that “all plant cells photosynthesise” or that “respiration happens only in mitochondria.” Here: photosynthesis = chloroplast; respiration breakdown steps begin in cytoplasm and continue in mitochondria (eukaryotes).

Glycolysis: entry of sucrose and overview of the 10-step pathway

Glycolysis = a sequence of 10 enzyme-controlled reactions converting glucose (6C) → pyruvate (3C). While learning: always track where ATP is used, ATP is formed, and NADH + H+ is formed.

Sucrose entry into glycolysis (conversion to glycolytic intermediates)

Sucrose
→
Glucose + Fructose
→
Enter glycolysis
  • Sucrose is converted into glucose + fructose by invertase.
  • These two monosaccharides readily enter glycolysis.
Hexokinase phosphorylates both glucose and fructose so they enter as glucose-6-phosphate (G6P). Then G6P isomerises → fructose-6-phosphate (F6P). After this point, steps for glucose & fructose are the same.

ATP utilisation steps in glycolysis (energy investment)

ATP is utilised at two steps:
  1. Glucose → Glucose-6-phosphate (phosphorylation)
  2. Fructose-6-phosphate → Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (phosphorylation)
Exam trap: “ATP use” happens twice early in glycolysis—don’t forget the second ATP at F6P → F1,6-bisphosphate.

Splitting step: formation of two triose phosphates

Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (6C) splits into two 3C triose phosphates:
DHAPDihydroxyacetone phosphate (3C)
PGAL3-phosphoglyceraldehyde (3C) = glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate

NADH formation step (oxidation of PGAL)

Only one step forms NADH + H+ from NAD+ in glycolysis: PGAL → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPGA)
In this step:
  • Two redox-equivalents (two H atoms) removed from PGAL go to NAD+ → NADH + H+.
  • PGAL is oxidised.
  • Inorganic phosphate (Pi) participates to form BPGA.

ATP synthesis steps in glycolysis (energy payoff)

ATP is synthesised at two steps:
  1. BPGA → 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA) (energy released is trapped as ATP)
  2. Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) → pyruvic acid (ATP synthesised)
Exam tip: Because glucose splits into two 3-carbon molecules, both ATP-forming “payoff” steps happen twice per glucose (once per triose), even if the diagram shows the step once.

Fate of pyruvate: the three major pathways

Cells handle pyruvic acid (from glycolysis) in three major ways:
Lactic acid fermentationAnaerobic; pyruvate → lactic acid
Alcoholic fermentationAnaerobic; pyruvate → CO₂ + ethanol
Aerobic respiration (Krebs’ cycle)Complete oxidation → CO₂ + H₂O; requires O₂
Fermentation occurs under anaerobic conditions in many prokaryotes and unicellular eukaryotes. Complete oxidation of glucose to CO₂ + H₂O uses Krebs’ cycle (aerobic respiration) and requires O₂ supply.

Fermentation (anaerobic respiration pathways)

Alcoholic fermentation (yeast): pyruvate → CO₂ + ethanol

In yeast, fermentation = incomplete oxidation of glucose under anaerobic conditions where pyruvic acid becomes CO₂ + ethanol.
Pyruvic acid decarboxylasereleases CO₂ from pyruvate
Alcohol dehydrogenaseforms ethanol

Lactic acid fermentation: pyruvate → lactic acid

Some bacteria produce lactic acid from pyruvic acid under anaerobic conditions. In animal cells (e.g., muscle during exercise), when oxygen is inadequate, pyruvic acid is reduced to lactic acid.
Enzyme in muscles: lactate dehydrogenase catalyses reduction of pyruvate to lactic acid when O₂ is insufficient.

Role of NADH/NAD⁺ in fermentation (why fermentation can continue)

In both alcoholic & lactic acid fermentation:
  • NADH + H+ is the reducing agent.
  • NADH + H+ is reoxidised to NAD+.
Regenerating NAD+ is essential to keep glycolysis running anaerobically.

Energy yield and hazards of fermentation

  • Fermentation releases not much energy.
  • < 7% of the energy in glucose is released.
  • Not all released energy is trapped in high-energy ATP bonds.
  • Fermentation is hazardous because end products are acid (lactic acid) or alcohol (ethanol).
Exam calculation cue: Net ATP in fermentation = net ATP of glycolysis only: ATP made during glycolysis − ATP used during glycolysis. Fermentation reactions mainly regenerate NAD⁺, not add large ATP yield.
Real-life link (yeast): Yeasts can poison themselves—around 13% ethanol, yeast dies. So naturally fermented drinks usually can’t exceed about 13% alcohol unless boosted beyond natural fermentation.

Aerobic respiration: mitochondrial oxidation of pyruvate

Aerobic respiration = complete oxidation of organic substances in the presence of oxygen → releases CO₂, H₂O + a large amount of energy stored in the substrate. In eukaryotes, these steps occur in the mitochondria and require O₂.

Entry step: pyruvate transport into mitochondria

Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm — but aerobic respiration needs the “mitochondrial workspace” → so pyruvate must be moved into the mitochondria.
For aerobic respiration within mitochondria, the glycolysis end product pyruvate is transported from the cytoplasm into the mitochondria.

Crucial events of aerobic respiration and their mitochondrial locations

Two crucial events:
  • Complete oxidation of pyruvate by stepwise removal of all hydrogen atoms, leaving three molecules of CO₂.
  • Electrons (removed with hydrogen) are passed to molecular O₂ with simultaneous synthesis of ATP.
Compartmentation (must-know):
  • First process (pyruvate oxidation to CO₂) occurs in the mitochondrial matrix.
  • Second process (electron transfer to O₂ with ATP synthesis) is located on the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate: formation of acetyl-CoA

After entering the mitochondrial matrix, pyruvate undergoes oxidative decarboxylation catalysed by the enzyme complex pyruvic dehydrogenase. This reaction requires several coenzymes including NAD+ and Coenzyme A (CoA) (Mg²⁺ is also as required).
Pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction (as given): Pyruvic acid + CoA + NAD+ —(Pyruvate dehydrogenase; Mg²⁺)→ Acetyl CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H+
From one glucose, glycolysis produces two pyruvic acid molecules, and during their oxidative decarboxylation, two NADH are produced in total.

Link to Krebs’ cycle (TCA cycle): naming and origin

Acetyl CoA enters a cyclic pathway called the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, more commonly called the Krebs’ cycle, named after Hans Krebs who first elucidated it.

Start of TCA cycle (first reactions up to α-ketoglutarate)

First step: condensation of the acetyl group with oxaloacetic acid (OAA) and water to form citric acid. Enzyme: citrate synthase. A molecule of CoA is released.
Acetyl group + OAA + H₂O
—(citrate synthase)→
Citric acid + CoA (released)
Citrate is then isomerised to isocitrate.
After isocitrate formation, there are two successive steps of decarboxylation, leading to the formation of α-ketoglutaric acid.

Electron Transport System (ETS) and Oxidative Phosphorylation

Why NADH/FADH2 matter (link from TCA to ATP formation)

During the citric acid (TCA) cycle, energy is conserved mainly as reduced coenzymes (NADH + H+ and FADH2), while only a small amount appears as ATP directly. The “bulk ATP” is produced later when these reduced coenzymes are oxidised in the ETS and their electrons ultimately reduce O2 to H2O.
Think of NADH/FADH2 as “charged batteries” made in TCA — the big payoff (ATP) happens when they’re “discharged” in the ETS.
Up to this stage (glycolysis + pyruvate oxidation + TCA), glucose has been broken down to CO2, and reduced coenzymes (NADH + H+, FADH2) have accumulated—but O2 has not been directly used yet. Oxygen’s key role appears in the terminal stage (ETS) as the final acceptor.

Electron Transport System (ETS): definition and location

Electron Transport System (ETS) = the metabolic pathway in which electrons pass from one carrier to another in a defined sequence, finally reaching O2 to form H2O. Location: present in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

ETS components and electron flow (Complex I to IV)

Complex INADH dehydrogenase; oxidises NADH (matrix side) and transfers e− to ubiquinone
Complex IIEntry point for e− from FADH2 generated during succinate oxidation (TCA)
Ubiquinone (Q)Lipid-soluble carrier in inner membrane; becomes reduced to ubiquinol (QH2)

  • NADH (from mitochondrial matrix, produced in TCA) is oxidised by NADH dehydrogenase (Complex I) and its electrons move to ubiquinone.
  • FADH2 donates reducing equivalents via Complex II (formed during oxidation of succinate in TCA), also feeding into ubiquinone.
  • Ubiquinol is oxidised as electrons pass to cytochrome c through Complex III.
  • Cytochrome c shuttles electrons to Complex IV, where electrons ultimately reduce O2 to H2O.
Exam trap: Do not confuse carrier positions—cytochrome c is a mobile carrier on the outer surface of the inner membrane (between III and IV), while ubiquinone is embedded within the inner membrane.

Coupling electron flow to ATP formation (Complex V)

As electrons pass through carriers via Complex I to IV, the process is coupled to ATP synthase (Complex V), which catalyses formation of ATP from ADP + inorganic phosphate (Pi).
ATP synthesis reaction (mitochondria): ADP + Pi → ATP
ATP yield depends on electron donor:
  • Oxidation of 1 NADH → 3 ATP
  • Oxidation of 1 FADH2 → 2 ATP

Role of oxygen in aerobic respiration

In aerobic respiration, O2 participates only at the terminal stage of ETS, but it is vital because it removes hydrogen from the system. Thus, oxygen acts as the final hydrogen acceptor, enabling continued electron flow and oxidation of NADH/FADH2.
No oxygen = “traffic jam” at the end of the chain → electrons can’t be passed on → NADH/FADH2 can’t unload energy properly.
How to phrase in exams: “Oxygen is the final hydrogen/electron acceptor in the ETS; by accepting electrons (and hydrogen), it forms water and drives respiration forward.”

Why it is called oxidative phosphorylation

  • In respiration, the proton gradient (used to make ATP) is generated using energy released during oxidation–reduction (redox) reactions in the ETS.
  • This contrasts with photophosphorylation, where light energy is used to generate the proton gradient.
Therefore: ATP formation linked to oxidation is called oxidative phosphorylation.

The Respiratory Balance Sheet

Purpose and why it is “theoretical”

A respiratory balance sheet estimates the net ATP gain per glucose oxidised. In real living systems, it remains largely theoretical because multiple pathways operate simultaneously and intermediates may be diverted for other cellular needs.

Assumptions required to calculate net ATP per glucose

Calculations are made only if we assume:
Sequential orderly pathwaySubstrates form products step-by-step, and glycolysis → TCA → ETS occur one after another
Glycolytic NADH enters mitochondriaNADH formed in glycolysis is transferred into mitochondria and undergoes oxidative phosphorylation
No diversion of intermediatesNo pathway intermediates are used to synthesise any other compound
Only glucose is respiredNo alternative substrates enter the pathway at intermediate stages

Why these assumptions fail in living systems

  • Pathways function simultaneously, not strictly one after another.
  • Substrates can be withdrawn and introduced as needed (intermediates used in biosynthesis).
  • ATP is utilised as and when needed (continuous turnover).
  • Enzymatic rates are controlled by multiple regulatory mechanisms.
Analogy: A “balance sheet” is like calculating profits assuming a factory uses only one raw material, never diverts parts for other products, and runs each department strictly in sequence—useful for understanding efficiency, but not fully realistic.

Net ATP gain (as given for the theoretical calculation)

Under the above assumptions, the net gain during aerobic respiration of one glucose molecule can be 38 ATP.

Fermentation vs Aerobic Respiration (Comparison)

Exam questions often ask for three key differences between fermentation and aerobic respiration—learn these as crisp points.
Extent of glucose breakdownFermentation = partial breakdown; aerobic respiration = complete degradation to CO2 + H2O
ATP yield per glucoseFermentation = net gain of only 2 ATP per glucose degraded to pyruvic acid; aerobic respiration = many more ATP
Reoxidation of NADHFermentation: NADH → NAD+ slowly; aerobic respiration: oxidation is very vigorous
Common exam slip: Writing “fermentation produces no ATP.” Fermentation includes glycolysis, so the net gain is 2 ATP per glucose.

Amphibolic Pathway

Entry of different substrates into the respiratory pathway

Glucose is the favoured substrate for respiration. Typically, all carbohydrates are first converted into glucose before entering respiration.
Other substrates can also be respired, but they do not enter at the first step of the respiratory pathway; they enter at different points.
CarbohydratesUsually converted to glucose before respiration
FatsBroken into glycerol + fatty acids first
Fatty acidsDegraded to acetyl CoA → enters pathway
GlycerolConverted to PGAL → enters pathway
ProteinsProteases → amino acids; after deamination enter as pyruvate/acetyl CoA or within Krebs’ cycle
Idea to remember: Different nutrients “join the highway” (respiratory pathway) at different entry ramps: fatty acids at acetyl CoA, glycerol at PGAL, amino acids at several points depending on structure.

Why respiration is called an amphibolic pathway

Catabolism = breakdown processes in living organisms. Anabolism = synthesis processes in living organisms.
Respiration is traditionally viewed as catabolic because it breaks down substrates to release energy. However, respiratory intermediates are also withdrawn for biosynthesis.
Fatty acids breakdown
→
Acetyl CoA enters respiratory pathway
Fatty acids synthesis need
→
Acetyl CoA withdrawn from respiratory pathway
  • Respiratory pathway provides intermediates used for synthesis (anabolism).
  • Respiratory pathway also accepts substrates for breakdown (catabolism).
Conclusion / named classification: Because the respiratory pathway operates in both anabolism and catabolism, it is best termed an amphibolic pathway (not purely catabolic).
Exam phrasing tip: Write: “Respiratory pathway is amphibolic because respiratory intermediates are common links between breakdown and synthesis (e.g., acetyl CoA used both in fatty acid breakdown and withdrawn for fatty acid synthesis).”

Respiratory Quotient (RQ)

Definition and formula

During aerobic respiration, O2 is consumed and CO2 is released. The ratio of volume of CO2 evolved to volume of O2 consumed is called the Respiratory Quotient (RQ) (or respiratory ratio).
RQ = (volume of CO2 evolved) / (volume of O2 consumed)
RQ depends on the type of respiratory substrate being oxidised.

RQ for carbohydrates (complete oxidation)

When carbohydrates are completely oxidised, equal volumes of CO2 and O2 are produced/consumed.
C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O + Energy RQ = 6/6 = 1.0

RQ for fats (example: tripalmitin)

When fats are respiratory substrates, RQ < 1.
2C51H98O6 (Tripalmitin) + 145O2 → 102CO2 + 98H2O + energy RQ = 102/145 ≈ 0.7
Must-know value: RQ for fats is less than 1; for tripalmitin it is approximately 0.7.

RQ for proteins

  • When proteins are used as respiratory substrates, RQ ≈ 0.9.

RQ in real living organisms (mixed substrates)

In living organisms, respiratory substrates are often more than one; pure proteins or pure fats are never used as respiratory substrates.
