BIOMOLECULES

Biomolecules: Chemical Composition of Living Organisms (Intro)

Central idea: Living organisms show wide diversity, but at the chemical level we ask: Are living and non-living matter made of the same elements/compounds?
Think of it like comparing the “ingredient list” of a plant/animal vs the earth’s crust — the ingredients overlap, but the amounts change.
  • Elemental analysis comparison: If we analyse plant tissue, animal tissue, or microbial paste, we find elements like C, H, O, etc., and their content per unit mass.
  • If we do the same for earth’s crust (non-living), we also get a similar list of elements.
Exam point: In absolute terms, there is no unique element found only in living matter — all elements present in earth’s crust are also present in living tissues. Key difference is quantitative: Living organisms have a higher relative abundance of carbon (C) and hydrogen (H) compared with earth’s crust.
A Comparison of Elements Present in Non-living and Living Matter
  • Hydrogen (H): Earth’s crust 0.14% ; Human body 9.5%
  • Carbon (C): Earth’s crust 0.03% ; Human body 18.5%
  • Oxygen (O): Earth’s crust 46.6% ; Human body 65.0%
  • Nitrogen (N): Earth’s crust very little ; Human body 3.3%
  • Sulphur (S): Earth’s crust 0.03% ; Human body 0.3%
  • Sodium (Na): Earth’s crust 2.8% ; Human body 0.2%
  • Calcium (Ca): Earth’s crust 3.6% ; Human body 1.5%
  • Magnesium (Mg): Earth’s crust 2.1% ; Human body 0.1%
  • Silicon (Si): Earth’s crust 27.7% ; Human body negligible
Common mistake: Writing “living things contain different elements than non-living.” Correct statement: elements largely overlap; proportions differ (especially C and H are higher in living tissues).

9.1 How to Analyse Chemical Composition?

Acid extraction: separating acid-soluble pool and acid-insoluble fraction

Chemical analysis approach (basic school-level outline):
Take living tissue (e.g., vegetable piece / liver)
→
Grind in trichloroacetic acid (Cl3CCOOH) using mortar-pestle
→
Thick slurry formed
→
Strain through cheesecloth/cotton
→
Two fractions obtained
Filtrate= acid-soluble pool (contains many small, soluble organic compounds)
Retentate= acid-insoluble fraction (material retained on cloth; insoluble components)
Acid-soluble pool: Scientists have found thousands of organic compounds in it (small molecular weight constituents are represented later in Figure 9.1).
“Filtrate” = what passes through the cloth. “Retentate” = what gets retained on the cloth.

Isolation and purification (what is done after extraction)

To identify a particular organic compound from a tissue sample:
  • Extract the compounds from tissue.
  • Apply separation techniques repeatedly until one compound is separated from all others.
  • This is called isolation and purification of the compound.
  • Then apply analytical techniques to determine the molecular formula and the probable structure.
Exam phrasing: “Separation till pure” = isolation + purification. “Analytical techniques” then give molecular formula + probable structure (not just name).

Biomolecules vs inorganic constituents: how do we detect inorganic matter?

Biomolecules: “All the carbon compounds that we get from living tissues” are called biomolecules. But living tissues also contain inorganic elements and compounds.
Destructive test for inorganic content (wet weight → dry weight → ash):
Weigh fresh tissue = wet weight
→
Dry it: water evaporates
→
Remaining = dry weight
→
Burn completely: carbon compounds oxidise to gases (CO2, H2O vapour)
→
Residue left = ash
Ash: The inorganic residue remaining after complete burning of the dried tissue; contains inorganic elements (e.g., Ca, Mg).
  • Elemental analysis reports elemental composition (H, O, Cl, C, etc.).
  • Compound analysis identifies the organic and inorganic constituents present in living tissues.
  • Inorganic compounds like sulphate, phosphate, etc., can also occur in the acid-soluble fraction.
Burning removes carbon-based stuff as gases — what’s left (ash) is your clue for inorganic minerals.

Representative inorganic constituents of living tissues

Representative inorganic ions and compounds found in living tissues:
  • Sodium: Na+
  • Potassium: K+
  • Calcium: Ca++
  • Magnesium: Mg++
  • Water: H2O
  • Examples of inorganic compounds: NaCl, CaCO3, PO43−, SO42−
Do not confuse: “acid-soluble pool” is not “only organic”; it can also contain inorganic ions/compounds (e.g., phosphate, sulphate).

Biology doesn’t group molecules by “functional groups” (aldehydes/ketones/etc.) the way chemistry often does — instead it clubs them by biological role + biomolecule class.
Biological categories used here:

Amino acids

Definition: Amino acids are organic compounds containing an amino group (–NH2) and an acidic group (–COOH) as substituents on the same carbon (the α-carbon). Therefore, they are called α-amino acids and are substituted methanes.
α-carbon has four substituents occupying its four valencies:
  • Hydrogen (H)
  • Carboxyl group (–COOH)
  • Amino group (–NH2)
  • Variable R group (determines the specific amino acid)
Protein amino acids: Many amino acids exist, but those occurring in proteins are only 20 types (proteinaceous amino acids).
  • Glycine: R = hydrogen
  • Alanine: R = methyl (–CH3)
  • Serine: R = hydroxy methyl (–CH2OH)
Basis of amino-acid properties: The chemical and physical properties depend on the amino group, carboxyl group, and the R group.
Classification based on number of amino and carboxyl groups:
  • Acidic amino acids: e.g., glutamic acid
  • Basic amino acids: e.g., lysine
  • Neutral amino acids: e.g., valine
Aromatic amino acids: tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan
Ionizable nature: –NH2 and –COOH groups are ionizable, so amino acid structure changes with pH. Zwitterion: The form (labelled B in the text) in which amino acid exists with both positive and negative charges (internal salt form) is the zwitterionic form.
Exam trap: “Amino acids change structure with pH” is due to ionization of –NH2 and –COOH. Mention zwitterion explicitly when asked about ionic forms.

Lipids

Lipids: Generally water insoluble biomolecules.

Fatty acids (simple lipids)

Fatty acid structure: A carboxyl group (–COOH) attached to an R group (hydrocarbon chain).
  • R group could be methyl (–CH3), ethyl (–C2H5), or longer chains with higher number of –CH2 groups.
  • Chain length range given: 1 carbon to 19 carbons in the R group (as described).
  • Palmitic acid: 16 carbons total (including the carboxyl carbon).
  • Arachidonic acid: 20 carbons total (including the carboxyl carbon).
Saturation classification:
  • Saturated fatty acids: no double bond
  • Unsaturated fatty acids: one or more C=C double bonds

Glycerol and glycerides (fats and oils)

Glycerol: A simple lipid; chemically trihydroxy propane. Glycerides: Many lipids contain both glycerol + fatty acids where fatty acids are esterified with glycerol.

Fats vs oils (basis: melting point):
  • Oils: lower melting point (example: gingelly oil), so remain liquid even in winters.
  • Fats: comparatively higher melting point (hence more solid under similar conditions).
Do not define fats/oils by source (plant/animal). In this text, distinction is explicitly by melting point.

Phospholipids and complex lipids

Phospholipids: Lipids that contain phosphorus and a phosphorylated organic compound.
  • Location: Found in the cell membrane.
  • Example: Lecithin.
  • Complex lipids: Some tissues, especially neural tissues, contain lipids with more complex structures.

Nitrogen bases, nucleosides, nucleotides, and nucleic acids

Heterocyclic ring compounds: Living organisms contain many carbon compounds with heterocyclic rings, including nitrogen bases.
Nitrogen bases listed: adenine, guanine, cytosine, uracil, thymine
Nucleoside: Nitrogen base attached to a sugar. Nucleotide: Nucleoside + phosphate group esterified to the sugar.
Nucleosidesadenosine, guanosine, thymidine, uridine, cytidine
Nucleotidesadenylic acid, thymidylic acid, guanylic acid, uridylic acid, cytidylic acid
  • DNA and RNA consist of nucleotides only.
  • Function: DNA and RNA function as genetic material.
Quick distinction for exams: “-side” (nucleoside) = base + sugar; “-tide” (nucleotide) = base + sugar + phosphate.

9.2 Primary and Secondary Metabolites

Metabolites = biomolecules (organic compounds) found in living organisms (reason for this naming is discussed later in the chapter). Living organisms contain thousands of compounds (small + big) that chemists can isolate, determine structure of, and sometimes synthesise.

Primary metabolites

Primary metabolites are the common categories of compounds present in animal tissues (as in Fig. 9.1 of the chapter). They have identifiable functions and known roles in normal physiological processes.

Secondary metabolites

Secondary metabolites = compounds (in addition to primary metabolites) found especially in plant, fungal and microbial cells. We currently do not understand the role/function of all secondary metabolites in their host organisms.
Despite unclear roles in the host, many secondary metabolites are extremely valuable for human welfare (e.g., rubber, drugs, spices, scents, pigments). Some also have ecological importance.
Think of secondary metabolites like “extra add-ons” in plants/microbes: not always needed for basic survival steps, but super useful for us (medicine, flavour, colour) and for ecology (interaction/defence).
PigmentsCarotenoids; Anthocyanins
AlkaloidsMorphine; Codeine
TerpenoidsMonoterpenes; Diterpenes
Essential oilsLemon grass oil
ToxinsAbrin; Ricin
LectinsConcanavalin A
DrugsVinblastin; Curcumin
Polymeric substancesRubber; Gums; Cellulose
Exam trap: Secondary does not mean “unimportant.” It means “not part of the universal core set” and often with unknown roles in the host but major human/ecological uses.

9.3 Biomacromolecules

Acid soluble pool vs acid insoluble fraction (by molecular weight and composition)

Acid soluble pool: compounds with molecular weights roughly 18 to ~800 daltons (Da). Acid insoluble fraction: contains only four classes of organic compounds:
Proteinsacid insoluble
Nucleic acidsacid insoluble
Polysaccharidesacid insoluble
Lipidsacid insoluble fraction
Except for lipids, these have molecular weights typically ≥ 10,000 Da.
Thus, biomolecules are grouped into:
  • Micromolecules (small): molecular weight < 1000 Da; generally in acid soluble pool.
  • Macromolecules / biomacromolecules (large): found in acid insoluble fraction.

Why lipids appear in the “macromolecular (acid insoluble)” fraction

Lipids individually are small (usually not exceeding ~800 Da), so by size alone they are not true macromolecules. They still occur in the acid insoluble fraction because they are present as membrane structures (cell membrane and other membranes).
Tissue grinding disrupts cells
→
Membranes break into fragments
→
Fragments form vesicles (not water soluble)
→
Vesicles separate with acid insoluble pool
Why “insoluble”? Membrane lipids clump into vesicles (tiny bubbles) instead of dissolving like small sugars/amino acids.
Write clearly in exams: Lipids are not strictly macromolecules; they appear in the insoluble fraction due to aggregation into membranes/vesicles, not due to large individual molecular mass.

What the two fractions represent

  • Acid soluble pool ≈ roughly the cytoplasmic composition.
  • Acid insoluble fraction = macromolecules from cytoplasm + organelles.
  • Together, they represent the entire chemical composition of living tissues/organisms.

Average composition of cells (abundance-based summary)

From abundance viewpoint, water is the most abundant chemical in living organisms.

9.4 Proteins

Basic nature: polypeptides and peptide bonds

Proteins are polypeptides: linear chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds (as depicted in Fig. 9.3).

Proteins as polymers: heteropolymer vs homopolymer

A protein is a polymer made from 20 types of amino acids (e.g., alanine, cysteine, proline, tryptophan, lysine, etc.). Therefore, proteins are heteropolymers (different monomers).
Homopolymer = polymer with only one type of monomer repeating ‘n’ times. Heteropolymer = polymer containing more than one type of monomer (proteins fit here).
HOMO/HETERO
HOMO — same monomer repeats
HETERO — different monomers combine (like proteins)

Essential vs non-essential amino acids (nutrition linkage)

  • Essential amino acids: body cannot synthesise; must come through diet/food.
  • Non-essential amino acids: body can make.
  • Dietary proteins are the source of essential amino acids.

Functions of proteins + examples

Proteins perform many roles, including transport across membranes, defence, hormones, enzymes, receptors, etc.

9.7 Structure of Proteins

“Structure” means different things across disciplines

  • Inorganic chemistry: structure often refers to molecular formula (e.g., NaCl, MgCl2).
  • Organic chemistry: structure often shown as a 2D representation (e.g., benzene, naphthalene).
  • Physics: emphasises 3D views of molecular structure.
  • Biology (proteins): describes protein structure at four levels.

Secondary Structure: Local Folding (α-helix and other forms)

A protein “thread” (polypeptide chain) does not stay as a long, rigid rod — it undergoes folding. Secondary structure = folding of regions of the polypeptide into regular patterns such as a helix (like a revolving staircase) and other forms.
In proteins, only right-handed helices are observed. Only some portions of the chain form helices; other regions fold into other secondary structures.
Visual cue: not the whole chain is a spring—only segments coil; the rest makes other folds.
  • α-helix: helical folding of part of the chain (staircase-like).
  • Other secondary forms: additional folds in non-helical regions (collectively included under secondary structure).
Figure 9.3: Various levels of Protein Structure
Tertiary structure = the long protein chain folds upon itself (like a hollow woollen ball), producing an overall three-dimensional structure.
Tertiary structure is absolutely necessary for the many biological activities of proteins.
Think: a thread (primary sequence) is useless as a “tool” until it folds into a specific 3D shape—this final shape enables function.

Quaternary Structure: Assembly of Subunits

Some proteins consist of an assembly of more than one polypeptide (subunits). Quaternary structure = the architecture/arrangement of these folded subunits with respect to each other (e.g., a linear string of spheres, spheres stacked like a cube/plate, etc.).
Example (Adult human haemoglobin, Hb): has 4 subunits arranged as 2 α + 2 β, where the two α subunits are identical to each other and the two β subunits are identical to each other.
PrimaryAmino-acid sequence; has N-terminus and C-terminus
SecondaryLocal folding (e.g., right-handed α-helix; other folded regions)
TertiaryOverall 3D folding of one polypeptide (woollen-ball-like)
QuaternaryArrangement of multiple folded subunits (e.g., Hb = 2α + 2β)
Exam trap: Don’t confuse tertiary (3D of a single chain) with quaternary (assembly of multiple subunits).

9.8 Enzymes

Nature of Enzymes; Ribozymes

Almost all enzymes are proteins. Some nucleic acids can also behave like enzymes; these are called ribozymes.
An enzyme, like any protein, has:
  • Primary structure (amino-acid sequence)
  • Secondary structure
  • Tertiary structure

Active Site (from Tertiary Structure)

In the tertiary structure, the polypeptide chain criss-crosses forming crevices/pockets. Active site = one such crevice or pocket into which the substrate fits.
Tertiary folding
→
pockets/crevices form
→
active site
→
substrate fits
Enzymes catalyse reactions at a high rate specifically via their active site.

Enzymes vs Inorganic Catalysts; Thermal Stability

  • Inorganic catalysts: generally work efficiently at high temperatures and high pressures.
  • Enzymes: get damaged at high temperatures (e.g., above 40°C).
Enzymes from organisms living at extremely high temperatures (e.g., hot vents, sulphur springs) can remain stable and catalytically active even at 80°–90°C. Thermal stability is an important quality of enzymes from thermophilic organisms.
Don’t treat “enzymes denature above 40°C” as absolute—thermophilic enzymes are key exceptions and may remain active up to 80–90°C.

9.8.1 Chemical Reactions

Physical Change vs Chemical Reaction

Physical change: change in shape without breaking bonds (physical process). Also physical: change in state of matter (e.g., ice → water; water → vapour). Chemical reaction: bonds are broken and new bonds are formed during transformation.
Physical changeShape changes; bonds not broken
Physical change (state)ice → water; water → vapour
Chemical reactionBonds break + new bonds form
  • Inorganic reaction example: Ba(OH)2 + H2SO4 → BaSO4 + 2H2O
  • Organic reaction example: hydrolysis of starch into glucose

Rate of a Physical/Chemical Process

Rate = amount of product formed per unit time.
rate = δP/δt
Rate can also be called velocity if the direction is specified.

Temperature Dependence (Rule of Thumb)

General rule of thumb: the rate doubles or decreases by half for every 10°C change in temperature in either direction (other factors also influence rate).

Catalysed vs Uncatalysed Reactions; Carbonic Anhydrase Example

Catalysed reactions proceed at rates vastly higher than uncatalysed ones.
CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 Enzyme: Carbonic anhydrase (present within the cytoplasm)
  • Without enzyme: very slow; about 200 molecules of H2CO3 formed in 1 hour.
  • With carbonic anhydrase: about 600,000 molecules per second.
  • Overall acceleration: about 10 million times.
When quoting enzyme “power,” include the numbers + conclusion: catalysis can increase rate by orders of magnitude (here ~107).

Metabolic pathway: a multistep chemical reaction where each step is catalysed by the same enzyme complex or by different enzymes.

9.8.2 How do Enzymes bring about such High Rates of Chemical Conversions?

Substrate, Product, and the Need for Binding at the Active Site

Substrate (S): the chemical that is converted into product. Product (P): the chemical formed from the substrate. Enzymes (3D proteins with an active site) convert S → P.
S → P
Before any chemistry happens, the substrate must:
  • Diffuse towards the enzyme’s active site
  • Bind within the cleft/pocket (active site)
Think of the active site like a specific “parking spot” — the substrate has to arrive and park correctly before the reaction can proceed.

ES Complex and Transition State Formation

Binding causes obligatory formation of an enzyme–substrate (ES) complex (E = enzyme). This complex is transient. While substrate is bound at the active site, a new structure called the transition state structure is formed. Then the required bond breaking/making occurs, and the product is released from the active site.
Substrate diffuses to active site
→
ES complex forms (transient)
→
Transition state structure
→
Bonds break/make
→
Product(s) released
Sequence to write in exams: active site → ES complex → transition state → product release. Missing “transition state” or calling ES complex stable are common errors.

Concept of Activation Energy (Energy Profile of a Reaction)

Figure 9.4: Concept of activation energy
Reaction pathway: During conversion of substrate (S) to product (P), the molecule passes through multiple altered structural states. Most intermediate structural states are unstable; stability depends on the energy status of that structure.
Energy profile graph interpretation (as in the activation energy diagram):
  • Y-axis = potential energy content
  • X-axis = progress of reaction / progression of structural transformation through the transition state
Transition state: a high-energy, unstable structural state that must be reached during S → P conversion.
Activation energy (Ea) = difference in average energy content of substrate (S) and the transition state.
Exothermic vs endothermic (based on relative energy levels of S and P):
  • If P is at a lower energy level than S → reaction is exothermic (energy released).
  • In an exothermic reaction, one need not supply energy (heating) to form product.
  • Even if a reaction is exothermic/spontaneous OR endothermic/energy requiring, S must still pass through the high-energy transition state.
Role of enzymes: Enzymes bring down the energy barrier (lower Ea), making conversion of S → P easier by reducing the required transition state energy.
Exam pitfall: Do not confuse “exothermic/spontaneous” with “no activation energy.” All reactions require passing through a transition state; enzymes reduce the barrier, they do not eliminate the need for a transition state.

Nature of Enzyme Action (Catalytic Cycle)

Each enzyme (E) has a substrate-binding site (active site region) that binds its substrate (S) to form a highly reactive enzyme–substrate complex (ES).
ES complex is essential for catalysis: formation of ES is required for the enzyme to catalyse the reaction effectively.
Overall sequence with intermediate complexes: E + S → ES → EP → E + P Where EP is the enzyme–product complex and enzyme emerges unchanged.
Eenzyme
Ssubstrate

→
2. Binding induces enzyme shape change (tighter fit)

4. Products released; free enzyme ready again
  • ES is short-lived and converts to product via EP.
  • Enzyme is not consumed: after product release, it can bind another substrate molecule and repeat the cycle.
Exam phrasing: Step 2 describes induced fit (substrate binding induces enzyme to alter shape, fitting more tightly).

Temperature and pH

  • Enzymes function effectively only in a narrow range of temperature and pH.
  • Each enzyme has an optimum temperature and an optimum pH where activity is highest.
  • Activity declines below and above the optimum values.

Why extremes reduce activity (exam explanation): Low temperature can keep the enzyme temporarily inactive (activity reduced but enzyme preserved), whereas high temperature causes denaturation of proteins, destroying enzymatic activity.
Distinguish effects: Low temperature usually causes reversible inactivity; high temperature commonly causes irreversible loss due to denaturation.

Vmax: maximum velocity of enzymatic reaction beyond which velocity does not increase even if substrate concentration increases further.

Chemical Regulation and Inhibition

Some chemicals bind enzymes and regulate activity. If binding shuts off enzyme activity, it is inhibition, and the chemical is an inhibitor.
Competitive inhibition: inhibitor closely resembles the substrate in molecular structure and competes with it for the substrate-binding site of the enzyme.

→

→

→
Enzyme action declines

  • Example: inhibition of succinic dehydrogenase by malonate, which closely resembles succinate in structure.
  • Application: competitive inhibitors are often used to control bacterial pathogens.
Exam wording: Competitive inhibitor must be described as structurally similar to substrate and acting by competition for substrate-binding site.

Classification and Nomenclature of Enzymes

Thousands of enzymes are classified based on the type of reaction catalysed. Enzymes are divided into 6 classes, each with 4–13 subclasses, and are named using a four-digit number (enzyme commission style nomenclature).
Oxidoreductases / DehydrogenasesCatalyse oxidation–reduction between two substrates
TransferasesTransfer a group (other than H) from one substrate to another
HydrolasesCatalyse hydrolysis of multiple bond types
LyasesRemove groups without hydrolysis; often leave double bonds
IsomerasesInterconvert isomers (optical/geometric/positional)
LigasesJoin two compounds by forming new bonds (often energy-coupled)

Oxidoreductases / Dehydrogenases

General reaction: S reduced + S′ oxidised → S oxidised + S′ reduced

Transferases

General reaction (group transfer, G ≠ hydrogen): S–G + S′ → S + S′–G

Hydrolases

Catalyse hydrolysis of:
  • ester bonds
  • ether bonds
  • peptide bonds
  • glycosidic bonds
  • C–C bonds
  • C–halide bonds
  • P–N bonds

Lyases

Catalyse removal of groups from substrates by mechanisms other than hydrolysis, typically leaving double bonds.

Isomerases

Catalyse interconversion of:
  • optical isomers
  • geometric isomers
  • positional isomers

Ligases

Catalyse linking together of two compounds; examples of bonds formed include:
  • C–O
  • C–S
  • C–N
  • P–O

Co-factors (Apoenzyme, Types, and Roles)

Some enzymes require non-protein constituents called co-factors to become catalytically active.
Apoenzyme: the protein portion of an enzyme (inactive by itself in enzymes that require cofactors).

Three kinds of cofactors:
Prosthetic groupOrganic; tightly bound to apoenzyme
Co-enzymeOrganic; transient association during catalysis; used across multiple reactions
Metal ionRequired by some enzymes; forms coordination bonds at active site and with substrate

Prosthetic Groups

Prosthetic group: an organic compound that is tightly bound to the apoenzyme; often forms part of the active site.
  • Example: In peroxidase and catalase (enzymes breaking down hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen), haem is the prosthetic group and is part of the active site.

Co-enzymes

Co-enzymes: organic compounds that associate with apoenzymes transiently, usually during catalysis; can act as cofactors for many different enzyme-catalysed reactions.
Vitamin link: essential chemical components of many coenzymes are vitamins.
  • NAD and NADP contain the vitamin niacin.

Metal Ions

Some enzymes require metal ions for activity. Metal ions form coordination bonds with side chains at the active site and simultaneously form one or more coordination bonds with the substrate.
  • Example: Zinc is a cofactor for the proteolytic enzyme carboxypeptidase.
Functional proof of importance: catalytic activity is lost when the cofactor is removed, showing cofactors have a crucial role in enzyme catalysis.
Exam trap: Apoenzyme ≠ complete enzyme. In cofactor-requiring enzymes, apoenzyme alone is inactive; activity requires the cofactor (prosthetic group/coenzyme/metal ion).

BIOMOLECULES (Wrap-up: Macromolecules & Their Functions)

Levels of Protein Structure

Proteins are organised structurally at four levels:
PrimaryLinear sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain
SecondaryLocal folding patterns (e.g., α-helix, β-pleated sheet) mainly via H-bonds
TertiaryOverall 3D folding of a single polypeptide; forms functional domains/active sites
QuaternaryAssociation of ≥2 polypeptide subunits into a functional protein complex
Exam tip: When asked “What is tertiary structure?”, state: the complete 3D conformation of a single polypeptide chain (not the amino-acid sequence—that is primary structure).

Nucleic Acids: Role in Cells

Nucleic acids serve as the genetic material. They carry hereditary information and are passed from parental generation to progeny.
Core exam statement: Nucleic acids store and transmit hereditary information.

Polysaccharides: Structural & Storage Roles

Polysaccharides function in:
  • Structural support: components of cell walls in plants and fungi
  • Structural protection: component of exoskeleton of arthropods
  • Energy storage: stored reserve carbohydrates
  • Starch: storage polysaccharide in many plants
  • Glycogen: storage polysaccharide in animals (and also in some other organisms)
Don’t mix up roles: “Cell wall/exoskeleton” points to structural polysaccharides, while “starch/glycogen” points to storage polysaccharides.

Proteins perform a wide variety of cellular functions, including:

Enzymes and Ribozymes

Enzymes are proteins that catalyse biochemical reactions in cells. Ribozymes are nucleic acids that possess catalytic power (i.e., RNA can also act as a catalyst).

Key Properties of Proteinaceous Enzymes

Proteinaceous enzymes (protein enzymes) show the following properties:
  • Substrate specificity: act on specific substrate(s)
  • Optimum temperature: maximal activity occurs at a particular temperature
  • Optimum pH: maximal activity occurs at a particular pH
  • Denaturation at high temperatures: high heat disrupts protein structure, reducing/abolishing activity
  • Lower activation energy: reduce the energy barrier for the reaction
  • Increase reaction rate greatly: catalysis markedly speeds up biochemical reactions
Activation energy principle (conceptual): Enzymes lower the activation energy of a reaction, therefore increase the reaction rate (without being consumed in the process).
Common exam mistake: Writing “enzymes increase activation energy” is incorrect. They lower activation energy and thus enhance the reaction rate.
Real-life analogy: Activation energy is like a hill that reactants must cross. An enzyme provides an alternate easier path (a lower pass), so crossing becomes faster even though start and end points are unchanged.
