Handwritten Notes

CELL CYCLE AND CELL DIVISION

Cell Cycle

Core idea: All organisms begin life as a single cell. Large multicellular bodies arise because cells repeatedly undergo growth + division.
Think “one starter cell” → repeated copying builds a whole organism (like cloning a document again and again).
Cell reproduction: Every division of one parental cell produces two daughter cells. Daughter cells can grow and divide again, forming a population of cells derived from one original cell.
Cell division is coordinated with:
  • DNA replication (duplication of genome)
  • Cell growth (increase in cytoplasm; continuous overall)
  • Cell division (partitioning of replicated chromosomes and cytoplasm)
These must occur in a coordinated manner so that progeny cells receive intact genomes.
Cell cycle (definition): The sequence of events in which a cell:
duplicates its genome
→
synthesises other cell constituents
→
divides into two daughter cells
Control: The events of chromosome replication and distribution during division are under genetic control.
Key timing fact: Although cell growth is broadly continuous, DNA synthesis occurs only during one specific stage of the cell cycle (the S phase).

Phases of Cell Cycle

A typical eukaryotic cell cycle (example: human cells in culture) completes one division in about 24 hours, but this varies widely:
  • Human cells in culture: ~24 h per cycle
  • Yeast: ~90 minutes per cycle
Two basic phases of the cell cycle:
InterphaseBetween two successive M phases; preparation for division
M PhaseMitosis phase; actual cell division
Duration emphasis (human ~24 h cycle):
  • M phase (cell division proper): ~1 hour
  • Interphase: >95% of total cell-cycle duration
Exam point: Interphase is often called a “resting phase” but it is highly active—the cell grows and replicates DNA in an orderly manner.
M Phase (what it includes): Starts with nuclear division and usually ends with cytoplasmic division.
KaryokinesisSeparation of daughter chromosomes (nuclear division)
CytokinesisDivision of cytoplasm to form two cells

Interphase Subphases (G1, S, G2)

Interphase is divided into three phases:
G1 phaseGap 1: growth; no DNA replication
S phaseSynthesis: DNA replication; DNA amount doubles
G2 phaseGap 2: proteins made for mitosis; growth continues

G1 phase (Gap 1)

Position: Interval between mitosis and initiation of DNA replication.
  • Cell is metabolically active
  • Cell continuously grows
  • No DNA replication occurs
G1 = “grow first” (build up cell materials before copying DNA).

S phase (Synthesis phase)

Defining event: DNA synthesis/replication occurs.
DNA content change across S phase: If initial DNA amount is 2C, after replication it becomes 4C.
Chromosome number does NOT increase in S phase:
  • If the cell is diploid (2n) in G1, it remains 2n even after S phase.
  • Only the amount of DNA doubles; chromosome count stays constant.
Animal cell-specific note during S phase:
  • DNA replication begins in the nucleus.
  • Centriole duplicates in the cytoplasm.

G2 phase (Gap 2)

  • Proteins are synthesised in preparation for mitosis
  • Cell growth continues
G2 = “gear-up” phase: final prep before the big split (mitosis).

Quiescent stage (G0)

G0 (quiescent stage): Some adult animal cells exit G1 and enter an inactive stage where they remain metabolically active but do not proliferate. Re-entry: Cells can resume division if called upon depending on the organism’s requirement.
  • Non-dividing example: many adult heart cells do not appear to divide.
  • Occasional division: many cells divide only as needed (e.g., to replace cells lost due to injury or cell death).
Common exam trap: “Resting” does not mean inactive. Interphase is active; G0 is non-proliferative (though metabolically active).

Mitosis occurrence in animals vs plants (ploidy context)

  • Animals: Mitosis is generally seen only in diploid somatic cells.
  • Exception (animals): Some haploid cells can divide mitotically (e.g., male honey bees).
  • Plants: Mitotic divisions can occur in both haploid and diploid cells.
Link to alternation of generations: In plants, haploid stages (gametophytic phase) can show mitosis as part of their growth and development.

M Phase

M phase: The most dramatic part of the cell cycle, involving major reorganisation of virtually all cell components. Equational division: Because the number of chromosomes in parent and progeny cells remains the same, mitosis is called equational division.
Stage boundaries: Mitosis is described in stages for convenience, but cell division is a progressive process; boundaries between stages are not perfectly sharp.

Prophase

Position in cycle: Prophase (first stage of karyokinesis) follows the S and G2 phases of interphase.
What chromosomes look like before prophase: In S and G2, newly formed DNA molecules are not distinct—they remain intertwined.
Defining change of prophase: Initiation of condensation of chromosomal material.
  • Chromosomal material becomes untangled during chromatin condensation.
  • Chromatin condenses into compact mitotic chromosomes.
Completion of prophase: characteristic events (use these as checkpoints in descriptions/diagrams):
  • Mitotic chromosomes become clearly visible, each composed of two chromatids attached at the centromere.
  • Centrosomes (duplicated earlier in interphase) begin moving to opposite poles.
  • Each centrosome radiates microtubules called asters.
  • The two asters + spindle fibres together form the mitotic apparatus.
Analogy: Think of chromatin as a long thread. In prophase, the thread is wound into compact, manageable “packages” (chromosomes) so it can be moved and separated accurately.
Exam wording precision: In prophase, chromosomes are two chromatids joined at the centromere; chromosome number does not increase because chromatids are still counted as part of the same chromosome until they separate later.

CELL CYCLE AND CELL DIVISION (Mitosis continuation)

Metaphase (10.2.2)

Start of metaphase: marked by complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope, so chromosomes are spread through the cytoplasm. Best stage to study chromosome morphology: chromosome condensation is complete; chromosomes are clearly visible under the microscope.
Metaphase chromosome structure: each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids held together by a centromere. Kinetochore: small disc-shaped structures on the surface of the centromere; they are the attachment sites for spindle fibres.
Metaphase alignment: all chromosomes come to lie at the equator of the cell.
  • One chromatid of each chromosome attaches (via its kinetochore) to spindle fibres from one pole.
  • Its sister chromatid attaches (via its kinetochore) to spindle fibres from the opposite pole.
Metaphase plate: the plane of alignment of chromosomes at metaphase.
Key feature 1Spindle fibres attach to kinetochores of chromosomes
Key feature 2Chromosomes move to spindle equator and align along the metaphase plate via fibres to both poles
Exam tip: Don’t confuse centromere (region joining sister chromatids) with kinetochore (protein/disc-like structure on centromere where spindle fibres attach).

Anaphase (10.2.3)

Onset of anaphase: each chromosome on the metaphase plate splits simultaneously; sister chromatids separate and are now called daughter chromosomes (of the future daughter nuclei).
Movement pattern: daughter chromosomes migrate to opposite poles. As each chromosome moves away from the equatorial plate, the centromere remains directed toward the pole (leading edge), and the arms trail behind.
Key event 1Centromeres split and chromatids separate
Key event 2Chromatids (now daughter chromosomes) move to opposite poles
Common mistake: In anaphase of mitosis, it is the sister chromatids that separate (after centromere split), not homologous chromosomes.

Telophase (10.2.4)

Telophase: final stage of karyokinesis (nuclear division). Chromosomes that reach the poles decondense and lose individuality (no longer visible as distinct chromosomes); chromatin collects at each pole.
  • Chromosomes cluster at opposite spindle poles and their identity as discrete elements is lost.
  • Nuclear envelope develops around each chromosome cluster, forming two daughter nuclei.
  • Nucleolus, Golgi complex, and ER reform.
Link-back detail (often tested): by the end of prophase, Golgi complexes, ER, nucleolus, and nuclear envelope are not visible; in telophase they reappear/reform.

Cytokinesis (10.2.5)

Mitosis completes cell division by:
Karyokinesis (nuclear division)
→
Cytokinesis (cytoplasmic division)
→
Two daughter cells
Cytokinesis: separation of cytoplasm that divides one cell into two daughter cells at the end of mitosis.
Animal cell cytokinesis: occurs by formation of a furrow in the plasma membrane; it deepens and finally joins in the centre, splitting the cytoplasm into two.
Plant cell cytokinesis: different because plant cells have a relatively inextensible cell wall.
  • Wall formation begins in the centre of the cell and grows outward to meet the existing lateral walls.
  • New wall begins as a simple precursor called the cell plate.
  • The cell plate represents the middle lamella between the walls of two adjacent cells.
Organelle distribution during cytoplasmic division: mitochondria and plastids get distributed between the two daughter cells.
Syncytium formation: in some organisms, karyokinesis is not followed by cytokinesis, producing a multinucleate condition called a syncytium.
  • Example: liquid endosperm in coconut.
Exam contrast to memorise: Animal cells divide by a cleavage furrow; plant cells divide by a cell plate (new wall forms from centre outward).

Significance of Mitosis (10.3)

  • Mitosis = equational division: usually restricted to diploid cells (chromosome number is maintained).
  • Exception: in some lower plants and some social insects, haploid cells also divide by mitosis.
Outcome of mitosis: produces diploid daughter cells with identical genetic complement (genetically similar to parent cell).
Growth in multicellular organismsIncrease in cell number occurs due to mitosis
Restores nucleo-cytoplasmic ratioCell growth disturbs nucleus:cytoplasm balance; division restores it
Repair/replacementReplaces worn-out/damaged cells
Continuous plant growthDue to mitosis in meristematic tissues
  • Cell repair/replacement examples: cells of the upper epidermis, cells lining the gut, and blood cells are constantly replaced.
  • Plant growth: mitotic divisions in meristematic tissues—the apical meristem and the lateral cambium—cause continuous growth of plants throughout life.
High-yield phrasing: “Mitosis maintains chromosome number (equational) and produces genetically identical daughter cells; essential for growth, repair, and maintaining nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio.”

MEIOSIS (10.4)

  • Meiosis: specialised cell division in which chromosome number is reduced to half, producing haploid daughter cells from specialised diploid cells.
  • Biological context: sexual reproduction involves fusion of two haploid gametes (each with a complete haploid set of chromosomes).
Life-cycle significance:
Meiosis
→
Haploid phase ensured
→
Fertilisation restores diploid phase
Where it occurs: during gametogenesis in plants and animals, producing haploid gametes.

Key features of meiosis

Two divisions, one replicationTwo sequential cycles: meiosis I and meiosis II; only one DNA replication cycle
Replication timingMeiosis I begins after parental chromosomes replicate in S phase to form sister chromatids
Synapsis + recombinationHomologous chromosomes pair; recombination occurs between non-sister chromatids
End productsFour haploid cells formed at end of meiosis II
Common mistake: DNA replicates once (before meiosis I), not before meiosis II.

Phases of meiosis (grouping of events)

Meiotic events are grouped into phases under two divisions:
Meiosis IProphase I → Metaphase I → Anaphase I → Telophase I
Meiosis IIProphase II → Metaphase II → Anaphase II → Telophase II

Meiosis I

Meiosis I (first meiotic division) = first division where homologous chromosomes separate, reducing chromosome number by half (reductional division).
Prophase I is longer + more complex than mitotic prophase.

Leptotene

  • Chromosomes become gradually visible under the light microscope.
  • Chromosome compaction continues throughout leptotene.

Zygotene

Synapsis = pairing/association of chromosomes during zygotene.
Homologous chromosomes = the paired chromosomes that synapse (one from each parent).
Synaptonemal complex = complex structure formed along paired homologous chromosomes during synapsis (seen in electron micrographs).
Bivalent / tetrad = complex formed by a pair of synapsed homologous chromosomes (four chromatids total). More clearly visible at the next stage.
Leptotene + zygotene are relatively short-lived compared with pachytene.

Pachytene

  • The four chromatids of each bivalent become distinct and clearly appear as tetrads.
Recombination nodules appear in pachytene = sites where crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
Crossing over = exchange of genetic material between two homologous chromosomes (specifically between non-sister chromatids).
Crossing over is enzyme-mediated — enzyme: recombinase.
Crossing over causes recombination of genetic material.
  • Recombination between homologous chromosomes is completed by the end of pachytene, leaving homologues linked at the sites of crossing over.

Diplotene

The start of diplotene is recognised by:
  • Dissolution of the synaptonemal complex
  • Recombined homologous chromosomes begin to separate
  • They remain attached only at crossover sites
Chiasmata (singular: chiasma) = X-shaped visible structures where homologous chromosomes remain connected at crossing over sites during diplotene.
Special case: In oocytes of some vertebrates, diplotene can last months or years (prolonged arrest stage).

Diakinesis

Final stage of prophase I; marked by:
  • Terminalisation of chiasmata
  • Chromosomes become fully condensed
  • Meiotic spindle assembles to prepare homologous chromosomes for separation
  • By the end: nucleolus disappears and nuclear envelope breaks down
Diakinesis represents the transition to metaphase.

Metaphase I

  • Bivalents align on the equatorial plate.
  • Microtubules from opposite spindle poles attach to the kinetochore of homologous chromosomes.

Anaphase I

  • Homologous chromosomes separate to opposite poles.
  • Sister chromatids remain associated at their centromeres.
Common exam trap: In anaphase I, homologous chromosomes separate; sister chromatids do NOT separate here (centromeres do not split in anaphase I).

Telophase I and Interkinesis

In telophase I:
  • Nuclear membrane and nucleolus reappear
  • Cytokinesis follows, producing a dyad of cells (two cells)
  • Chromosomes may undergo some dispersion but do not reach the extremely extended state of an interphase nucleus.
Interkinesis = stage between meiosis I and meiosis II; generally short-lived.
No replication of DNA occurs during interkinesis.
  • Interkinesis is followed by prophase II, which is much simpler than prophase I.

Meiosis II

Meiosis II resembles a normal mitosis (equational division): sister chromatids separate.

Prophase II

  • Meiosis II begins immediately after cytokinesis, usually before chromosomes have fully elongated.
  • Chromosomes become compact again; by the end of prophase II the nuclear membrane disappears.

Metaphase II

  • Chromosomes align at the equator.
  • Microtubules from opposite poles attach to the kinetochores of sister chromatids.

Anaphase II

  • Begins with simultaneous splitting of the centromere of each chromosome (which held sister chromatids together).
  • Sister chromatids move to opposite poles by shortening of microtubules attached to kinetochores.
Fast distinction: Anaphase I = homologues separate (centromeres intact). Anaphase II = centromeres split and sister chromatids separate.

Telophase II

  • Meiosis ends as chromosomes at each pole are enclosed again by a nuclear envelope.
  • Cytokinesis follows, producing a tetrad of cells i.e., four haploid daughter cells.

Significance of Meiosis

  • Conservation of chromosome number across generations: In sexually reproducing organisms, meiosis reduces chromosome number by half in gametes; fertilisation restores it, maintaining the species-specific chromosome number.
  • Genetic variability: Meiosis increases genetic variability from one generation to the next (linked to recombination/crossing over), and such variations are crucial for evolution.

High-Yield Revision Notes

Why mitosis is called equational division

Equational division = a division in which the chromosome number remains unchanged in the daughter cells compared to the parent cell.
Mitosis is called equational division because:
  • Sister chromatids separate and are distributed equally to two poles.
  • Each daughter cell receives one chromatid of every replicated chromosome.
  • So, if the parent cell is 2N, each daughter cell is also 2N (same chromosome number).
Do not confuse chromosome number with DNA content: after S-phase DNA doubles, but chromosome number does not change until chromatids are counted as separate chromosomes at anaphase.

Stages of the cell cycle where specific events occur

Know the exact stage name linked to each hallmark event.
Chromosomes moved to spindle equatorMetaphase (mitosis)
Centromere splits; chromatids separateAnaphase (mitosis)
Pairing of homologous chromosomesZygotene (Prophase I, meiosis)
Crossing over between homologous chromosomesPachytene (Prophase I, meiosis)
Pairing (synapsis) is a zygotene event, but crossing over occurs after pairing is established, classically in pachytene.

Synapsis, bivalent, chiasmata (definitions + what to draw)

Synapsis: the pairing and close association of homologous chromosomes during zygotene of prophase I (meiosis), aided by formation of the synaptonemal complex. Bivalent: the paired unit formed by two homologous chromosomes during synapsis; since each homolog has two sister chromatids after S-phase, a bivalent contains 4 chromatids (also called a tetrad). Chiasmata (singular: chiasma): the visible X-shaped points where non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes have undergone crossing over; they represent sites of genetic recombination and help hold homologs together until anaphase I.
What your diagram must show in an exam:
  • Two homologous chromosomes aligned side-by-side (a bivalent/tetrad = 4 chromatids).
  • A clear crossing point between non-sister chromatids labeled as chiasma/chiasmata.
  • Indicate that synapsis creates the bivalent (pairing), while crossing over produces chiasmata.
Common error: labeling a chiasma as the centromere. Chiasma = crossover point between chromatids; centromere = chromatid attachment point on the same chromosome.

Cytokinesis in plant cells vs animal cells

Cytokinesis = division of the cytoplasm to form separate daughter cells, following nuclear division (karyokinesis).
Animal cell cytokinesisBy cleavage furrow (contractile ring constricts membrane inward)
Plant cell cytokinesisBy cell plate formation (vesicles fuse at center; new wall forms outward)
Key reason for differencePlant cells have a rigid cell wall; cannot pinch inward like animal cells
Analogy: Animal cells “tie a belt” (furrow constriction). Plant cells “build a partition wall” (cell plate) from the middle.

Examples where four meiotic daughter cells are equal vs unequal in size

Meiosis produces four haploid cells, but their sizes depend on whether cytoplasm is divided equally.
  • Equal-sized four daughter cells: typical of spermatogenesis (forming four spermatids → sperm) in animals.
  • Unequal-sized four products: typical of oogenesis in animals where one large ovum and small polar bodies form due to unequal cytokinesis.
Exam trap: saying “meiosis always makes four equal cells.” Equal vs unequal depends on the organism/tissue and the pattern of cytokinesis (equal in male gamete formation; unequal in female gamete formation in many animals).

Anaphase of mitosis vs Anaphase I of meiosis

This distinction is frequently tested because it explains why mitosis is equational and meiosis I is reductional.
What separates?Mitosis: sister chromatids; Meiosis I: homologous chromosomes
Centromere behaviorMitosis: centromeres split; Meiosis I: centromeres do NOT split
Type of divisionMitosis: equational; Meiosis I: reductional (2N → N)
Chromatid status at poleMitosis: single chromatids become chromosomes; Meiosis I: each chromosome still has two chromatids
Key phrase: Anaphase I separates homologs; Anaphase II separates chromatids. Don’t mix up anaphase (mitosis) with anaphase I (meiosis).

Significance of meiosis

Meiosis is essential for sexual reproduction because it produces haploid cells and generates variation.
  • Reduction of chromosome number (2N → N) ensures that fertilization restores diploidy (N + N → 2N), maintaining a constant species chromosome number across generations.
  • Genetic variation arises due to:
    • Crossing over (recombination) in prophase I.
    • Independent assortment (random segregation of homologous chromosomes) in meiosis I.
  • Helps evolution/adaptation by producing new gene combinations.

Can there be mitosis without DNA replication in S phase?

Normal cell cycle logic: S phase replicates DNA to form sister chromatids, enabling accurate segregation during mitosis.
In the normal cell cycle, mitosis without prior S-phase DNA replication does not occur because:
  • Without replication, chromosomes would not form sister chromatids.
  • Accurate and equal distribution of the genome to two daughter cells would be compromised.
  • Cell-cycle checkpoints typically prevent entry into mitosis if DNA replication is incomplete.

Can there be DNA replication without cell division?

Yes, DNA replication can occur without cell division in certain cases, leading to increased DNA content without cytokinesis/mitosis.
  • This may produce cells with higher DNA content (polyploidy or endoreduplication-like states) where replication repeats but division does not follow.
In exam answers, distinguish clearly between:
  • DNA replication (S phase) increasing DNA content (C)
  • Cell division affecting cell number and distribution of chromosomes/DNA

General pattern in a diploid (2N) somatic cell:
  • G1: chromosomes = 2N; DNA content = 2C.
  • S phase: DNA replicates; chromosomes remain 2N; DNA content increases 2C → 4C.
  • G2: chromosomes = 2N; DNA content = 4C.
  • M phase (key idea):
    • Up to metaphase: still 2N chromosomes with 4C DNA.
    • At anaphase (when sister chromatids separate): each chromatid is counted as a chromosome temporarily, so chromosome number in the single cell is effectively doubled (conceptually), but after cytokinesis each daughter nucleus returns to 2N.
    • After cytokinesis: each daughter cell has 2N chromosomes and 2C DNA.
