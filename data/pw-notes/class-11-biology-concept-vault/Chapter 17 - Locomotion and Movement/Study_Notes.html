LOCOMOTION AND MOVEMENT

Locomotion and Movement

Movement vs Locomotion

Movement = any change in position/state of a body part or the whole organism. Locomotion = a type of voluntary movement that results in a change of place/location (whole body shifts from one place to another).
Link: All locomotions are movements, but all movements are not locomotions.
  • Simple movement example: Streaming of protoplasm in Amoeba.
  • Other movements: Cilia/flagella/tentacles movement; in humans—limbs, jaws, eyelids, tongue, etc.
  • Locomotion examples: Walking, running, climbing, flying, swimming.
Same structure, multiple roles: A locomotory structure need not be “only for locomotion”.
  • Paramoecium: cilia move food through cytopharynx and also help in locomotion.
  • Hydra: tentacles capture prey and can help in locomotion.
  • Humans: limbs help in posture change and locomotion.

In exam answers, do not write “movement = locomotion”. Always state the distinction: locomotion must involve change of place and is described here as voluntary.

Classification of muscles

Muscles are classified using different criteria: location, appearance, and nature of regulation. Based on location, three types are identified: skeletal, visceral, cardiac.
Locationwhere found
Appearancestriated vs nonstriated
Regulationvoluntary vs involuntary

Skeletal muscle (striated, voluntary)

  • Location: closely associated with skeletal components
  • Appearance: striped under microscope → called striated muscle
  • Regulation: under voluntary control of nervous system → called voluntary muscle
  • Functions: mainly locomotion and changes of body posture

Visceral muscle (smooth/nonstriated, involuntary)

  • Location: inner walls of hollow visceral organs (e.g., alimentary canal, reproductive tract)
  • Appearance: no striations; smooth → smooth muscle (also called nonstriated)
  • Regulation: not under voluntary nervous control → involuntary muscle
  • Examples of roles: transportation of food through digestive tract; movement of gametes through genital tract

Cardiac muscle (striated, involuntary)

  • Location: heart
  • Organisation: many cardiac muscle cells assemble in a branching pattern to form cardiac muscle
  • Appearance: striated
  • Regulation: involuntary (nervous system does not control activities directly)
Exam trap: “striated” does not always mean “voluntary”. Cardiac muscle is striated but involuntary.

Myofibrils/myofilaments and the basis of striations

Myofilaments / Myofibrils = numerous parallel filaments in sarcoplasm; responsible for contractile machinery. Striated appearance is due to distribution of two proteins: Actin and Myosin.
ActinThin filaments
MyosinThick filaments
  • Actin filaments are thin filaments.
  • Myosin filaments are thick filaments.
  • Both arranged like rod-like structures, parallel to each other and to the longitudinal axis of myofibrils.
Striations = the “barcode look” from alternating regions of thin vs thick filament distribution.

Bands and lines in a myofibril

I-bandLight; isotropic; contains actin (thin)
A-bandDark; anisotropic; contains myosin (thick)
Z-lineElastic fibre; bisects I-band; thin filaments attach
M-lineMidline of A-band; holds thick filaments together
H-zoneCentral A-band region with only thick filaments (no overlap)
IAZMH
I — I-band = lIght; thIn (actin)
A — A-band = dArk; thick (myosin)
Z — Z-line = thin filaments “Zip” on here (anchors; in I-band centre)
M — M-line = Middle of A-band (thick held together)
H — H-zone = Heavy only (thick only; no overlap)
  • A and I bands alternate throughout the length of myofibrils.
  • Z-line is at the centre of each I-band and anchors thin filaments.
  • M-line is at the middle of A-band and stabilises thick filaments.

Sarcomere: functional unit of contraction

Sarcomere = the portion of a myofibril between two successive Z-lines; it is the functional unit of contraction.

17.2.1 Structure of Contractile Proteins

Figure 17.3: (a) An actin (thin) filament (b) Myosin monomer (Meromyosin)

Actin (thin filament): components and regulation

Each actin (thin) filament consists of:
Two F-actinsHelically wound around each other
G-actinsMonomers that polymerise to form each F-actin
TropomyosinTwo filaments run close to F-actins along their length
TroponinComplex protein at regular intervals on tropomyosin
  • Two F-actins (filamentous actins) helically wound around each other
  • Each F-actin is a polymer of monomeric G-actins (globular actins)
  • Tropomyosin: two filaments run close to F-actins along their length
  • Troponin: complex protein distributed at regular intervals on tropomyosin
Resting state control: A subunit of troponin masks the active binding sites for myosin on the actin filament.
At rest: troponin = “site cover” so myosin can’t grab actin yet.

Myosin (thick filament): meromyosin, HMM/LMM, cross arms, enzyme sites

Each myosin (thick) filament is a polymerised protein made of many monomers called meromyosins.
MeromyosinMonomer unit; many make up one thick filament
HMMHeavy meromyosin = globular head + short arm
LMMLight meromyosin = tail
Cross armProjecting HMM portions from thick filament surface
  • Each meromyosin has:
    • Globular head + short arm = Heavy Meromyosin (HMM)
    • Tail = Light Meromyosin (LMM)
  • Cross arm = projecting HMM (head + short arm) portions that extend outward from the thick filament surface at regular distance and angle.
  • The globular head:
    • acts as an ATPase enzyme
    • has ATP-binding sites
    • has actin-binding (active) sites
Common confusion: “cross arm” refers to the projecting HMM (head + short arm), not the tail (LMM).

Muscle Contraction (Sliding-Filament Mechanism)

Motor unit: a motor neuron + all muscle fibres connected to it. Neuromuscular junction (NMJ) / motor end plate: junction between a motor neuron and the sarcolemma of a muscle fibre.

Troponin (subunit on actin filaments): binding of Ca++ to troponin causes removal of masking of the active sites on actin for myosin binding.

→

  • Cross-bridge: binding of myosin head to actin at exposed active sites (requires energy from ATP hydrolysis).
  • Direction of sliding: actin filaments are pulled towards the centre of the A band.

Exam trap: ATP is required not only for “powering” contraction, but also for breaking the cross-bridge (new ATP binding causes cross-bridge dissociation).

Sarcomere Band Changes During Contraction

During shortening (contraction):
  • I band reduces
  • A band retains its length
Mechanism: Z lines attached to actin are pulled inwards, causing sarcomere shortening.
Analogy: Sliding-filament action is like pulling ropes (actin) inward from both ends toward the middle; the central region (A band) stays the same length while the outer gap (I band) shrinks.

Relaxation depends on: removal of Ca++ from sarcoplasm by pumping it back into sarcoplasmic cisternae, restoring masking of actin filaments.

Reaction Time and Fatigue

Reaction time: varies among muscles (fibres of different muscles respond at different rates). Fatigue: repeated activation can cause lactic acid accumulation due to anaerobic breakdown of glycogen in muscles.
Don’t write: fatigue is due to “lack of oxygen” alone. Here, the stated cause is lactic acid accumulation from anaerobic glycogen breakdown.

Red Fibres vs White Fibres

Myoglobin: red coloured oxygen-storing pigment in muscle.
Red fibresHigh myoglobin → reddish; many mitochondria; use stored O2 for ATP → “aerobic muscles”
White fibresLow myoglobin → pale/whitish; few mitochondria; high sarcoplasmic reticulum; depend mainly on anaerobic energy
  • Red fibres: high myoglobin + many mitochondria → efficient aerobic ATP production.
  • White fibres: less myoglobin + fewer mitochondria but more sarcoplasmic reticulum → rely on anaerobic processes.

17.4 Joints (Introduction)

Joints: essential for all types of movements involving the bony parts of the body; locomotory movements require joints.

Joints (Articulations): Role in Locomotion

Joint (articulation) = a point of contact between bones, or between bone and cartilage.
Muscles generate force
→
Movement occurs through joints
→
Joint acts as a fulcrum
Movability at joints varies depending on different factors; therefore joints are classified based on structure and movement capacity.

Structural Classification of Joints

Fibrous jointsImmovable; bones joined by dense fibrous connective tissue
Cartilaginous jointsSlightly movable; bones joined by cartilage
Synovial jointsFreely movable; fluid-filled synovial cavity present

Fibrous Joints

Fibrous joints do not allow any movement.
  • Example: Flat skull bones fuse end-to-end via dense fibrous connective tissue forming sutures → together form the cranium.

Cartilaginous Joints

Cartilaginous joints: bones are joined with the help of cartilage.
  • Example: Joint between adjacent vertebrae in vertebral column → permits limited movement.

Synovial Joints

Synovial joints have a fluid-filled synovial cavity between the articulating surfaces of two bones. This arrangement allows considerable movement → crucial in locomotion and many other movements.
Exam focus: Identify the type of synovial joint from the bones involved (classic examples below).
Ball-and-socketHumerus ↔ pectoral girdle
HingeKnee joint
PivotAtlas ↔ axis
GlidingBetween carpals
SaddleCarpal ↔ metacarpal of thumb
Common confusion: Do not mix up gliding (carpals) with saddle (thumb carpal–metacarpal), and remember atlas–axis is pivot.
Analogy: Think of a joint as the pivot point (fulcrum) of a lever system: bones are levers, muscles provide effort, and the joint is the turning point that enables movement.

Disorders of Muscular and Skeletal System

Myasthenia Gravis

Myasthenia gravis = autoimmune disorder affecting the neuromuscular junction → leads to fatigue, weakening, and paralysis of skeletal muscle.

Muscular Dystrophy

Muscular dystrophy = progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle, mostly due to a genetic disorder.

Tetany

Tetany = rapid spasms (wild contractions) in muscle due to low Ca++ in body fluids.
Exam tip: Tetany is classically linked to hypocalcaemia (low Ca++)—state the ionic cause explicitly.

Arthritis

Arthritis = inflammation of joints.

Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis = age-related disorder characterised by:
  • Decreased bone mass
  • Increased chances of fractures
Common cause: decreased levels of estrogen.

Gout

Gout = inflammation of joints due to accumulation of uric acid crystals.

Chapter Summary (High-Yield Consolidation)

Movement vs Locomotion

Movement is an essential feature of all living beings. Locomotion = a voluntary movement that causes an animal to change its place.
Think: Movement can happen without changing location (e.g., internal movements), but locomotion = movement + displacement.
  • Examples of movement forms in animals: protoplasmic streaming, ciliary movements, movements of fins, limbs, wings, etc.
Animals generally move in search of food, shelter, mate, breeding ground, better climate, or to protect themselves.

Muscle fibre = anatomical unit of muscle. Each muscle fibre has many myofibrils arranged parallelly. Each myofibril contains many serially arranged sarcomeres = functional unit of muscle.

Sarcomere Bands and Filaments

Each sarcomere contains:
  • Central A band made of thick myosin filaments.
  • Two half I bands (on either side of A band) made of thin actin filaments.
  • Z lines mark the boundaries of sarcomere (I bands are marked by Z lines).
Actin and myosin are polymerised proteins with contractility.
Sketch tip in margins: Z | I A I | Z (Z lines at the ends!)

Regulatory Proteins and Myosin Head Features

In resting actin filament, active sites for myosin are masked by troponin. Myosin head contains:
  • ATPase activity
  • ATP-binding sites
  • Active sites for actin
⚠ Exam trap: masking of actin’s active sites is often stated with the regulatory complex — here remember the line: masked by troponin (resting state).

Fatigue and Fibre Types

Repeated stimulation of muscles leads to fatigue. Muscles are classified as Red fibres and White fibres based primarily on the amount of red coloured myoglobin pigment.

Joints Recap (Exam Link to Locomotion)

Three types of joints occur between bones or bone and cartilage: fibrous, cartilaginous, synovial. Among these, synovial joints allow considerable movement and thus play a significant role in locomotion.
