CELL: THE UNIT OF LIFE

Unit 3: Cell—Structure and Functions

Biology = the study of living organisms. Early biology mainly described organisms by form + appearance → showed huge diversity. Cell theory revealed the unity beneath this diversity: all life forms have cellular organisation.
Why cell theory mattered: It explained unity of life added a key requirement for life processes:
  • Integrity (intactness) of cellular organisation is necessary for living phenomena (physiological/behavioural) to be demonstrated/observed.

Physico-chemical (Reductionist) Approach to Studying Life

Reductionist Biology: A physico-chemical approach to understand living organisms by applying concepts + techniques of physics and chemistry to biological questions.
Analyse living tissues for elements/compounds
→
Identify types of organic compounds present
→
Ask: what do these compounds do inside cells?
→
Explain molecular basis of physiological processes + disease
  • What it enables: Use cell-free systems to investigate processes and describe them in molecular terms.
  • Key question addressed: “What is the molecular basis of physiological processes (digestion, excretion, memory, defence, recognition, etc.)?”
  • Medical relevance: Explains abnormal processes during diseased conditions at a molecular level.
Exam tip: Don’t mix these up!
  • Cell theory → cellular unity of life.
  • Reductionism → a method (physico-chemical, often cell-free) to explain life processes molecularly.

Chapter 8: Cell — The Unit of Life

8.1 What is a Cell?

Cell = the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms.
Unicellular organisms can:
  • exist independently
  • perform all essential functions of life
👉 Therefore, anything less than a complete cell cannot ensure independent living.

Key Discoveries in Cell Biology (Historical)

Antonie Von LeeuwenhoekFirst saw & described a live cell
Robert BrownDiscovered the nucleus
Microscopy (→ electron microscope)Revealed detailed cellular structures
Common exam error: Mixing up who discovered what—Leeuwenhoek (live cell) vs Robert Brown (nucleus).

Living vs Non-living: The Essential Difference

When comparing an organism to an inanimate object, the defining feature is the presence of the cell—the basic unit of life—present in all living organisms.

Unicellular vs Multicellular Organisms

UnicellularSingle cell; that one cell performs all essential life functions
MulticellularMany cells (e.g., humans)

8.2 Cell Theory

Cell theory was formulated by Schleiden and Schwann and later refined by Virchow.

Contributions Leading to Cell Theory

  • Matthias Schleiden (1838), German botanist: examined many plants; concluded all plants are composed of cells forming plant tissues.
  • Theodor Schwann (1839), German zoologist:
    • Studied animal cells; reported a thin outer layer now called the plasma membrane.
    • From plant tissue studies: concluded cell wall is unique to plant cells.
    • Proposed that bodies of animals and plants are composed of cells and products of cells.
Limitation of early cell theory: did not explain how new cells are formed.
Schleiden + Schwann (formulated)
→
Gap: origin of new cells
→
Virchow (final shape)
  • Rudolf Virchow (1855): stated that new cells arise by division from existing cells: Omnis cellula-e cellula (“every cell from a pre-existing cell”). This gave cell theory its final shape.
Virchow’s principle (named statement): Omnis cellula-e cellula — new cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Cell Theory (Modern Form)

  1. All living organisms are composed of cells and products of cells.
  2. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.
Exam tip: Write the two modern points exactly; include “products of cells” in point (1) and “pre-existing cells” in point (2).

8.3 An Overview of Cell

Typical Observations: Plant vs Animal Cells

Onion peel cell (plant)Cell wall = distinct outer boundary; just inside lies the cell membrane
Human cheek cell (animal)Outer delimiting structure is the cell (plasma) membrane (no cell wall)

Nucleus, Chromosomes, DNA

Inside each cell is a dense, membrane-bound structure called the nucleus. The nucleus contains chromosomes, which in turn contain the genetic material DNA.

Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Cells

Eukaryotic cellsHave a membrane-bound nucleus
Prokaryotic cellsLack a membrane-bound nucleus
Cytoplasm: a semi-fluid matrix occupying the volume of the cell; it is the main arena of cellular activities in both plant and animal cells.
Living state maintenance: Various chemical reactions occur in the cytoplasm to keep the cell in the “living state.”

Cell Organelles: Membrane-bound vs Non-membrane-bound

Organelles (in eukaryotes): membrane-bound distinct structures such as:
Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)Membrane-bound organelle in eukaryotes
Golgi complexMembrane-bound organelle in eukaryotes
LysosomesMembrane-bound organelle in eukaryotes
MitochondriaMembrane-bound organelle in eukaryotes
MicrobodiesMembrane-bound organelle in eukaryotes
VacuolesMembrane-bound organelle in eukaryotes
Prokaryotic cells lack such membrane-bound organelles.
Ribosomes are non-membrane-bound organelles found in all cells (both prokaryotic and eukaryotic).
  • Where ribosomes are found:
    • in the cytoplasm
    • within mitochondria
    • within chloroplasts (plants)
    • on rough ER
Centrosome: a non-membrane-bound organelle present in animal cells that helps in cell division.
Common mistake: Saying “prokaryotes have no organelles at all.” They lack membrane-bound organelles, but they do have ribosomes (non-membrane-bound).

Cell Size, Shape, and Functional Correlation

Cells differ greatly in size, shape, and activities. Cell shape often varies with the function performed.
  • Smallest cells: Mycoplasmas (~ 0.3 μm in length as stated in text).
  • Bacterial size: typically 3–5 μm.
  • Largest isolated single cell: egg of an ostrich.
  • Human RBC diameter: ~ 7.0 μm.
  • Longest cells: nerve cells are among the longest.
Common cell shapes: disc-like, polygonal, columnar, cuboid, thread-like, irregular.
Link shape to function (idea to use in answers): A cell’s geometry is not random; it often supports its job (e.g., long nerve cells for signal transmission; biconcave RBC for efficient exchange).

Figure-based Content: Examples of Cell Shapes (Figure 8.1)

White blood cellsBranched and long (also shown as amoeboid form in figure context)

8.4 Prokaryotic Cells (Start of Section)

Prokaryotic cells are represented by: bacteria, blue-green algae, mycoplasma, and PPLO (Pleuro Pneumonia Like Organisms).
  • Prokaryotic cells are generally smaller than eukaryotic cells.
  • They multiply more rapidly than eukaryotic cells.
  • They vary greatly in shape and size, but their basic organisation is fundamentally similar.
Think of prokaryotes like “mini, fast, efficient” cells: lots of variety in appearance, but the same basic setup inside.

Basic Shapes of Bacteria

BacillusRod-like
CoccusSpherical
VibrioComma-shaped
SpirillumSpiral
Exam tip: When asked “basic shapes of bacteria,” list all four with shape descriptors: bacillus (rod), coccus (sphere), vibrio (comma), spirillum (spiral).

Prokaryotic Cells: Basic Organisation

Prokaryotes are cells lacking a well-defined, membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (except ribosomes).
No nuclear membrane
→
DNA not enclosed in a nucleus
  • Cell wall surrounds the cell membrane in prokaryotes except in Mycoplasma.
  • Cytoplasm is the semi-fluid matrix filling the cell.
  • No well-defined nucleus: genetic material is naked (not enclosed by a nuclear membrane).
  • Genomic DNA: typically a single chromosome of circular DNA.
  • No eukaryote-like organelles present; ribosomes are the exception.
  • Inclusions (inclusion bodies) are unique storage structures (detailed later).
  • Mesosome: specialised, differentiated infoldings of the cell membrane characteristic of prokaryotes.
Nuclear membrane is a feature of eukaryotes, not prokaryotes; hence prokaryotic DNA is not packaged inside a nucleus.

Plasmids

Plasmids are small circular DNA molecules present in many bacteria outside the genomic (chromosomal) DNA.
  • Function: plasmid DNA confers unique phenotypic characters to bacteria.
  • Example phenotype: antibiotic resistance.
  • Application (noted in text): plasmid DNA is used to monitor bacterial transformation with foreign DNA (you will learn details in higher classes).
Plasmids = “bonus DNA rings” that can give bacteria extra powers (like surviving an antibiotic).
Exam trap: plasmids are not the main chromosome; they are extra-chromosomal circular DNA that can carry special traits (e.g., antibiotic resistance).

Relative Sizes (Figure reference)

Size comparison highlighted in the figure (orders of magnitude):
Typical bacteria~ 1–2 μm
PPLO (Mycoplasma)~ 0.1 μm
Viruses~ 0.02–0.2 μm
Typical eukaryotic cell~ 10–20 μm
PPLO refers to Mycoplasma-like organisms (very small); also recall Mycoplasma lacks a cell wall.

8.4.1 Cell Envelope and its Modifications (Prokaryotes)

Cell envelope (bacteria) is a chemically complex, tightly bound, three-layered protective structure.
GlycocalyxOutermost layer
Cell wallMiddle layer
Plasma membraneInnermost layer
Although each layer has a distinct function, they act together as a single protective unit.

Gram Classification (based on cell envelope and Gram stain response)

Bacteria are classified (by differences in cell envelope and response to Gram staining developed by Gram) into:
Gram-positiveTake up the Gram stain
Gram-negativeDo not take up the Gram stain
Do not overextend: in this portion, only the stain response and basis (cell envelope differences) are required—do not invent wall composition details beyond the given text.

Glycocalyx: Slime layer vs Capsule

Glycocalyx varies among bacteria in composition and thickness.
Slime layerLoose sheath form of glycocalyx
CapsuleThick and tough form of glycocalyx

Cell Wall (bacteria): Role

Cell wall:
  • Determines the shape of the cell.
  • Provides strong structural support.
  • Prevents the bacterium from bursting or collapsing.
Quick picture in mind: cell wall = the “firm outer frame” that stops the cell from popping or caving in.

Plasma Membrane (bacteria): Nature

Plasma membrane is selectively permeable and interacts with the outside world.
The bacterial plasma membrane is structurally similar to the eukaryotic plasma membrane.

Mesosome

Mesosome is a special membranous structure formed by extensions (infoldings) of the plasma membrane into the cell.
Forms of mesosomal extensions:
VesiclesSac-like extensions
TubulesTube-like extensions
LamellaePlate/sheet-like extensions
Functions of mesosomes (as given):
  • Cell wall formation
  • DNA replication and distribution to daughter cells
  • Respiration
  • Secretion processes
  • Increase surface area of the plasma membrane
  • Increase enzymatic content

Chromatophores (in cyanobacteria)

In some prokaryotes like cyanobacteria, there are membranous extensions into the cytoplasm called chromatophores that contain pigments.
Exam tip: Do not confuse chromatophores (prokaryotes; pigment-containing extensions) with chloroplasts (eukaryotic organelles).

Surface Structures: Flagella, Pili, Fimbriae

Bacterial cells may be motile or non-motile. Motility is mainly via flagella.

Flagella: Arrangement and Parts

Flagella are thin filamentous extensions from the cell wall in motile bacteria. Bacteria show variation in number and arrangement of flagella.
Bacterial flagellum has three parts:
FilamentLongest part; extends from cell surface to outside
HookConnects filament to basal body
Basal bodyAnchoring part at cell envelope

Pili and Fimbriae (non-motile surface structures)

Pili and fimbriae are bacterial surface structures that do not play a role in motility.
  • Pili: elongated tubular structures made of a special protein.
  • Fimbriae: small bristle-like fibres sprouting out of the cell.
  • Function of fimbriae (given examples): help bacteria attach to rocks in streams and to host tissues.
Analogy: Think of fimbriae like tiny “Velcro bristles” for sticking to surfaces (rocks/host tissues), while flagella act like propellers for movement.

8.4.2 Ribosomes and Inclusion Bodies (Prokaryotes)

Ribosomes: Structure, Size, Function, Polysomes

In prokaryotes, ribosomes are associated with the plasma membrane and are the site of protein synthesis.
Size: about 15 nm × 20 nm.
Prokaryotic ribosomes are made of two subunits: 50S and 30S, together forming 70S ribosomes.
Exam trap: 70S is the complete prokaryotic ribosome; its subunits are 50S + 30S (do not write 35S + 35S or “70S subunits”).
Polyribosomes/Polysome: several ribosomes attached to a single mRNA forming a chain; they translate the same mRNA into proteins.
Single mRNA
→
Many ribosomes attach
→
Polysome formed
→
Protein synthesis (translation)

Inclusion Bodies: Definition and Types

Inclusion bodies are storage sites of reserve material in prokaryotes, present in the cytoplasm as non-membrane-bound structures lying free in cytoplasm.
  • Examples: phosphate granules, cyanophycean granules, glycogen granules.
  • Gas vacuoles: found in blue-green and purple and green photosynthetic bacteria.
Key phrase: inclusion bodies are not bound by any membrane system (unlike many eukaryotic storage/organelles).

8.5 Eukaryotic Cells: Overview and Plant vs Animal Differences

Eukaryotes include protists, plants, animals, and fungi.
  • Extensive compartmentalisation of cytoplasm due to membrane-bound organelles.
  • Organised nucleus with a nuclear envelope.
  • Complex locomotory and cytoskeletal structures.
  • Genetic material organised into chromosomes.

Plant Cells vs Animal Cells

All eukaryotic cells are not identical. Key differences between plant and animal cells:
Plant cellsCell wall, plastids, large central vacuole present
Animal cellsCentrioles present; no cell wall/plastids/large central vacuole
Wording to learn: plant cells have cell walls, plastids, and a large central vacuole (absent in animal cells); animal cells have centrioles (absent in almost all plant cells).

8.5.1 Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane)

Detailed membrane structure became clear after electron microscope studies in the 1950s. Earlier, chemical studies (notably on human RBC membranes) indicated the membrane’s composition and probable structure.

Membrane Composition: Lipids, Proteins, Carbohydrates

Cell membrane is mainly composed of lipids and proteins; later studies revealed carbohydrates also occur.
Major lipidsPhospholipids
Additional lipidCholesterol
Also presentMembrane proteins + carbohydrates

Phospholipid Bilayer: Orientation and Significance

Phospholipids are arranged in a bilayer with:
  • Polar heads towards the outer sides
  • Hydrophobic tails towards the inner part
This arrangement protects the nonpolar tails of saturated hydrocarbons from the aqueous environment.
Think “water on both sides”: heads face the watery outside + watery cytosol; tails hide inside like an oil layer.
Exam trap: heads face water (outside/inside cytosol), tails face away from water (towards bilayer interior).

Protein–Lipid Ratio (RBC example)

The ratio of protein and lipid varies among cell types. In humans, the erythrocyte (RBC) membrane has approximately:
Protein~52%
Lipids~40%

Membrane Proteins: Integral vs Peripheral

Based on ease of extraction, membrane proteins are classified into:
Peripheral proteinsLie on the surface of the membrane
Integral proteinsPartially or totally buried in the membrane
The membrane model referenced is the Fluid Mosaic Model (Figure reference): a phospholipid bilayer with proteins and carbohydrates; cholesterol is also present.

Fluid Mosaic Model of Plasma Membrane (Singer & Nicolson, 1972)

Fluid mosaic model: Cell membrane is a lipid bilayer with proteins embedded in it; the lipid layer is quasi-fluid, allowing lateral movement of membrane proteins within the bilayer.
Lipid bilayer (quasi-fluid)
→
Proteins embedded
→
Proteins move laterally
Membrane fluidity: The ability of membrane components (especially proteins) to move laterally within the lipid bilayer; it is a measurable property of the membrane.
Why membrane fluidity matters (functions): Essential for processes such as cell growth, formation of intercellular junctions, secretion, endocytosis, and cell division.
Cell growthMembrane expansion + rearrangement
Intercellular junctionsContacts need movable components
SecretionVesicle fusion requires flexible membrane
EndocytosisMembrane invagination + pinching off
Cell divisionMembrane reshaping during cytokinesis
Do not confuse “fluidity” with the membrane being liquid-like in shape; it mainly refers to lateral mobility within the bilayer, not free mixing with the cytoplasm.

Transport Across Plasma Membrane

Selectively permeable membrane: Plasma membrane allows passage of some molecules while restricting others.

Passive Transport (No energy required)

Passive transport: Movement of molecules across the membrane without energy (ATP) requirement.
Simple diffusionNeutral solutes move down concentration gradient (high → low)
OsmosisDiffusion of water (high → low water concentration)
Facilitated transportPolar molecules require carrier proteins to cross lipid bilayer
Simple diffusion: Neutral solutes pass directly through membrane along concentration gradient (from higher to lower concentration). Osmosis: Movement of water across a membrane from higher to lower concentration of water; it is specifically diffusion of water.
High concentration
→
Low concentration
Why polar molecules need carriers: Polar molecules cannot pass through the nonpolar lipid bilayer, hence require a carrier protein to facilitate transport.
Exam trap: “Osmosis” is only for water; do not use it for solutes. Also, diffusion is described in terms of the substance’s own concentration gradient.

Active Transport (Energy-dependent)

Active transport: Transport of ions/molecules against their concentration gradient (from lower to higher concentration). It is ATP-dependent.
Low concentration
→
High concentration
→
ATP used
  • Example: Na+/K+ pump (moves ions against their gradients using ATP).
Do not label carrier-assisted movement as active unless it is explicitly against gradient and uses ATP.

Cell Wall

Cell wall: A non-living rigid outer covering present outside the plasma membrane in plants and fungi.
Functions of cell wall: Gives shape, provides protection from mechanical damage and infection, enables cell-to-cell interaction, and acts as a barrier to undesirable macromolecules.
ShapeMaintains cell form
ProtectionMechanical damage + infection
Cell-to-cell interactionSupports neighbouring connections
BarrierBlocks undesirable macromolecules

Composition of Cell Wall

Algae cell wallCellulose, galactans, mannans + minerals (e.g., CaCO3)
Other plants cell wallCellulose, hemicellulose, pectins, proteins

Primary Wall, Secondary Wall, and Middle Lamella

Primary wall: Cell wall of a young plant cell; it is capable of growth. Secondary wall: Formed as the cell matures on the inner side (towards the plasma membrane); growth capability gradually diminishes as secondary wall forms.
Young cell
→
Primary wall (growing)
→
Secondary wall forms (inner side)
→
Growth diminishes
Middle lamella: A layer mainly of calcium pectate that cements/glues neighbouring plant cells together.
Middle lamella = the “cement” between bricks (plant cells).
Plasmodesmata: Channels that traverse the cell wall and middle lamella, connecting the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells.
Keyword accuracy: middle lamella = calcium pectate; plasmodesmata = cytoplasmic connections across cell walls.

Endomembrane System

Endomembrane system: A functional grouping of membranous organelles whose functions are coordinated (even though each organelle is structurally and functionally distinct).
EREndoplasmic reticulum
Golgi complexGolgi apparatus/bodies
LysosomesHydrolytic enzyme vesicles
VacuolesStorage/transport/osmoregulation spaces
Not part of endomembrane system: Mitochondria, chloroplast, peroxisomes (their functions are not coordinated with ER–Golgi–lysosome–vacuole system).
Exam trap: Many students wrongly include mitochondria in endomembrane system because it has membranes; classification here is based on functional coordination, not just “membrane-bound”.

Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

ER (endoplasmic reticulum): A network/reticulum of tiny tubular membranous structures scattered in the cytoplasm (seen by electron microscopy).
Compartmentalisation by ER: ER divides intracellular space into:
  • Luminal compartment = inside ER
  • Extra-luminal compartment = cytoplasm (outside ER)

Rough ER (RER) vs Smooth ER (SER)

RERRibosomes attached on outer surface
SERNo ribosomes; appears smooth
RER: Prominent in cells actively involved in protein synthesis and secretion; extensive and continuous with outer nuclear membrane. SER: Major site of lipid synthesis; in animal cells, steroidal hormones (lipid-like) are synthesised in SER.
High-yield linkage: RER ↔ proteins, SER ↔ lipids/steroids; RER continuity with outer nuclear membrane is commonly asked.

Golgi Apparatus

Discovery: Camillo Golgi (1898) observed densely stained reticular structures near nucleus; named Golgi bodies.
Structure: Golgi consists of many flat, disc-shaped sacs called cisternae, each about 0.5–1.0 µm in diameter, stacked parallel. Number of cisternae varies per Golgi complex.

Cis and Trans Faces

CITR
C — Cis = Convex (forming face)
I — Incoming vesicles (typically from ER)
T — Trans = Transports out
R — Release from concave (maturing face)
Cis face (forming face): Convex side, oriented to receive vesicles (typically from ER). Trans face (maturing face): Concave side, from which processed products/vesicles are released. Relationship: Cis and trans faces are entirely different yet interconnected.

Functions of Golgi Apparatus

Core role: Packaging of materials for delivery to intracellular targets or for secretion outside the cell.
Vesicles from ER
→
Fuse with cis face
→
Move through cisternae
→
Released from trans face
Like a “post office”: ER sends parcels → Golgi sorts/labels → trans face dispatches to the right address.
Functional association with ER: Golgi stays close to ER because ER-derived vesicles fuse with Golgi cis face for processing/packaging. Modification: Many proteins synthesised by ribosomes on ER are modified in Golgi cisternae before release from trans face. Important synthetic sites: Golgi is a major site for formation of glycoproteins and glycolipids.
Directional recall: Cis receives, Trans releases; also remember the terms forming face (cis) and maturing face (trans).

Lysosomes

Lysosomes: Membrane-bound vesicular structures formed by packaging in the Golgi apparatus.
Contents: Rich in hydrolytic enzymes (hydrolases) including:
  • Lipases (lipids)
  • Proteases (proteins)
  • Carbohydrases (carbohydrates)
These enzymes are optimally active at acidic pH and can digest carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids.
Lysosomes are not just “waste bins”; exam answers must include: Golgi origin, hydrolases, and acidic pH optimum.

Vacuoles

Vacuole: A membrane-bound space in cytoplasm containing water, sap, excretory products, and other materials not useful for the cell.
Tonoplast: The single membrane that bounds the vacuole.
Plant cell vacuole size: Vacuoles can occupy up to 90% of the cell volume.

Tonoplast Transport Function (Plants)

Active transport into vacuole: Tonoplast facilitates transport of ions and other materials against concentration gradients into the vacuole, producing concentrations higher in vacuole than in cytoplasm.

Special Vacuoles in Protists

Contractile vacuole (Amoeba)Osmoregulation and excretion
Food vacuole (many protists)Formed by engulfing food particles
Don’t omit “tonoplast” in definitions; it is a frequent 1-mark term. Also specify Amoeba for contractile vacuole examples.

Mitochondria (Intro: visibility, number, shape, size)

Visibility: Mitochondria are not easily visible under microscope unless specifically stained.
Number per cell: Variable; depends on physiological activity of the cell.
Shape & size variability: Considerable variation; typically sausage-shaped/cylindrical. Typical dimensions: Diameter 0.2–1.0 µm (average 0.5 µm); length 1.0–4.1 µm.
Exam phrasing: state both (i) variability with activity and (ii) typical size range with average diameter.

Mitochondria (structure, contents, functions, division)

Mitochondrion is a double membrane-bound organelle whose inner membrane divides its lumen into two aqueous compartments:
  • Outer compartment (between outer and inner membranes; inter-membrane space)
  • Inner compartment (the space enclosed by inner membrane), filled with dense homogeneous matrix

Membranes and cristae

  • Outer membrane: forms the continuous limiting boundary of the organelle.
  • Inner membrane: forms infoldings called cristae (singular: crista) projecting towards the matrix.
Cristae increase surface area for mitochondrial functions (especially respiration-related reactions). Both membranes have their own specific enzymes associated with mitochondrial function (membrane-specific enzyme systems).

Function: site of aerobic respiration

Mitochondria are the sites of aerobic respiration and produce cellular energy as ATP; hence called the “power houses” of the cell.

Genetic system and protein synthesis machinery in the matrix

Single circular DNA moleculeOwn genetic material inside matrix
A few RNA moleculesSupports expression of mitochondrial genes
Ribosomes (70S)Organelle translation machinery
Components required for protein synthesisEverything needed for making some proteins
Presence of circular DNA and 70S ribosomes in mitochondria is a recurring exam point (organelle has its own genetic system and translational machinery).

Division

Mitochondria divide by fission.
Do not confuse cristae (mitochondria) with grana/thylakoids (chloroplast). Both are membrane folds/stacks but belong to different organelles and processes.

Plastids (types, pigments, chloroplast structure)

Occurrence and general features

Plastids occur in all plant cells and in euglenoides. They are generally large and hence easily observed under a microscope. They contain specific pigments that impart specific colours to plants.

Classification based on pigments

Basis of classification: type of pigments present (or absence of pigments in leucoplasts).
ChloroplastContains chlorophyll + carotenoids; traps light energy for photosynthesis
ChromoplastContains fat-soluble carotenoids (carotene, xanthophylls, etc.); yellow/orange/red colours
LeucoplastColourless; storage of nutrients; varied shapes and sizes

Leucoplast subtypes (storage plastids)

AmyloplastStores carbohydrates (starch); e.g., potato
ElaioplastStores oils and fats
AleuroplastStores proteins
Exam trap: Amyloplast = starch (carbohydrate storage), Elaioplast = oils/fats, Aleuroplast = proteins. Learn as a fixed triad.

Chloroplast location, shape, size, and number

  • Majority of chloroplasts in green plants occur in mesophyll cells of leaves.
  • Shapes: lens-shaped, oval, spherical, discoid, ribbon-like.
  • Size: length 5–10 μm, width 2–4 μm.
  • Number varies: from 1 per cell in Chlamydomonas (green alga) to 20–40 per cell in mesophyll cells.

Chloroplast envelope and internal organisation

Like mitochondria, chloroplasts are double membrane-bound. The inner chloroplast membrane is relatively less permeable.
Outer membrane
→
Inner membrane (less permeable)
→
Stroma (inside)
Stroma: the space limited by the inner chloroplast membrane.
  • Thylakoids: organised flattened membranous sacs present in the stroma.
  • Grana (singular: granum): stacks of thylakoids arranged like piles of coins.
  • Intergranal thylakoids: thylakoids connecting regions between grana.
  • Stroma lamellae: flat membranous tubules that connect thylakoids of different grana.
  • Lumen: space enclosed by the thylakoid membrane.
Chlorophyll pigments are present in the thylakoids (not free in stroma).

Stroma contents and biosynthetic role

EnzymesRequired for synthesis of carbohydrates and proteins
DNASmall double-stranded circular DNA molecules
RibosomesPresent in stroma
Chloroplast ribosomes are 70S, smaller than cytoplasmic ribosomes (80S).
Analogy: chloroplast is like a “factory campus”: the outer wall is the double envelope, the stroma is the factory ground with enzymes and instructions (DNA), and the grana/thylakoids are the “solar panels” where pigments capture light.

Ribosomes (discovery, composition, types, subunits, Svedberg unit)

Discovery and basic nature

Ribosomes are granular structures first observed under the electron microscope as dense particles by George Palade (1953).
  • Composed of RNA and proteins
  • Not surrounded by any membrane

Types: 80S vs 70S (and their subunits)

80S ribosomeEukaryotic; subunits: 60S (large) + 40S (small)
70S ribosomeProkaryotic; subunits: 50S (large) + 30S (small)
Each ribosome has two subunits (large and small). Both 70S and 80S ribosomes follow this two-subunit organisation.

Meaning of “S”

S stands for Svedberg’s Unit (= sedimentation coefficient), an indirect measure of density and size.
Do not add subunits arithmetically: 60S + 40S ≠ 100S because “S” is sedimentation behavior, not simple mass.

Definition and components

  • Linkers: peripheral doublets are interconnected by linkers.

Basal bodies

Both cilium and flagellum emerge from a centriole-like structure called the basal body.
Exam phrasing: write “9 peripheral doublets + 2 central singlets” (i.e., 9+2). Don’t say “9 pairs + 1 pair” vaguely.

Centrosome and Centrioles (structure and roles)

Centrosome: definition and arrangement

Centrosome is an organelle usually containing two cylindrical centrioles, surrounded by amorphous pericentriolar material.
  • The two centrioles lie perpendicular to each other.
  • Each centriole shows a cartwheel-like organisation.

Centriole microtubule pattern (triplets) and internal parts

Centrioles are made of nine evenly spaced peripheral fibrils of tubulin protein.
  • Each peripheral fibril is a triplet.
  • Adjacent triplets are linked.
Hubcentral proteinaceous part in the proximal region
Radial spokesprotein spokes connecting the hub to tubules of peripheral triplets

Functions of centrioles

Basal body roleforms the basal body of cilia or flagella
Spindle formationforms spindle fibres → spindle apparatus during cell division in animal cells
Do not mix patterns: centriole = 9 triplets (cartwheel); axoneme = 9+2 (doublets + central pair).

Nucleus (history, structure, nuclear envelope, nucleolus, chromatin)

Historical notes

Robert Brown (1831)first described nucleus as a cell organelle
Flemmingnamed the basic dye-stained nuclear material as chromatin

Interphase nucleus: components

Interphase nucleus (nucleus when the cell is not dividing) contains:
  • Chromatin: highly extended, elaborate nucleoprotein fibres
  • Nuclear matrix / nucleoplasm
  • One or more nucleoli (singular: nucleolus)

Nuclear envelope (double membrane) and perinuclear space

Nuclear envelope consists of two parallel membranes with a gap called the perinuclear space of 10–50 nm. It forms a barrier between nuclear contents and cytoplasm.
  • Outer nuclear membrane is usually continuous with endoplasmic reticulum (ER).
  • Outer membrane also bears ribosomes.

Nuclear pores and transport

Nuclear envelope is interrupted at places by nuclear pores formed by fusion of the two membranes. Nuclear pores permit bidirectional movement of RNA and protein molecules between nucleus and cytoplasm.

Number of nuclei in cells (variation)

  • Typically one nucleus per cell.
  • Variations in nuclear number are frequently observed (some organisms have more than one nucleus per cell).
  • Some mature cells lack a nucleus, e.g. erythrocytes of many mammals and sieve tube cells of vascular plants.
If asked to justify “enucleate but living”: note these mature cells can lack nuclei yet function (e.g., mammalian RBCs; sieve tube elements), relying on prior synthesis/support from associated cells.

Nucleoplasm (nuclear matrix), nucleolus and chromatin

Nucleoplasm (nuclear matrix) contains nucleolus and chromatin.
Nucleolus:
  • Spherical structure in nucleoplasm
  • Not membrane-bound; its contents are continuous with nucleoplasm
  • Site of active rRNA synthesis
Larger and more numerous nucleoli occur in cells actively carrying out protein synthesis (high rRNA demand → more ribosomes).

Chromatin and Chromosomes (Interphase vs Dividing Cell)

Chromatin = loose, indistinct network of nucleoprotein fibres seen in the interphase nucleus. Chromosomes = structured, condensed form of chromatin seen during stages of cell division (hence visible only in dividing cells).
Key contrast for exams:

→

Chemical Composition of Chromatin

Chromatin contains:
  • DNA
  • Basic proteins called histones
  • Non-histone proteins
  • RNA
Think of chromatin as a “DNA–protein–RNA complex”: DNA is the information thread; histones/non-histones are the organising/spooling proteins; RNA is also present as part of the nuclear material.

DNA Length and Distribution in Human Cells

Fact to remember: A single human cell contains about 2 metres of DNA thread distributed among 46 chromosomes (23 pairs). (Details of DNA packaging into chromosomes are studied later.)
Common slip: writing “46 pairs” instead of “46 chromosomes = 23 pairs”.

Chromosome Structure: Centromere and Kinetochore

Each chromosome has a primary constriction called the centromere. On the sides of the centromere are disc-shaped structures called kinetochores. Centromere function (structural): it holds the two chromatids of a chromosome together.
Kinetochore is a distinct structure associated with the centromere region (disc-shaped; on the sides of the centromere). In diagrams, label: chromatids, centromere (primary constriction), kinetochores.

Classification of Chromosomes Based on Centromere Position

Basis of classification: position of the centromere determines relative arm lengths.
MetacentricCentromere in the middle → two equal arms
Sub-metacentricCentromere slightly away from middle → one shorter + one longer arm
AcrocentricCentromere close to an end → one extremely short + one very long arm
TelocentricCentromere at the terminal end (terminal centromere)
Exam diagram tip: show the centromere position clearly and indicate arm-length differences (equal; slightly unequal; extremely unequal; terminal).

Secondary Constriction and Satellite

Some chromosomes show secondary constrictions that are non-staining and occur at a constant location. This can give the appearance of a small fragment called a satellite.
Satellite is an appearance produced due to a constant, non-staining secondary constriction—don’t confuse it with an actual detached chromosome piece.

Microbodies

Microbodies = membrane-bound, minute vesicles containing various enzymes, present in both plant and animal cells.
  • Key identifying features: minute vesicles + enzymes + membrane bound + in plants and animals.

Chapter Summary

Cell as the unit of life: All organisms are made of cells or aggregates of cells. Cells vary in shape, size, and functions.

Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic (Basis)

Classification depends on presence/absence of:
  • Membrane-bound nucleus
  • Other membrane-bound organelles
Accordingly, cells/organisms are eukaryotic or prokaryotic.

Golgi Body (Structure + Function)

Golgi body = membranous organelle composed of flattened sacs. Function: cell secretions are packed in Golgi and transported from the cell.
Golgi = the cell’s packing + shipping center: labels, packs, and sends materials out.

Lysosomes (Key Feature + Function)

Lysosomes are single membrane structures containing enzymes for digestion of all types of macromolecules.

Ribosomes (Location + Function)

Ribosomes are involved in protein synthesis. Location:
  • Free in cytoplasm, OR
  • Associated with ER

Mitochondria (Function + Membranes + Cristae)

  • Function: oxidative phosphorylation and generation of ATP (adenosine triphosphate)
  • Double membrane bound
  • Outer membrane: smooth
  • Inner membrane: folded into cristae
Mitochondria: powerhouse vibes — more cristae folds = more surface area for ATP-making steps.
