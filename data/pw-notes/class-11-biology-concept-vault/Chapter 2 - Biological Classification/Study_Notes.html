Biological Classification: Need, History & Changing Criteria

Biological Classification: Need, History & Changing Criteria

Biological classification = arranging living organisms into groups based on shared characteristics. Human attempts began “instinctively” (non-scientific), driven by utility (food, shelter, clothing). With time, classification shifted toward scientific criteria and evolutionary relationships.
Think of it like sorting a huge library: first by “what helps me now” (usefulness), later by “what it truly is” (scientific + evolutionary relatedness).

Early attempts: Aristotle

Aristotle was among the earliest to attempt a more scientific classification using morphological characters (observable form/structure).
Plantsclassified as trees, shrubs, herbs (simple morphology)
Animalsdivided into red-blooded vs not red-blooded

Two-Kingdom Classification (Linnaeus)

Two-Kingdom system (developed in Linnaeus’ time): all organisms placed into either Kingdom Plantae or Kingdom Animalia.
Why it became inadequate: It did not distinguish between key biological differences:
  • Eukaryotes vs prokaryotes
  • Unicellular vs multicellular
  • Photosynthetic organisms (e.g., green algae) vs non-photosynthetic organisms (e.g., fungi)
Although easy to understand, many organisms did not fit neatly into “plant” or “animal.”

Expansion of classification criteria beyond gross morphology

As limitations became clear, classification increasingly used multiple criteria, not just gross morphology.
  • Cell structure
  • Nature of cell wall
  • Mode of nutrition
  • Habitat
  • Methods of reproduction
  • Evolutionary (phylogenetic) relationships
⚠ Exam trap: “Plant” and “animal” kingdoms remained present across systems, but which organisms belong to them and the number/nature of other kingdoms changed as criteria improved.

Whittaker’s Five Kingdom Classification (1969)

R.H. Whittaker (1969) proposed the Five Kingdom Classification: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia.

Main criteria used by Whittaker

  • Cell structure
  • Body organisation
  • Mode of nutrition
  • Reproduction
  • Phylogenetic relationships (evolutionary relationships)

Three-domain system (context only)

A three-domain system has also been proposed in which Kingdom Monera is split into two domains, and the remaining eukaryotic kingdoms fall into a third domain, effectively giving a six-kingdom classification. (Details are deferred to higher classes.)

Why earlier “Plants” grouping caused problems (and what Whittaker fixed)

Earlier systems placed bacteria, blue-green algae, fungi, mosses, ferns, gymnosperms, angiosperms under “Plants,” mainly because they all had cell walls. This single-character approach grouped very different organisms together.

Need for new kingdoms based on deeper criteria
  • Prokaryotes (bacteria, cyanobacteria/blue-green algae) were grouped with eukaryotes (many algae, fungi, land plants).
  • Unicellular and multicellular forms were mixed (e.g., Chlamydomonas and Spirogyra both placed under algae).
  • Heterotrophs (fungi) were not separated from autotrophs (green plants), despite major differences, including cell wall chemistry.

Considering such characteristics led to placing fungi in a separate kingdom (Kingdom Fungi).

Kingdom Protista as a “new grouping” under changed criteria

Whittaker’s system grouped unicellular eukaryotes into Kingdom Protista, even if earlier systems placed them in different kingdoms.
  • Chlamydomonas and Chlorella: earlier placed in algae within Plants (both have cell walls).
  • Paramoecium and Amoeba: earlier placed in Animal kingdom (lack cell wall).
This mixing happened because classification criteria changed. Such changes can continue in the future as understanding of characteristics and evolutionary relationships improves.

Modern goal: phylogenetic classification

Over time, classification aims to reflect not only morphological, physiological, and reproductive similarities but also be phylogenetic—i.e., based on evolutionary relationships.

Kingdom Monera: Bacteria (Introduction)

Kingdom Monera contains bacteria as the sole members.

Occurrence and abundance

  • Most abundant micro-organisms
  • Occur almost everywhere
  • Hundreds may be present in a handful of soil
  • Live in extreme habitats: hot springs, deserts, snow, deep oceans (where few life forms survive)
  • Many live in or on other organisms as parasites
Analogy to remember ubiquity: If most organisms need “comfortable conditions,” bacteria are the “survivors” that can occupy nearly every niche—from boiling springs to deep oceans—and can also live on/in hosts as parasites.

Biological Classification: Bacteria (overview)

Bacteria: structure vs behaviour
  • Structure: very simple
  • Behaviour/metabolism: very complex; as a group they show the most extensive metabolic diversity compared to many other organisms

Nutrition in bacteria: Autotrophs vs heterotrophs

Autotrophic bacteria synthesise their own food from inorganic substrates.
Photosynthetic autotrophsUse light energy to synthesise food
Chemosynthetic autotrophsUse energy released by oxidising inorganic substances
Heterotrophic bacteria depend on other organisms or dead organic matter for food. Majority of bacteria are heterotrophs.
Do not mix up: autotrophs use inorganic substrates to make food; heterotrophs rely on pre-formed organic matter (living or dead).

Archaebacteria

Archaebacteria are special bacteria that live in extreme (harsh) habitats. Their key distinguishing feature is a different cell wall structure from other bacteria, which enables survival under extreme conditions.
HalophilesLive in extremely salty areas
ThermoacidophilesLive in hot springs
MethanogensLive in marshy areas; produce methane
Methanogens occur in the gut of ruminants (e.g., cows, buffaloes) and are responsible for methane (biogas) production from the dung of these animals.
Real-life link: Biogas plants exploit methane production from dung; methanogens are the biological agents behind this conversion.

Eubacteria (“true bacteria”)

Eubacteria are the ‘true bacteria’ and include thousands of different forms.

General characteristics

  • Cell wall: rigid cell wall present
  • Motility: if motile, they have a flagellum

Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae)

Cyanobacteria (also called blue-green algae) contain chlorophyll a similar to green plants and hence are photosynthetic autotrophs.
OrganisationUnicellular / Colonial / Filamentous
HabitatFreshwater / Marine / Terrestrial
Colony featureOften surrounded by a gelatinous sheath
Blooms: Cyanobacteria often form blooms in polluted water bodies.
Nitrogen fixation: Some cyanobacteria can fix atmospheric nitrogen in specialised cells called heterocysts.
  • Examples (heterocystous N2-fixers): Nostoc, Anabaena
Exam trap: “Blue-green algae” are bacteria (prokaryotic), not true algae (eukaryotic), even though they are photosynthetic.

Chemosynthetic autotrophic bacteria

Chemosynthetic autotrophic bacteria obtain energy by oxidising inorganic substances and use this released energy for ATP production.
  • They oxidise substances such as nitrates, nitrites and ammonia.
Ecological role: They play a major role in recycling nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorous, iron and sulphur.

Heterotrophic bacteria: importance and harm

Heterotrophic bacteria are the most abundant bacteria in nature; most act as decomposers.
Useful rolesCurd formation; antibiotic production; nitrogen fixation in legume roots
Harmful rolesPathogens of humans, crops, farm animals and pets
  • Helpful examples/uses: making curd from milk; production of antibiotics; fixing nitrogen in legume roots
  • Pathogenic examples (diseases): cholera, typhoid, tetanus, citrus canker

Reproduction in bacteria

Mycoplasma

Mycoplasma are organisms that completely lack a cell wall.
  • Smallest living cells known
  • Can survive without oxygen
  • Many are pathogenic in animals and plants

Kingdom Protista

Protista includes all single-celled eukaryotes. Boundary note: Kingdom limits are not well defined (e.g., one biologist’s “photosynthetic protistan” may be another’s “plant”).
Included here: Chrysophytes, Dinoflagellates, Euglenoids, Slime moulds, Protozoans.
  • Habitat: primarily aquatic
  • Linking role: connects features of kingdoms dealing with plants, animals and fungi

General features of protists

Eukaryotic cell plan → protist cells have:
  • a well-defined nucleus
  • membrane-bound organelles
  • in some, flagella or cilia
Reproduction in Protista occurs by:
  • Asexual methods
  • Sexual methods involving cell fusion and zygote formation

Diatoms: cell wall structure and significance

Diatom cell wall forms two thin overlapping shells that fit together like a soap box. Walls embedded with silica → walls are indestructible.
Analogy: diatom wall = a two-part soap box (lid + base) made rigid by silica.
Diatomaceous earth = large deposits of diatom cell walls accumulated in habitats over billions of years.
Uses of diatomaceous earth: gritty soil used for:
  • Polishing
  • Filtration of oils and syrups
Ecological importance: Diatoms are the chief “producers” in the oceans.

Dinoflagellates

Dinoflagellates are mostly marine and photosynthetic.
Colour variation: may appear yellow, green, brown, blue or red depending on the main pigments in their cells.

Cell covering and flagella arrangement

Cell wall: stiff cellulose plates on the outer surface. Flagella: most have two flagella:
Longitudinal flagellumruns along the length

Red tides and toxicity

Red tides: rapid multiplication of red dinoflagellates can make seawater appear red.
  • Example: Gonyaulax
Toxins released during blooms (large numbers) may kill other marine animals such as fishes.
Exam phrasing to remember: “Red dinoflagellates undergo rapid multiplication” → sea appears red; “toxins released” → fish kills.

Euglenoids

Euglenoids are mostly freshwater organisms found in stagnant water.

Pellicle (in place of a cell wall)

Instead of a cell wall, euglenoids have a protein-rich layer called pellicle, which makes the body flexible.

Flagella and nutrition (mixotrophy)

Flagella: two flagella—one short and one long. Nutrition:

Slime Moulds

Slime moulds are saprophytic protists.

Feeding and movement

The body moves along decaying twigs and leaves, engulfing organic material.

Plasmodium formation and spore-bearing fruiting bodies

Suitable conditions: slime moulds form an aggregation called plasmodium, which may grow and spread over several feet. Unfavourable conditions: plasmodium differentiates into fruiting bodies that bear spores at their tips.
  • Spores have true walls
  • Extremely resistant—can survive for many years even under adverse conditions
  • Dispersal: spores are dispersed by air currents
Do not omit the condition-dependence in answers: suitable → plasmodium; unfavourable → fruiting bodies + spores.

Alternation of generations

Alternation of generations = plant life cycle shows two distinct phases that alternate:
  • Diploid sporophytic phase (2n)
  • Haploid gametophytic phase (n)
The length of each phase and whether each is free-living or dependent varies among different plant groups.
Exam trap: “Alternation of generations” is not just the presence of two phases; it specifically means they alternate and may differ in duration and independence across groups (algae → bryophytes → pteridophytes → gymnosperms → angiosperms).

Viruses, Viroids, Prions and Lichens

In Whittaker’s five-kingdom classification, there is no mention of:
  • Lichens
  • Some acellular organisms: viruses, viroids, prions

Viruses

Viruses are non-cellular organisms. Outside a living cell they exist as an inert crystalline structure; inside a host cell they replicate by taking over the host machinery, often killing the host. This is why viruses are not considered truly “living” if living organisms are defined as those having a cell structure.
Living vs non-living angle (typical exam discussion point): Viruses behave like chemicals (inert crystals) outside a host, but show biological reproduction only inside a host cell.

Historical milestones and terminology

Meaning of “virus”venom or poisonous fluid
Dmitri Ivanowsky (1892)tobacco mosaic disease microbe passed through bacteria-proof filters → smaller than bacteria
M.W. Beijerinck (1898)infected extract infects healthy plants; named “virus”; Contagium vivum fluidum
W.M. Stanley (1935)viruses can be crystallised; crystals largely proteins
Obligate parasites: Viruses are inert outside their specific host cell and can replicate only inside it.

Structure and genetic material

A virus is a nucleoprotein (nucleic acid + protein). The genetic material is infectious.
Genetic material rule: Viral genetic material is either RNA or DNA; no virus contains both RNA and DNA.
Plant virusesgenerally single-stranded RNA (ssRNA)
Animal virusesssRNA or double-stranded RNA or double-stranded DNA (dsDNA)
Bacteriophages(infect bacteria) usually dsDNA

Capsid, capsomeres and symmetry

Capsid = protein coat that protects viral nucleic acid. Capsomeres = small subunits making up the capsid.
  • Arrangement of capsomeres: in helical or polyhedral geometric forms.
Do not confuse: capsid (entire coat) vs capsomeres (subunits). Also, “helical/polyhedral” refers to capsomere arrangement.

Diseases caused by viruses

Human diseasesmumps, small pox, herpes, influenza; AIDS also caused by a virus
Plant symptomsmosaic formation; leaf rolling & curling; yellowing; vein clearing; dwarfing; stunted growth

Viroids

Viroids are infectious agents discovered by T.O. Diener (1971), smaller than viruses, causing potato spindle tuber disease.
  • Composition: free RNA only.
  • No protein coat (unlike viruses) → hence the name viroid.
  • RNA has low molecular weight.

Prions

Prions are infectious agents made of abnormally folded protein (no nucleic acid mentioned here) and are similar in size to viruses.
Notable prion diseases:
  • Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) = “mad cow disease” in cattle
  • Analogous human disease: Creutzfeldt–Jacob disease (CJD)

Lichens

Lichens are symbiotic (mutually useful) associations between algae and fungi.
  • Phycobiont = algal component (autotrophic)
  • Mycobiont = fungal component (heterotrophic)

Environmental application: Lichens are very good pollution indicators because they do not grow in polluted areas.
