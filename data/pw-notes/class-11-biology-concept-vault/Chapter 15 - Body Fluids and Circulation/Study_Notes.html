BODY FLUIDS AND CIRCULATION

Body Fluids and Circulation (Introduction)

All living cells must continuously receive nutrients, O2 and other essential substances, and must continuously eliminate wastes/toxic products for normal tissue function.
Animals have evolved different transport mechanisms:
Simple animals (sponges, coelenterates) Circulate surrounding water through body cavities to allow exchange of substances with cells
Complex animals Use specialised internal body fluids for transport
Major transport fluids in humans Blood (primary) and lymph (helps transport certain substances)
Analogy: Body fluids act like a “delivery + waste-collection service” for each cell—bringing supplies (O2, nutrients) and removing garbage (CO2, nitrogenous wastes).

Blood

Blood is a special connective tissue consisting of:
  • Fluid matrix = plasma
  • Formed elements = erythrocytes (RBCs), leucocytes (WBCs), platelets

Plasma

  • Appearance: straw-coloured, viscous fluid
  • Proportion of blood: ~55%
  • Water content: ~90–92%
  • Proteins: ~6–8%
Major plasma proteins and their functions:
Fibrinogen Required for blood clotting/coagulation
Globulins Mainly involved in defence mechanisms
Albumins Help maintain osmotic balance
Other components of plasma:
  • Mineral ions: Na+, Ca++, Mg++, HCO3–, Cl–, etc.
  • Nutrients/transported molecules in transit: glucose, amino acids, lipids, etc.
  • Clotting factors: present in plasma largely in inactive form
Serum = plasma without clotting factors.
Exam trap: Do not confuse plasma (has clotting factors) with serum (plasma minus clotting factors).

Formed Elements

Formed elements = erythrocytes + leucocytes + platelets; together they constitute ~45% of blood.

Erythrocytes (RBCs)

  • Most abundant blood cells
  • Count (healthy adult man): ~5.0–5.5 million mm–3 of blood
  • Site of formation in adults: red bone marrow
  • Nucleus: absent in most mammals (RBCs are enucleated)
  • Shape: biconcave
  • Pigment: haemoglobin (iron-containing complex protein) gives red colour
  • Haemoglobin concentration (healthy individual): 12–16 g per 100 mL blood
  • Function: important in transport of respiratory gases
  • Life span: ~120 days
  • Destruction site: spleen (“graveyard of RBCs”)
Formed in: Red bone marrow (adults)
→
Circulate ~120 days
→
Destroyed mainly in: Spleen
Be precise in answers: RBCs are formed in red bone marrow (adults) and destroyed mainly in the spleen; lifespan is 120 days.

Leucocytes (WBCs)

  • Called white blood cells because they are colourless (lack haemoglobin)
  • Nucleated
  • Count: ~6000–8000 mm–3 of blood
  • Generally short-lived
Two main categories of WBCs:
Granulocytes Neutrophils, Eosinophils, Basophils
Agranulocytes Lymphocytes, Monocytes

Granulocytes

  • Neutrophils: most abundant WBCs, ~60–65% of total WBCs
  • Eosinophils: ~2–3% of total WBCs
  • Basophils: least abundant, ~0.5–1% of total WBCs
  • Neutrophils are phagocytic (destroy foreign organisms).
  • Basophils secrete histamine, serotonin, heparin etc.; involved in inflammatory reactions.
  • Eosinophils resist infections and are also associated with allergic reactions.

Agranulocytes

  • Monocytes: ~6–8% of total WBCs; phagocytic (destroy foreign organisms).
  • Lymphocytes: ~20–25% of total WBCs; major types = B lymphocytes and T lymphocytes.
B and T lymphocytes are responsible for immune responses of the body.
NEB
N — Neutrophils
E — Eosinophils
B — Basophils

Hint: GrANulocytes have “Grains” (granules).
High-yield distinction: Neutrophils + Monocytes are specifically stated as the main phagocytic cells here.

Platelets (Thrombocytes)

  • Also called: thrombocytes
  • Nature: cell fragments (not complete cells)
  • Origin: produced from megakaryocytes (special bone marrow cells)
  • Count: ~1,50,000–3,50,000 mm–3 of blood
  • Role: release substances involved in coagulation/clotting
Megakaryocytes (bone marrow)
→
Platelets (cell fragments)
→
Coagulation / clotting
Clinical point: Reduced platelet count → clotting disorders → excessive blood loss.

Blood Groups

Human blood appears similar but differs in specific traits. Many blood group systems exist; two widely used worldwide are:
ABO systemBased on A/B antigens on RBC surface
Rh systemAnother major blood grouping used globally (details covered later in chapter)

ABO Grouping

Basis: Presence/absence of two surface antigens on RBCs: Antigen A and Antigen B. Plasma contains natural antibodies against the absent antigen(s): antibodies are proteins produced in response to antigens.
Transfusion principle: Donor blood must be matched with recipient blood to avoid severe clumping (destruction of RBCs) due to antigen–antibody reaction.
ABO blood groups: antigens on RBCs, antibodies in plasma, and donor compatibility
Group ARBC: Antigen A • Plasma: anti-B • Receives from A, O
Group BRBC: Antigen B • Plasma: anti-A • Receives from B, O
Group ABRBC: Antigens A & B • Plasma: nil antibodies • Receives from AB, A, B, O
Group ORBC: nil antigens • Plasma: anti-A & anti-B • Receives from O only
  • Universal donor: Group O (can donate to any ABO group).
  • Universal recipient: Group AB (can accept from any ABO group).
Common confusion: “Universal donor/recipient” here refers to ABO compatibility as described. In practice, matching involves more factors, but for exam answers stick to the stated ABO logic.

Rh Grouping

Rh antigen (Rh factor) is an antigen present on the surface of RBCs, similar to one found in Rhesus monkeys (hence the name Rh).
Distribution in humans: present in the majority (~80%) of humans.
Rh positive (Rh+ve)Rh antigen present on RBC surface
Rh negative (Rh−ve)Rh antigen absent on RBC surface
Antibody formation: An Rh−ve person, if exposed to Rh+ve blood, forms specific antibodies against Rh antigens.
Transfusion rule: Rh group should also be matched before blood transfusions (to prevent antibody formation and reactions).

Rh Incompatibility in Pregnancy (Erythroblastosis Foetalis)

Special case: Rh incompatibility may occur between an Rh−ve pregnant mother and an Rh+ve foetus.
First pregnancy (Rh− mother, Rh+ foetus)
→
Foetal Rh antigens usually not exposed to mother (placenta separates blood)
Why the first pregnancy is usually safe: In the first pregnancy, foetal Rh antigens generally do not get exposed to the mother’s Rh− blood because maternal and foetal blood are separated by the placenta.
During delivery of first child
→
Small amount of Rh+ foetal blood may enter mother
→
Mother starts producing anti-Rh antibodies
Mechanism in subsequent pregnancies: In later pregnancies, Rh antibodies from the mother (Rh−ve) can leak into foetal blood (if foetus is Rh+ve) and destroy foetal RBCs.
Outcome: Destruction of foetal RBCs may be fatal to the foetus or cause severe anaemia and jaundice in the baby.
Condition name: This Rh incompatibility disease is called erythroblastosis foetalis.
Prevention: Avoided by administering anti-Rh antibodies to the mother immediately after the delivery of the first child.
Exam trap: The mother does not naturally have anti-Rh antibodies; they form only after exposure to Rh+ blood (commonly at first delivery). The major risk is in subsequent Rh+ pregnancies.

Coagulation (Clotting) of Blood

Coagulation/clotting: Blood clots in response to injury/trauma to prevent excessive blood loss.
What you observe in real life: The dark reddish-brown scum that forms over a cut is a clot (coagulum).
Clot (coagulum) composition: Mainly a network of thread-like fibrins in which dead and damaged formed elements of blood are trapped.

Biochemical Sequence of Clot Formation

Inactive fibrinogen (plasma)
→
Fibrin threads
Key conversion: Fibrin forms by conversion of inactive fibrinogen present in plasma by the enzyme thrombin.
Inactive prothrombin (plasma)
→
Thrombin
Thrombin formation: Thrombin is formed from another inactive plasma substance called prothrombin.
Thrombokinase (enzyme complex): Required for the conversion steps; it is formed by a cascade process (series of linked enzymic reactions) involving many plasma factors initially present in an inactive state.
Initiation of coagulation:
  • Platelets release certain factors when stimulated by injury/trauma → activates coagulation mechanism.
  • Tissues at injury site also release factors that can initiate coagulation.
Role of calcium: Calcium ions (Ca2+) play a very important role in clotting.
Exam tip: Write clotting as a sequence (fibrinogen → fibrin via thrombin; prothrombin → thrombin; needs thrombokinase + Ca2+; initiated by platelets/tissue factors) and state the purpose: prevention of blood loss.

Lymph (Tissue Fluid)

Formation of tissue (interstitial) fluid: As blood passes through capillaries in tissues, some water and many small water-soluble substances move out into spaces between tissue cells, while larger proteins and most formed elements remain within blood vessels.
Blood in capillaries (tissues)
→
Water + small solutes filter out
→
Fluid between cells = tissue fluid
Names: This fluid outside capillaries is called interstitial fluid or tissue fluid.
Moves outwater + small water-soluble substances
Stays in bloodlarger proteins + most formed elements
Composition: Tissue fluid has the same mineral distribution as plasma.
Exchange medium: Exchange of nutrients, gases, etc. between blood and cells occurs through tissue fluid.
Think of tissue fluid as the “middle layer” — blood doesn’t hand nutrients directly to every cell; it passes them into this fluid first.

Lymphatic System and Lymph

Lymphatic system: An elaborate network of vessels that collects tissue fluid and drains it back to the major veins.
Tissue fluid (between cells)
→
Collected by lymphatic vessels
→
Drained to major veins
Lymph: The fluid present in the lymphatic system is called lymph.
  • Colour: Lymph is colourless.
  • Cells: Contains specialised lymphocytes.
  • Function: Lymphocytes are responsible for immune responses.
  • Transport: Lymph carries nutrients and hormones, etc.
Fat absorption: Fats are absorbed through lymph in lacteals present in the intestinal villi.
Fats (in intestine)
→
Lacteals (intestinal villi)
→
Lymph
Exam trap: Don’t write that fats go directly into blood capillaries from villi—textbook point here is: fats → lacteals → lymph.

Circulatory Pathways

Open vs Closed Circulatory System

Open circulatory systemBlood pumped by heart enters large vessels then flows into open spaces/body cavities called sinuses
Closed circulatory systemBlood pumped by heart remains within a closed network of blood vessels
Examples:
ArthropodsOpen circulatory system
MolluscsOpen circulatory system
AnnelidsClosed circulatory system
ChordatesClosed circulatory system
Advantage of closed system: considered more advantageous because the flow of fluid can be more precisely regulated.

Heart Chambering Across Vertebrates

All vertebrates possess a muscular chambered heart, but the number of chambers varies.
Fishes2-chambered: 1 atrium + 1 ventricle
Amphibians3-chambered: 2 atria + 1 ventricle
Reptiles (except crocodiles)3-chambered: 2 atria + 1 ventricle
Crocodiles4-chambered: 2 atria + 2 ventricles
Birds4-chambered: 2 atria + 2 ventricles
Mammals4-chambered: 2 atria + 2 ventricles

Human Circulatory System

Human circulatory system (blood vascular system): consists of (i) a muscular, chambered heart, (ii) a network of closed branching blood vessels, and (iii) blood as the circulating fluid.

Position, Origin, Protection of Heart

  • Origin: Heart is mesodermally derived.
  • Location: In the thoracic cavity, between the two lungs, slightly tilted to the left.
  • Size: Approximately the size of a clenched fist.
  • Covering: Protected by a double-walled membranous bag called pericardium.
  • Pericardial fluid: Pericardium encloses pericardial fluid.
Quick picture: fist-sized pump, between lungs, slightly left — wrapped in a double bag (pericardium) with fluid for smooth movement.

Heart Valves, Cardiac Muscles & Nodal Tissue

Semilunar valves are present at the openings of the pulmonary artery and the aorta (respectively) and ensure one-way blood flow from ventricles into these arteries. Heart valves ensure that blood flows only in one direction:
  • Atria → Ventricles (via atrio-ventricular valves: tricuspid and bicuspid/mitral)
  • Ventricles → Pulmonary artery / Aorta (via semilunar valves)
  • They prevent backward flow of blood.
Think of valves like “one-way doors” in hallways—once blood passes, the door shuts to stop it coming back.
Cardiac muscle forms the entire heart. Ventricular walls are much thicker than atrial walls (because ventricles pump blood out of the heart into major arteries under higher pressure).

Nodal Tissue (Specialised Cardiac Musculature) & Conducting System

Nodal tissue = specialised cardiac musculature distributed in the heart that forms the heart’s intrinsic conduction system.
Sino-atrial node (SAN)Patch of nodal tissue in the right upper corner of the right atrium
Atrio-ventricular node (AVN)Mass of nodal tissue in the lower left corner of the right atrium, near the atrio-ventricular septum
AV bundle (Bundle of His)Bundle of nodal fibres from AVN; passes through atrio-ventricular septa to the top of the interventricular septum
Right & Left bundle branchesAV bundle divides into right and left bundle
Purkinje fibresMinute fibres arising from bundle branches; spread throughout the ventricular musculature of respective sides
Autoexcitability of nodal musculature: Nodal tissue can generate action potentials without any external stimulus (autoexcitable). Rate differs across nodal system: Different parts generate different numbers of action potentials per minute. SAN generates the maximum number of action potentials: 70–75 min−1. Because SAN initiates and maintains the rhythmic contractile activity of the heart, it is called the pacemaker. Normal human heart rate is typically 70–75 beats min−1 (average 72 beats min−1).
Exam trap: Don’t confuse the structures and roles—SAN is the normal pacemaker (highest firing rate), while AVN primarily conducts the impulse from atria to ventricles via the AV bundle and its branches.

Cardiac Cycle

Cardiac cycle = a sequential set of events in the heart that is cyclically repeated, consisting of systole and diastole of both atria and ventricles.

Sequence of Events in One Cardiac Cycle

Joint diastole (all chambers relaxed)
→
Atrial systole (atria contract)
→
Ventricular systole (ventricles contract)
→
Ventricular diastole (ventricles relax)
→
Return to joint diastole
  • Start: Joint diastole — all four chambers are relaxed.
  • AV valves open (tricuspid and bicuspid/mitral); semilunar valves closed.
  • Blood flows from pulmonary veins into the left atrium and from vena cava into the right atrium, and then into the ventricles through open AV valves.
  • SAN fires → action potential stimulates both atria → simultaneous atrial contraction = atrial systole.
  • Atrial systole increases ventricular filling by ~30%.
  • Impulse conducted to ventricular side via AVN and AV bundle; from there the bundle of His transmits it through the ventricular musculature.
  • This triggers ventricular systole (ventricular contraction) while atria relax (atrial diastole) coinciding with ventricular systole.
  • Rising ventricular pressure closes tricuspid and bicuspid valves (closure due to attempted backflow into atria).
  • Further rise in ventricular pressure forces open semilunar valves guarding pulmonary artery (right) and aorta (left) → blood enters circulatory pathways.
  • Ventricles then relax = ventricular diastole → ventricular pressure falls → semilunar valves close to prevent backflow into ventricles.
  • As ventricular pressure declines further, atrial pressure pushes tricuspid and bicuspid valves open → blood moves freely into ventricles again.
  • Heart returns to joint diastole; SAN fires again and the sequence repeats.
Valve logic to remember: Valves open/close due to pressure differences. In ventricular systole, AV valves close first; when ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure, semilunar valves open. During ventricular diastole, semilunar valves close first; when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, AV valves open.

Cardiac Cycle Duration, Stroke Volume & Cardiac Output

At 72 beats per minute, the heart performs 72 cardiac cycles per minute. Hence, duration of one cardiac cycle:
Duration of cardiac cycle = 60 s / (heart rate per minute) For 72 min−1: 60/72 ≈ 0.8 s
Stroke volume = volume pumped out by each ventricle per beat ≈ 70 mL (per cardiac cycle).
Cardiac output = volume of blood pumped out by each ventricle per minute.
Cardiac output (CO) = Stroke volume (SV) × Heart rate (HR) Using typical values: SV ≈ 70 mL, HR ≈ 72 min−1 → CO ≈ 70 × 72 = 5040 mL min−1 ≈ 5000 mL (5 L) per minute
The body can alter stroke volume and heart rate, thereby changing cardiac output. Example: Cardiac output of an athlete is much higher than that of an ordinary person (due to enhanced ability to increase SV and/or HR).

Heart Sounds (Clinical Significance)

During each cardiac cycle, two prominent sounds are produced and can be heard with a stethoscope.
First heart sound (lub)Due to closure of tricuspid and bicuspid (mitral) valves
Second heart sound (dub)Due to closure of semilunar valves
Clinical diagnostic significance: Abnormal timing/intensity of “lub-dub” can indicate valve or cardiac dysfunction.

Standard ECG: Lead Placement

To obtain a standard ECG, the patient is connected with three electrical leads:
  • One to each wrist
  • One to the left ankle
For detailed evaluation, multiple leads may be attached to the chest region, but standard ECG refers to the three-lead setup above.

Waves/Complexes in ECG (P to T) and Their Meaning

P-waveAtrial depolarisation (electrical excitation) → leads to contraction of both atria
QRS complexVentricular depolarisation → initiates ventricular contraction; contraction starts shortly after Q and marks beginning of systole
T-waveVentricular repolarisation (return to normal state); end of T marks end of systole
  • Heart rate from ECG: Count the number of QRS complexes in a given time period to determine heartbeat rate.
  • Clinical significance: ECG shape is roughly similar among individuals for a given lead configuration; deviation from normal shape indicates possible abnormality or disease.
Common confusion: P-wave is atrial activity; QRS is ventricular depolarisation (and is used to count heart rate); T-wave is ventricular repolarisation (end of systole at end of T).

Double Circulation

Double circulation refers to blood flow through two distinct pathways:
Pulmonary circulation(heart ↔ lungs)
Systemic circulation(heart ↔ body tissues)
Blood flows by a fixed route through blood vessels—arteries and veins.

Structure of Arteries and Veins (Three Layers)

Each artery and vein consists of three layers:
Tunica intimaInner lining of squamous endothelium
Tunica mediaMiddle layer of smooth muscle and elastic fibres
Tunica externaExternal fibrous connective tissue with collagen fibres
Tunica media is comparatively thin in veins (relative to arteries).

Hepatic Portal Connection (Introduction)

A unique vascular connection exists between the digestive tract and the liver (introduced here as a special connection).

Hepatic Portal System & Coronary Circulation

Hepatic portal system is a special venous pathway in which blood from the intestine first goes to the liver before entering the general (systemic) circulation.
Hepatic portal vein carries blood from intestine → liver. This ensures nutrients absorbed from the gut are processed, stored, or detoxified by the liver prior to distribution to the body.
Coronary system is a special set of blood vessels present exclusively for supplying blood to and draining blood from the cardiac musculature (heart muscle).
Think: the heart is a pump that also needs its own fuel line. The coronary circulation is the heart’s personal supply route for oxygen and nutrients.

Regulation of Cardiac Activity

Myogenic (Intrinsic) Regulation

Normal activities of the heart are regulated intrinsically (auto-regulated) by specialised muscle tissue called nodal tissue. Because the heartbeat originates in muscle (not nerves), the heart is termed myogenic.
Exam trap: Myogenic ≠ “controlled by nerves”. Nerves modify cardiac activity; they do not initiate the normal heartbeat.

Neural Regulation via Medulla Oblongata and Autonomic Nervous System (ANS)

A special neural centre in the medulla oblongata can moderate cardiac function via the autonomic nervous system (ANS).
Sympathetic nervesIncrease heartbeat rate, ventricular contraction strength, and cardiac output
Parasympathetic nervesDecrease heartbeat rate, slow conduction of action potential, and reduce cardiac output
Adrenal medullary hormonesIncrease cardiac output (hormonal support to “fight/flight”)
  • Sympathetic stimulation → ↑ heart rate, ↑ force of ventricular contraction → ↑ cardiac output.
  • Parasympathetic stimulation → ↓ heart rate, ↓ speed of action potential conduction → ↓ cardiac output.
  • Adrenal medullary hormones also act to ↑ cardiac output.
Wording to use in answers: The medulla “moderates” cardiac activity through ANS; intrinsic nodal tissue “autoreregulates” and makes heart myogenic.

Disorders of Circulatory System

High Blood Pressure (Hypertension)

Hypertension = blood pressure higher than normal.
Normal BP: 120/80 mm Hg
  • 120 mm Hg = systolic (pumping pressure)
  • 80 mm Hg = diastolic (resting pressure)
If repeated BP checks show 140/90 mm Hg or higher → indicates hypertension.
Consequences: High blood pressure leads to heart diseases and affects vital organs like brain and kidney.
Exam tip: Always write BP with units mm Hg and explicitly state systolic vs diastolic meanings.

Coronary Artery Disease (CAD) / Atherosclerosis

Coronary Artery Disease (CAD) (often referred to as atherosclerosis) affects blood vessels that supply blood to the heart muscle.
Calcium + fat + cholesterol + fibrous tissue deposit in artery wall
→
Lumen narrows
→
Reduced blood flow to cardiac muscle
Cause: deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol, and fibrous tissues in artery walls → lumen narrows → reduced blood flow to cardiac muscle.

Angina (Angina Pectoris)

Angina (also called angina pectoris) is a symptom characterised by acute chest pain when not enough oxygen reaches the heart muscle.
  • Can occur in men and women of any age, but is more common in middle-aged and elderly.
  • Occurs due to conditions that affect blood flow (hence oxygen delivery) to cardiac muscle.
Think of the heart muscle like a runner: when oxygen delivery drops, it “complains” as chest pain (angina).

Heart Failure (Congestive Heart Failure)

Heart failure = state in which the heart is not pumping blood effectively enough to meet the body’s needs.
Also called congestive heart failure because congestion of the lungs is one of the main symptoms.
Do not confuse:
  • Heart failure ≠ cardiac arrest (heart stops beating)
  • Heart failure ≠ heart attack (heart muscle suddenly damaged due to inadequate blood supply)

Chapter Summary

Body Fluids: Blood and Lymph

Blood is a fluid connective tissue that transports essential substances to cells and carries waste away. Lymph (tissue fluid) is another transport fluid, derived from blood and present in spaces between tissue cells.
Blood composition: fluid matrix plasma + formed elements.
RBCs (erythrocytes)Formed element of blood
WBCs (leucocytes)Formed element of blood
Platelets (thrombocytes)Formed element of blood
Human blood grouping (ABO): A, B, AB, O based on presence/absence of two surface antigens A and B on RBCs. Rh grouping: based on presence/absence of Rhesus (Rh) factor antigen on RBC surface.
Lymph (tissue fluid) is almost similar to blood except for:
  • protein content (lower than blood)
  • formed elements (differ from blood)

Circulatory System Type and Basic Plan

All vertebrates and a few invertebrates have a closed circulatory system. Human circulatory system consists of: heart (muscular pump) + network of vessels + blood.

Heart Structure and Pacemaker

  • Heart has two atria and two ventricles.
  • Cardiac musculature is auto-excitable.
Sino-atrial node (SAN) generates the maximum number of action potentials per minute (70–75/min), therefore it sets the pace of heart activity and is called the pacemaker.

Cardiac Cycle: Systole and Diastole

Action potential triggers contraction of atria first, then ventricles (systole), followed by relaxation (diastole).
During systole, blood is forced:
  • from atria → ventricles
  • from ventricles → pulmonary artery and aorta
Cardiac cycle = sequential events in the heart that are cyclically repeated. A healthy person shows about 72 cardiac cycles per minute.

Stroke Volume and Cardiac Output

Stroke (beat) volume = volume of blood pumped out by each ventricle in one cardiac cycle ≈ 70 mL.
Cardiac Output (CO) = Stroke Volume (SV) × Heart Rate (HR) Approximate value in a healthy adult: ~5 litres/min.
Common mistake: Stroke volume is per beat; cardiac output is per minute. Use the product SV × HR.

Electrical Activity of Heart: ECG

The electrical activity of the heart can be recorded from the body surface using an electrocardiograph; the recording is an electrocardiogram (ECG).
ECG is of clinical importance (used to assess heart electrical function).

Double Circulation: Pulmonary and Systemic

Humans have complete double circulation with two pathways: pulmonary and systemic.
Right ventricle pumps deoxygenated blood
→
Lungs (oxygenation)
→
Left atrium receives oxygenated blood
Left ventricle pumps oxygenated blood to aorta
→
Body tissues (O₂ delivered)
→
Veins return deoxygenated blood to right atrium
Although the heart is autoexcitable, its functions are moderated by neural (ANS via medulla) and hormonal (adrenal medullary hormones) mechanisms.
