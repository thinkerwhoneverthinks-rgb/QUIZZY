Handwritten Notes

ANIMAL KINGDOM

Animals show enormous diversity in structure and form (over a million described species). Therefore, classification is essential to:
  • Organise diversity into manageable groups.
  • Provide a systematic position to newly described species by comparing key features.

4.1 BASIS OF CLASSIFICATION

Animal classification is based on fundamental structural and functional features common across groups, especially:
  • Arrangement of cells (levels of organisation)
  • Body symmetry
  • Nature/presence of coelom (body cavity)
  • Patterns of digestive, circulatory, reproductive systems

4.1.1 Levels of Organisation

All animals are multicellular, but differ in how cells are organised into higher structures.
Cellular levelCells form loose aggregates; limited division of labour; seen in sponges
Tissue levelSimilar-function cells form tissues; seen in coelenterates
Organ levelTissues group to form organs (specialised functions); seen in Platyhelminthes and higher phyla
Organ system levelOrgans associate into systems for major physiological functions; seen in annelids, arthropods, molluscs, echinoderms, chordates
Complexity of organ systems varies among groups. Two exam-favourite examples:
(A) Digestive system: incomplete vs complete
Incomplete digestive system
→
Single opening = mouth + anus (example: Platyhelminthes)
Complete digestive system
→
Two openings = mouth + anus
(B) Circulatory system: open vs closed
Open circulatory system
→
Blood pumped out of heart; cells/tissues directly bathed
Closed circulatory system
→
Blood flows inside vessels: arteries, veins, capillaries
Do not mix up level of organisation with symmetry or germ layers: these are separate bases of classification tested independently.

4.1.2 Symmetry

Symmetry describes how an animal body can be divided into equal halves by planes passing through the body.
AsymmetryNo plane through the centre divides body into equal halves; mostly in sponges
Radial symmetryAny plane through central axis divides into identical halves; coelenterates, ctenophores, echinoderms
Bilateral symmetryOnly one plane divides into identical left and right halves; annelids, arthropods, etc.
Text note: Echinodermata can show radial or bilateral symmetry depending on stage (commonly tested as a trick point).

4.1.3 Diploblastic and Triploblastic Organisation

Animals are classified by the number of embryonic germ layers formed during development.
DiploblasticTwo germ layers: outer ectoderm + inner endoderm; mesoglea in between; example: coelenterates
TriploblasticThree germ layers: ectoderm + mesoderm + endoderm; group range: Platyhelminthes to Chordates
  • Mesoglea: undifferentiated layer between ectoderm and endoderm in diploblasts.
  • Mesoderm: third germ layer present between ectoderm and endoderm in triploblasts.
Diploblastic ≠ acoelomate automatically. Germ layers and coelom are related concepts but asked separately; answer as per the term asked.

4.1.4 Coelom

Coelom = body cavity between body wall and gut wall that is lined by mesoderm.
CoelomateTrue coelom lined by mesoderm; examples: annelids, molluscs, arthropods, echinoderms, hemichordates, chordates
PseudocoelomateBody cavity not lined by mesoderm; mesoderm as scattered pouches; example: aschelminthes
AcoelomateNo body cavity; example: platyhelminthes
Key discriminator: lining by mesoderm.
  • Coelom: mesoderm-lined cavity
  • Pseudocoelom: cavity present but not mesoderm-lined; mesoderm appears as scattered pouches
  • Acoelomate: cavity absent

4.1.5 Segmentation

Segmentation = body externally and internally divided into segments with serial repetition of at least some organs.
  • Example: earthworm shows metameric segmentation.
Metamerism: the phenomenon/pattern of metameric segmentation (serially repeated segments).

4.1.6 Notochord

Notochord = mesodermally derived, rod-like structure formed on the dorsal side during embryonic development in some animals.
ChordatesAnimals that possess a notochord
Non-chordatesAnimals that do not form notochord; examples: Porifera to Echinoderms
Exam phrasing often asks: “formed during embryonic development” and “mesodermally derived”—include both in definitions.

4.2 CLASSIFICATION OF ANIMALS

Using the bases above, Animalia is broadly classified. The chapter then lists characteristic features of different phyla.

HabitatGenerally marine

4.2.2 Phylum – Coelenterata (Cnidaria)

Cnidaria (Coelenterata) = aquatic animals with cnidoblasts/cnidocytes containing nematocysts (stinging capsules) + a gastro-vascular cavity with a single opening.
HabitatAquatic; mostly marine
Mode of lifeSessile or free-swimming
SymmetryRadial
OrganisationTissue level
Germ layersDiploblastic
Cnidoblasts/Cnidocytes (with nematocysts) are typically on tentacles and body and are used for:
  • Anchorage
  • Defence
  • Capture of prey
Digestive system (gastro-vascular cavity)
  • Central gastro-vascular cavity with a single opening.
  • The opening is the mouth situated on hypostome.
  • Digestion: both extracellular and intracellular.
Skeletal feature
  • Some cnidarians (e.g., corals) have skeleton of calcium carbonate.
Two basic body forms
PolypSessile, cylindrical form (e.g., Hydra, Adamsia)
MedusaUmbrella-shaped, free-swimming form (e.g., Aurelia/jelly fish)
Metagenesis (alternation of generations): in cnidarians that exist in both forms—polyp produces medusae asexually, and medusa forms polyps sexually (example: Obelia).
Examples (must recall)
PhysaliaPortuguese man-of-war
AdamsiaSea anemone (polyp form example)
PennatulaSea-pen
GorgoniaSea-fan
MeandrinaBrain coral
AureliaJelly fish (medusa form example)
Exam trap: “Coelenterata” and “Cnidaria” refer to the same phylum here; always mention cnidocytes/nematocysts as the defining character.

Phylum – Ctenophora

Ctenophores (sea walnuts/comb jellies) are exclusively marine, radially symmetrical, diploblastic animals with tissue level of organisation.

Diagnostic features

  • Locomotion: body bears 8 external rows of ciliated comb plates (ctenes) for movement.
  • Digestion: both extracellular and intracellular.
  • Bioluminescence: well-marked.
Bioluminescence = property of a living organism to emit light.

Reproduction and development

  • Sexes not separate (monoecious/hermaphrodite condition).
  • Reproduction: only sexual.
  • Fertilisation: external.
  • Development: indirect.
Ctenophores are identified in exams by the combo: marine + radial symmetry + diploblastic + 8 comb rows + bioluminescence.

Examples

  • Pleurobrachia
  • Ctenoplana

Phylum – Platyhelminthes

Platyhelminthes = dorso-ventrally flattened worms (flatworms). Mostly endoparasites in animals, including humans.

Body plan and organisation

SymmetryBilateral
Germ layersTriploblastic
Body cavityAcoelomate
OrganisationOrgan level

Parasitic adaptations and nutrition

  • Hooks and suckers present in parasitic forms (attachment to host).
  • Some absorb nutrients from host directly through body surface.

Excretion and osmoregulation

Flame cells = specialised cells that help in osmoregulation and excretion in flatworms.

Reproduction, fertilisation, development

  • Sexes not separate (typically monoecious).
  • Fertilisation: internal.
  • Development: through many larval stages (complex/indirect).

Special feature

  • Planaria shows high regeneration capacity (important named example).

Examples

  • Taenia (tapeworm)
  • Fasciola (liver fluke)
Do not mix up: Platyhelminthes = acoelomate + organ level; many other worm phyla are organ-system level.

Phylum – Aschelminthes

Aschelminthes are roundworms with a body that is circular in cross-section.

Habit and habitat

Think: “roundworms everywhere” → soil, water, and inside hosts.
  • May be free-living (aquatic/terrestrial) or parasitic in plants and animals.

Body plan and organisation

Organisationorgan-system level
Symmetrybilateral
Germ layerstriploblastic
Body cavitypseudocoelomate

Digestive and excretory systems

  • Alimentary canal: complete with a well-developed muscular pharynx.
  • Excretion: excretory tube removes wastes from body cavity through an excretory pore.

Reproduction and development

Dioecious = sexes are separate; male and female are distinct individuals.
  • Sexes: separate (dioecious); often female longer than male.
  • Fertilisation: internal.
  • Development: may be direct (young resemble adult) or indirect.

Phylum – Mollusca

Mollusca is the second largest animal phylum. Molluscs are terrestrial or aquatic (marine/freshwater) with organ-system level of organisation.

Reproduction and development

  • Development: indirect.

Phylum – Echinodermata

Echinodermata (“spiny bodied”) have an endoskeleton of calcareous ossicles. All echinoderms are marine.

Body plan and organisation

  • Organisation: organ-system level.
  • Germ layers: triploblastic.
  • Coelom: coelomate.
  • Symmetry: adults radially symmetrical; larvae bilaterally symmetrical.

Digestive system orientation

Think “mouth down, anus up” in most echinoderms: ventral feeding surface + dorsal exit.
  • Digestive system complete.
  • Mouth: on lower (ventral) side.
  • Anus: on upper (dorsal) side.

Water vascular system (most distinctive feature)

Water vascular system is the most distinctive feature of echinoderms; it helps in locomotion, capture and transport of food, and respiration.

Excretion, reproduction, development

  • Excretory system: absent.
  • Sexes: separate.
  • Reproduction: sexual.
  • Fertilisation: usually external.
  • Development: indirect with free-swimming larva.

Examples

Asteriasstar fish
Echinussea urchin
Antedonsea lily
Cucumariasea cucumber
Ophiurabrittle star
Echinodermata exam identifiers: marine only + calcareous ossicles endoskeleton + water vascular system + adult radial/larval bilateral symmetry.

Phylum Chordata: Fundamental Characters & Subphyla

Chordates are animals that (at least at some stage) show the following key diagnostic features:
  • Notochord
  • Dorsal, hollow nerve cord
  • Pharyngeal gill slits (pharynx perforated)
  • Post-anal tail
General organisation mentioned for chordates here: bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, coelomate, with organ-system level of organisation, and typically a closed circulatory system.

Chordates vs Non-chordates (Salient Differences)

These are standard exam comparisons; learn as direct point-wise contrasts.
NotochordChordates: present; Non-chordates: absent
CNS position & natureChordates: dorsal, hollow, single; Non-chordates: ventral, solid, double
Gill slitsChordates: pharynx perforated by gill slits; Non-chordates: absent
Heart positionChordates: ventral; Non-chordates: dorsal (if present)
Post-anal tailChordates: present; Non-chordates: absent
Common exam trap: “Gill slits” in chordates refers to pharyngeal slits (pharynx perforations), not simply “gills” as an organ in fishes.

Subphyla of Chordata

Phylum Chordata is divided into three subphyla:
Urochordata (Tunicata)Protochordate; marine
CephalochordataProtochordate; marine
VertebrataNotochord in embryo; vertebral column in adult
Protochordates = Urochordata + Cephalochordata; they are exclusively marine.

Urochordata (Tunicata)

Defining feature: notochord is present only in the larval tail (not persistent in adult form).
  • Examples: Ascidia, Salpa, Doliolum

Cephalochordata

Defining feature: notochord extends from head to tail and is persistent throughout life.
  • Example: Branchiostoma (Amphioxus / Lancelet)

Vertebrata: Core Features

Vertebrates possess a notochord during embryonic period, which is replaced in the adult by a cartilaginous or bony vertebral column.
Statement to learn: All vertebrates are chordates, but all chordates are not vertebrates.
In addition to basic chordate characters, vertebrates have:
  • Ventral muscular heart with 2, 3, or 4 chambers
  • Kidneys for excretion and osmoregulation
  • Paired appendages (may be fins or limbs)

Chordata: Major divisions mentioned

Agnatha vs Gnathostomata (jawless vs jawed vertebrates):
  • Some vertebrates do not possess jaws → Agnatha
  • Most possess jaws → Gnathostomata
Agnatha is represented by the class Cyclostomata (most primitive chordates; ectoparasites on fishes). Gnathostomata has two superclasses: Pisces and Tetrapoda.

Pisces: Classes Chondrichthyes and Osteichthyes; both bear fins for locomotion. Chondrichthyes: Fishes with cartilaginous endoskeleton and are marine. Tetrapoda: Classes Amphibia, Reptilia, Aves, Mammalia; have two pairs of limbs.

Key class-wise points

Quick memory hook: Tetrapoda = “two pairs of limbs” → think “4 limbs blueprint”.
  • Amphibians: Adapted to live both on land and water.
  • Reptiles: Dry and cornified skin; limbs absent in snakes.
  • Thermoregulation: Fishes, amphibians and reptiles are poikilothermous (cold-blooded).
  • Aves: Warm-blooded; body covered with feathers; forelimbs modified into wings for flying; hind limbs adapted for walking, swimming, perching or clasping.
  • Mammals: Unique features—mammary glands and hairs; commonly show viviparity.
FAR
F — Fishes (poikilothermous / cold-blooded)
A — Amphibians (poikilothermous / cold-blooded)
R — Reptiles (poikilothermous / cold-blooded)
Warm vs cold: “FAR are cold” → Fishes, Amphibians, Reptiles = poikilothermous. (Then Aves + Mammalia = warm-blooded.)
