 STRUCTURAL ORGANISATION IN ANIMALS

Introduction

Unicellular vs Multicellular organisation
  • Unicellular organisms: a single cell performs all functions (digestion, respiration, reproduction, etc.).
  • Multicellular animals: the same basic functions are performed by different groups of cells in a coordinated, organised way.
Tissue (definition): In multicellular animals, a group of similar cells along with intercellular substances that performs a specific function is called a tissue.
Only four basic tissue types form all complex animals. These tissues combine in specific proportion and pattern to form organs (e.g., stomach, lung, heart, kidney).
Organmade of one or more tissue types
Organ systemmade of two or more organs working together
Goala particular / common function
Organ: A structure formed by one or more tissue types arranged to perform a particular function. Organ system: When two or more organs perform a common function through physical and/or chemical interaction, they form an organ system (e.g., digestive system, respiratory system).
Division of labour: Cells → tissues → organs → organ systems split work among specialised units, improving efficiency and ensuring survival of the organism as a whole.

Scale idea: Hydra may have thousands of cells of each type, while humans have billions of cells—so higher organisation is essential for coordination.

Organ and Organ System

In multicellular organisms, basic tissues organise to form organs, and organs associate to form organ systems. This organisation is necessary for efficient and better-coordinated activities among the millions of cells in an organism.
  • Example (organ made of multiple tissues): The heart contains all four tissue types—epithelial, connective, muscular, and neural.
Evolutionary trend (discernable trend in complexity): The increasing complexity observed in organs and organ systems shows a pattern called an evolutionary trend (details studied later).
Morphologyform / externally visible features

Morphology: Study of form or externally visible features.
  • In plants/microbes: morphology refers precisely to external form/features.
  • In animals: morphology refers to external appearance of organs or body parts.
Anatomy: Conventionally used for the study of the morphology of internal organs in animals (i.e., internal structure/features).
This chapter introduces the morphology and anatomy of frog as a representative vertebrate.

Frogs

Habitat & classification: Frogs live both on land and in freshwater and belong to:
PhylumChordata
ClassAmphibia
Common Indian species: Rana tigrina.
Poikilothermy / cold-bloodedness: Frogs do not maintain a constant body temperature; their body temperature varies with environmental temperature. Such animals are called cold blooded or poikilotherms.
Camouflage and mimicry: Frogs can change colour to hide from enemies (camouflage). This protective coloration is called mimicry.
Dormancy in extreme seasons: Frogs may not be seen during peak summer and winter because they shelter in deep burrows:
Aestivation“summer sleep” to avoid extreme heat
Hibernation“winter sleep” to avoid extreme cold

Morphology

Skin:
  • Smooth and slippery due to presence of mucus.
  • Skin is kept moist.
  • Dorsal (back) colour: generally olive green with dark irregular spots.
  • Ventral (belly) colour: uniformly pale yellow.
  • Water intake: frog does not drink water; it absorbs water through the skin.
Think: “frog skin = sponge + slip” → stays moist and can take in water directly through the skin.
Body divisions: Body is divisible into head and trunk.
  • Neck: absent
  • Tail: absent
External features (head region):
  • Nostrils: a pair present above the mouth.
  • Eyes: bulged; covered by nictitating membrane that protects eyes while in water.
  • Tympanum (ear): a membranous tympanum on either side of eyes that receives sound signals.
Limbs and locomotion:
  • Forelimbs and hind limbs help in swimming, walking, leaping, and burrowing.
  • Hind limbs: larger and more muscular; end in five digits.
  • Fore limbs: end in four digits.
  • Webbed digits: feet have webbing to aid swimming.
Sexual dimorphism in frogs: males and females differ externally.
  • Male frog: has sound-producing vocal sacs and a copulatory pad on the first digit of forelimb.
  • Female frog: lacks vocal sacs and copulatory pad.
Exam trap: Do not mix up digit counts—hind limb = 5 digits, fore limb = 4 digits. Also, “mimicry” here refers to protective coloration associated with camouflage.

Digestive System in Frog (Feeding → Digestion → Absorption → Egestion)

Bilobed tongue: Food is captured by a muscular tongue that is bilobed at the tip.

Digestion in Stomach

  • Digestive enzymes (as part of gastric juice) participate in digestion.
  • HCl and gastric juices are secreted from the walls of the stomach.
Chyme: The partially digested food formed in the stomach is called chyme.

Role of Duodenum (First part of Small Intestine)

Chyme passes from stomach into the duodenum, which receives secretions through a common bile duct:
Bilecomes from gall bladder; emulsifies fat
Pancreatic juicecomes from pancreas; digests carbohydrates and proteins
Bile emulsifies fat (breaks fat into small droplets to aid digestion). Pancreatic juice is specifically stated here to digest carbohydrates and proteins.

Final Digestion and Absorption in Intestine

  • Final digestion takes place in the intestine.
  • Absorption occurs through numerous folds on the inner intestinal wall called villi and microvilli.
Villi and microvilli: Finger-like folds that increase the surface area for absorption of digested food.

Respiration in Frog (Aquatic vs Terrestrial)

Frogs respire in water and on land by different methods.

Respiration in Water: Cutaneous Respiration

Cutaneous respiration: In water, the skin acts as the aquatic respiratory organ; dissolved O2 is exchanged through skin by diffusion.
Think: “Underwater breathing = skin does the job like a natural sponge letting gases pass by diffusion.”

Respiration on Land: Buccal Cavity + Skin + Lungs

  • On land, buccal cavity, skin, and lungs act as respiratory organs.
  • Pulmonary respiration = respiration by lungs.
Pulmonary respiration: Gas exchange occurring through the lungs.

Lungs: Structure and Air Pathway

  • Lungs are a pair of elongated, pink-coloured, sac-like structures.
  • Located in the upper part of the trunk region (thorax).

Respiration During Aestivation and Hibernation

During aestivation and hibernation, gaseous exchange takes place through the skin.
Do not mix up: In water, skin is the main respiratory organ; on land, lungs are used (pulmonary), but skin still participates and becomes crucial during aestivation/hibernation.

Circulatory (Vascular) and Lymphatic Systems in Frog

Type and Components

Blood vascular system: Well-developed, closed type in frog.
Blood vascular systemheart + blood vessels + blood
Lymphatic systemlymph + lymph channels + lymph nodes

Heart: Position, Covering, and Chambers

  • Heart is a muscular structure in the upper part of body cavity.
  • Covered by pericardium (membrane).
  • Three chambers: two atria + one ventricle.
Sinus venosus: A triangular structure that joins the right atrium and receives blood via major veins called vena cava.
Conus arteriosus: A sac-like structure on the ventral side into which the ventricle opens.

Blood Vessels: Arteries vs Veins

  • Arteries carry blood from the heart to all parts (arterial system).
  • Veins collect blood from body parts to the heart (venous system).

Special Venous Connections (Portal Systems)

Frogs show special venous connections:
Hepatic portal systemconnection between liver and intestine
Renal portal systemconnection between kidney and lower parts of the body
Exam phrasing: Identify the “former” as hepatic portal (liver–intestine) and the “latter” as renal portal (kidney–lower body).

Blood: Composition and Key Features

Blood composition: Plasma + cells (formed elements).
RBCs (erythrocytes)nucleated; contain haemoglobin
WBCs (leucocytes)white blood cells
Plateletsblood cells listed with RBCs/WBCs
Frog RBCs are nucleated and contain the red pigment haemoglobin.

Lymph: How It Differs from Blood

Lymph: Different from blood; it lacks few proteins and RBCs.

Functions and Mechanism of Circulation

  • Blood carries nutrients, gases and water to respective sites during circulation.
  • Circulation is achieved by the pumping action of the muscular heart.

Excretory System in Frog (Nitrogenous Waste Elimination)

Organs of the Excretory System

Excretory system: pair of kidneys, ureters, cloaca, and urinary bladder.

Kidneys: Position, Appearance, and Unit Structure

  • Kidneys are compact, dark red, bean-like.
  • Situated slightly posteriorly in the body cavity on both sides of vertebral column.
Nephron / Uriniferous tubule: Each kidney is composed of several structural and functional units called uriniferous tubules or nephrons.

Ureters and Their Openings (Male vs Female)

Male frog: Two ureters emerge from kidneys and act as urinogenital ducts opening into the cloaca. Female frog: Ureters and oviduct open separately into the cloaca.

Urinary Bladder and Cloaca

  • Urinary bladder: thin-walled; present ventral to the rectum.
  • Rectum also opens into the cloaca.

Nitrogenous Waste Type

Ureotelic animal: Frog excretes urea; hence it is ureotelic.

How Excretion Occurs

Excretory wastes are carried by blood into the kidney, where they are separated and excreted.
Common exam slip: Do not write “ammonotelic” for frog; the text explicitly states ureotelic (urea).

Eyes and internal ears are well-organised structures; the remaining sense organs are described as cellular aggregations around nerve endings.

Eyes

  • Eyes are a pair of spherical structures.
  • Situated in the orbit in skull.
  • They are simple eyes (possessing only one unit).

Ear (Tympanum and Internal Ear)

  • External ear is absent in frogs.
  • Only tympanum can be seen externally.
  • Ear functions in hearing and balancing (equilibrium).
Don’t write: “Frog has external pinna.” The text states external ear is absent; tympanum is visible externally.

Reproductive System in Frog

Male Reproductive System: Organs and Ducts

  • Male reproductive organs: a pair of yellowish ovoid testes.
  • Testes are adhered to the upper part of kidneys.
  • Attached by a double fold of peritoneum called mesorchium.
Vasa efferentia: 10–12 ducts arising from testes; they enter the kidneys and open into Bidder’s canal.

Cloaca: Definition and Function (Reproductive Context)

Cloaca: A small median chamber used to pass faecal matter, urine and sperms to the exterior.

Female Reproductive System

  • Female reproductive organs include a pair of ovaries.
  • Ovaries are situated near kidneys.
  • No functional connection between ovaries and kidneys.
  • A pair of oviducts arising from ovaries opens into the cloaca separately.
A mature female can lay 2500–3000 ova at a time.

Fertilisation and Development

  • Fertilisation is external and takes place in water.
  • Development involves a larval stage called tadpole.
  • Tadpole undergoes metamorphosis to form the adult frog.
Key exam line: “Fertilisation and development are external” and larva is tadpole which metamorphoses.

Economic and Ecological Importance of Frogs

  • Beneficial for mankind: eat insects and protect crops.
  • Ecological balance: important link in food chain and food web in ecosystems.
  • In some countries, muscular legs of frog are used as food by humans.
Because frogs feed on insects, they act as a natural biological control in fields and also transfer energy between trophic levels as prey/predator in food chains.

Summary

  • Division of labour: cells → tissues → organs → organ systems ensure survival of the whole body.
  • Tissue: group of cells + intercellular substances performing one or more functions.
  • Epithelia: sheet-like tissues lining body surface and cavities/ducts/tubes; have one free surface facing a body fluid or outside environment; cells connected at junctions.
  • Common frog: Indian bullfrog, Rana tigrina.
  • Skin: vascularised; has mucous glands; helps in respiration in water and on land.
  • Body regions: head and trunk.
  • Tongue: muscular, bilobed; captures prey.
  • Alimentary canal: oesophagus, stomach, intestine, rectum; open into cloaca; main digestive glands: liver and pancreas.
  • Respiration: skin in water; lungs on land.
  • Circulation: closed with single circulation; RBCs nucleated.
  • Nervous system: central, peripheral, autonomic.
  • Urinogenital system: kidneys and urinogenital ducts open into cloaca.
  • Reproduction: testes in male; ovaries in female; female lays 2500–3000 ova; fertilisation and development are external; tadpole larva metamorphoses.
