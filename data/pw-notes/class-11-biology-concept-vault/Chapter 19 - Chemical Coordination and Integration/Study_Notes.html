CHEMICAL COORDINATION AND INTEGRATION

INTRODUCTION

Neural coordination provides point-to-point, rapid control among organs.
  • Fast but short-lived effects
  • Nerve fibres do not innervate all cells
  • Many cellular functions need continuous regulation
Chemical coordination is therefore needed and is carried out by hormones.
Neural system + endocrine system jointly coordinate and regulate physiological functions of the body.
Think of it like: nerves = instant messages (quick, short), while hormones = announcements (slower, spread out, longer regulation).

19.1 Endocrine Glands and Hormones

Endocrine glands (ductless glands) and their secretions

Endocrine glands:
  • Lack ducts → hence called ductless glands
  • Release secretions directly into body fluids (classically: blood)
Hormones are the secretions of endocrine glands.

Definitions of hormone (classical vs current)

Classical definition: A chemical produced by endocrine glands, released into the blood, transported to a distant target organ. Current scientific definition (exam wording): Hormones are non-nutrient chemicals which act as intercellular messengers and are produced in trace amounts.
The new definition is broader: it includes many new molecules beyond those secreted only by organised endocrine glands.
⚠ Don’t restrict “hormone” to only blood-borne signals from a gland—exam expects the broader “non-nutrient intercellular messenger in trace amounts” idea.

VertebratesLarge number of chemicals act as hormones to coordinate functions

19.2.1 The Hypothalamus

Location: Basal part of diencephalon (forebrain). General role: Regulates a wide spectrum of body functions. Neurosecretory nuclei: Groups of neurosecretory cells (called nuclei) that produce hypothalamic hormones.
Primary endocrine role of hypothalamus: Its hormones regulate synthesis and secretion of pituitary hormones. Two types of hypothalamic hormones (based on effect on pituitary):
Releasing hormonesStimulate secretion of pituitary hormones
Inhibiting hormonesInhibit secretion of pituitary hormones
  • Example (releasing): Gonadotrophin releasing hormone (GnRH) → stimulates pituitary synthesis and release of gonadotrophins.
  • Example (inhibiting): Somatostatin → inhibits release of growth hormone (GH) from pituitary.

19.2.3 The Pineal Gland

Location: Dorsal side of forebrain. Hormone secreted: Melatonin.
Melatonin functions:
  • Regulates 24-hour (diurnal) rhythm of the body
  • Helps maintain normal rhythms of sleep–wake cycle and body temperature
  • Influences metabolism, pigmentation, menstrual cycle, and defense capability
Think of melatonin as the body’s “night-mode timer” → cues sleep rhythms + daily cycles.

19.2.4 Thyroid Gland

Gross structure:
  • Two lobes on either side of the trachea
  • Lobes interconnected by a thin flap of connective tissue called isthmus
Microscopic organisation:
  • Composed of follicles and stromal tissues
  • Each thyroid follicle: follicular cells enclosing a cavity
Hormones synthesised by follicular cells:
  • Tetraiodothyronine (Thyroxine) = T4
  • Triiodothyronine = T3
Iodine is essential for normal rate of thyroid hormone synthesis.
  • Iodine deficiency → hypothyroidism + enlargement of thyroid gland = goitre
  • Hypothyroidism during pregnancy → defective development & maturation of baby → stunted growth (cretinism), mental (continues in next page)
Exam wording precision: Goitre is described here specifically as enlargement of thyroid gland due to iodine deficiency leading to hypothyroidism.

Thyroid Gland: Disorders & Hormonal Functions

Hypothyroidism (adult women) → may cause irregular menstrual cycle.
Hyperthyroidism → abnormally high synthesis/secretion of thyroid hormones (often due to thyroid cancer or thyroid nodules) → adverse effects on body physiology.

Exophthalmic Goitre (Graves’ Disease)

Exophthalmic goitre = a form of hyperthyroidism (also called Graves’ disease).
Characteristic features (spot these fast in exams):
  • Enlargement of the thyroid gland (goitre)
  • Protrusion of eyeballs (exophthalmos)
  • Increased basal metabolic rate (BMR)
  • Weight loss

Physiological Roles of Thyroid Hormones & Thyrocalcitonin

  • Regulate basal metabolic rate (BMR) (major role).
  • Support RBC formation.
  • Control metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins, and fats.
  • Influence maintenance of water and electrolyte balance.
Thyrocalcitonin (TCT) = protein hormone secreted by thyroid gland → regulates blood calcium levels.
In calcium balance questions: remember TCT (thyroid) and PTH (parathyroid) act together to maintain blood Ca2+ homeostasis, but in opposite directions (PTH raises Ca2+; see next section).

Parathyroid Gland (19.2.5)

Location and Number

Four parathyroid glands are present on the back side of the thyroid gland:
  • Two pairs
  • One pair in each thyroid lobe

Hormone Secreted and Regulation

Parathyroid hormone (PTH) = a peptide hormone secreted by parathyroid glands.
Regulation of PTH secretion: controlled by circulating Ca2+ levels.

Actions of PTH (Hypercalcemic Hormone)

Net effect: PTH increases blood Ca2+ level → therefore it is a hypercalcemic hormone.

Thymus (19.2.6)

Structure and Location

Thymus = lobular gland located:
  • Between the lungs
  • Behind the sternum
  • On the ventral side of the aorta

Hormones: Thymosins and Immune Functions

Thymosins = peptide hormones secreted by the thymus.
  • Major role in immune system development.
  • Promote differentiation of T-lymphocytes → responsible for cell-mediated immunity.
  • Also promote production of antibodies → supports humoral immunity.
Think of the thymus as a “training academy” where T-cells learn their roles for defence.

Adrenal Gland (19.2.7)

Position and Parts

One pair of adrenal glands, located above each kidney. Two tissues (think: “outer coat + inner core”):

Adrenal Cortex Underproduction: Addison’s Disease

Addison’s disease: due to underproduction of adrenal cortex hormones.
Effect: alters carbohydrate metabolism → causes acute weakness and fatigue.

Adrenal Medulla: Catecholamines (Fight-or-Flight)

Adrenal medulla hormones:
  • Adrenaline (epinephrine)
  • Noradrenaline (norepinephrine)
These are called catecholamines.
When released: secreted rapidly in response to stress and during emergency situations. Hence called: emergency hormones / hormones of Fight or Flight.
Alertness & eye/skin responses↑ alertness, pupillary dilation, piloerection (raising of hairs), sweating
Cardiorespiratory effects↑ heart beat, ↑ strength of heart contraction, ↑ rate of respiration
Metabolic effectsStimulate glycogen breakdown → ↑ blood glucose; also stimulate breakdown of lipids and proteins
Think “emergency fuel + emergency readiness”: catecholamines make you more alert and simultaneously push stored fuels (glycogen, fats, proteins) toward rapid availability.

Pancreas (19.2.8)

Composite Nature and Endocrine Part

Pancreas is a composite gland: functions as both exocrine and endocrine gland. Endocrine pancreas consists of Islets of Langerhans. In a normal human pancreas: about 1–2 million islets, forming only 1–2% of pancreatic tissue.

Islet Cell Types and Hormones

α-cellsSecrete glucagon
β-cellsSecrete insulin

Glucagon: Hyperglycemic Hormone

Glucagon: a peptide hormone important for maintaining normal blood glucose levels.
Main target: liver cells (hepatocytes).
  • Stimulates glycogenolysis → ↑ blood sugar (hyperglycemia).
  • Stimulates gluconeogenesis → further contributes to hyperglycemia.
  • Reduces cellular glucose uptake and utilisation.
Conclusion: glucagon is a hyperglycemic hormone.

Insulin: Promotes Hypoglycemia and Glucose Storage

Insulin: a peptide hormone with a major role in glucose homeostasis.
Main targets: hepatocytes and adipocytes (cells of adipose tissue).
  • Enhances cellular glucose uptake and utilisation.
  • Causes rapid movement of glucose from blood to target cells → decreases blood glucose (hypoglycemia).
  • Stimulates conversion of glucose to glycogen (glycogenesis) in target cells.
Blood glucose homeostasis is maintained jointly by insulin and glucagon.
Exam trap: don’t mix up processes—glucagon drives glycogenolysis + gluconeogenesis (raises glucose), while insulin drives uptake + glycogenesis (lowers glucose).

Diabetes Mellitus and Insulin Therapy

Diabetes mellitus: results from prolonged hyperglycemia.
  • Associated with loss of glucose through urine.
  • Associated with formation of harmful compounds called ketone bodies.
  • Treatment: diabetic patients are successfully treated with insulin therapy.

Testis (19.2.9)

Location and Dual Role

Testis: a pair located in the scrotal sac (outside abdomen) in males. Dual function: primary sex organ + endocrine gland.
Outside the abdomen = cooler environment → helps sperm formation stay efficient.

Structure and Endocrine Cells

Testis is composed of:
Seminiferous tubulesSite associated with sperm formation
Stromal / interstitial tissueContains endocrine cells
Leydig (interstitial) cells in intertubular spaces secrete androgens, mainly testosterone.

Functions of Androgens (Testosterone)

  • Regulate development, maturation and function of male accessory sex organs: epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate gland, urethra, etc.
  • Stimulate muscular growth and male secondary sexual characters: facial and axillary hair, aggressiveness, low-pitched voice, etc.
  • Major stimulatory role in spermatogenesis (formation of spermatozoa).
  • Act on central neural system and influence male sexual behaviour (libido).
  • Produce anabolic (synthetic) effects on protein and carbohydrate metabolism.
Don’t write “testis produces sperm” alone—mention the endocrine role too: Leydig cells → testosterone.

19.3 Hormones of Heart, Kidney and Gastrointestinal Tract

Besides “classical endocrine glands”, some non-endocrine tissues also secrete hormones (important exam point). Key examples: heart, kidney, gastro-intestinal tract.

Hormone of Heart: Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF)

Atrial natriuretic factor (ANF) is a peptide hormone secreted by the atrial wall of the heart that decreases blood pressure.

Write the trigger-effect clearly in exams: increased BP stimulates ANF, which causes vasodilation, lowering BP.

Hormone of Kidney: Erythropoietin

Erythropoietin is a peptide hormone produced by juxtaglomerular cells of the kidney that stimulates erythropoiesis.
Erythropoiesis = formation of RBCs (red blood cells).

Hormones of Gastrointestinal (G-I) Tract

Endocrine cells present in different parts of the gastro-intestinal tract secrete four major peptide hormones:
GastrinActs on gastric glands; stimulates HCl + pepsinogen secretion
SecretinActs on exocrine pancreas; stimulates water + bicarbonate secretion
Cholecystokinin (CCK)Acts on pancreas & gall bladder; stimulates pancreatic enzymes + bile juice
Gastric inhibitory peptide (GIP)Inhibits gastric secretion and motility
  • Gastrin → gastric glands → ↑ hydrochloric acid (HCl) and ↑ pepsinogen
  • Secretin → exocrine pancreas → ↑ water and ↑ bicarbonate ions (HCO3−)
  • CCK → pancreas + gall bladder → ↑ pancreatic enzymes and ↑ bile juice (respectively)
  • GIP → ↓ gastric secretion and ↓ gastric motility
Think digestion control as “signal hormones” released from the gut to coordinate secretions: acid/enzymes/bile vs slowing the stomach when needed (GIP).

Growth Factors from Non-endocrine Tissues

Several other non-endocrine tissues secrete hormones called growth factors.
Growth factors: importance
  • Essential for normal growth of tissues
  • Essential for repairing/regeneration of tissues

19.4 Mechanism of Hormone Action

Hormone Receptors and Target Specificity

Hormones act on target tissues by binding to specific proteins called hormone receptors that are located only in target tissues.
Specificity principle: Each receptor is specific to one hormone only; therefore, receptors are specific and determine where a hormone can act.
Think: a hormone is like a “key” — only tissues with the matching “lock” (receptor) respond.
Types of receptors (by location):
Membrane-bound receptorsPresent on cell membrane of target cells
Intracellular receptorsPresent inside target cells; mostly nuclear receptors (in nucleus)

Hormones that interact with membrane-bound receptors normally do not enter the target cell. Instead, they generate second messengers that regulate cellular metabolism.
Second messengers (examples named): cyclic AMP (cAMP), IP3, Ca++.
