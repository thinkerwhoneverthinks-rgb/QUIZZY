Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

TOPIC1

INTRODUCTION AND IMPORTANCE: HALOALKANES & HALOARENES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Organo-halogen compounds: hydrocarbons where one or more H atoms are replaced by halogen (F, Cl, Br, I).
  • Replacement may occur in aliphatic chains or aromatic rings, giving different families of halides.
  • Haloalkanes (alkyl halides): halogen attached to an sp3 carbon of an alkyl group.
  • Haloarenes (aryl halides): halogen attached directly to an sp2 carbon of an aromatic ring.
  • Many organo-halogen compounds occur naturally; many others are synthetic and widely used.
  • Environmental note: many halogenated organics resist bacterial degradation and may persist in soil.

🖼️Diagram

Classification is based on where X is attached: alkyl sp3 carbon vs aromatic sp2 carbon.

🎯Key Concepts

  • Core idea: H → X substitution in hydrocarbons gives organo-halogen compounds.
  • Haloalkane = alkyl halide with C(sp3)–X bond (R–X).
  • Haloarene = aryl halide with C(sp2)–X bond (Ar–X).
  • Applications: solvents (for relatively non-polar compounds) and starting materials in synthesis.
  • Must-remember examples: chloramphenicol, thyroxine, chloroquine, halothane.
  • Persistence: many halogenated compounds resist breakdown by soil bacteria → environmental concern.

🔎WHY THIS CHAPTER MATTERS (JEE/NEET/BOARDS)

  • Haloalkanes/haloarenes are key intermediates in multi-step organic synthesis.
  • Used as solvents for relatively non-polar substances (lab + industry).
  • Polar C–X bond makes C electrophilic → many conversions (alcohols, amines, nitriles).
  • Many halogenated organics show environmental persistence.

⚙️REAL-LIFE LINKS: NATURAL + CLINICAL + ENVIRONMENT

  • Chloramphenicol (contains Cl): antibiotic produced by microorganisms; used against typhoid.
  • Thyroxine (contains I): hormone produced in body; deficiency leads to goiter.
  • Chloroquine: synthetic organo-halogen used in malaria treatment.
  • Halothane: halogenated compound used as an anaesthetic.
  • Some fully fluorinated compounds are considered potential blood substitutes in surgery.

TOPIC2

CLASSIFICATION OF HALIDES (HALOALKANES & HALOARENES)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Halides (organo‑halogen compounds) are hydrocarbons where one/more H are replaced by X (F, Cl, Br, I).
  • Basis 1: number of halogen atoms → monohalo, dihalo, polyhalo (≥3).
  • Basis 2 (for monohalo mainly): hybridisation/type of carbon bearing the C–X bond.
  • sp3 C–X compounds: alkyl, allylic, benzylic halides (X on tetrahedral carbon).
  • sp2 C–X compounds: vinylic and aryl halides (X on trigonal carbon).
  • Key trap: allylic/benzylic have X on sp3 carbon next to π system; vinylic/aryl have X on sp2 carbon of π system.

🖼️Diagram

Two exam bases: count of X atoms and hybridisation of the carbon directly bonded to X.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔢Monohalo/dihalo/polyhalo classification depends only on count of X atoms.
  • ⚛️sp3 C–X: alkyl (R–X), allylic, benzylic.
  • 🧬sp2 C–X: vinylic (on C=C) and aryl (on benzene ring).
  • 🏷️Primary/secondary/tertiary: defined by degree of carbon directly bonded to X (only for alkyl/benzylic/allylic sp3).
  • 🧩Homologous series of alkyl halides: CnH2n+1X (monohalo, saturated, acyclic).

🔎A. CLASSIFICATION BY NUMBER OF HALOGENS

  • Monohalo: contains one X atom (e.g., C2H5Cl).
  • Dihalo: contains two X atoms; can be geminal (same C) or vicinal (adjacent C).
  • Polyhalo: three or more X atoms (trihalo, tetrahalo, etc.).
  • In reactions/isomer counting, note: further substitution often gives polyhalo mixtures.

⚙️B. CLASSIFICATION BY NATURE OF CARBON BEARING C–X (MOST TESTED)

  • Alkyl halide (R–X): X on sp3 carbon of an alkyl group.
  • Allylic halide: X on sp3 carbon adjacent to C=C (allylic carbon).
  • Benzylic halide: X on sp3 carbon attached to benzene ring (benzylic carbon).
  • Vinylic halide: X directly on sp2 carbon of C=C (vinylic carbon).
  • Aryl halide (haloarene): X directly on sp2 carbon of aromatic ring.

⭐Important Points

  • Always classify using the carbon directly bonded to X, not the nearby unsaturation.
  • Allylic/benzylic: X on sp3 carbon adjacent to π system (often highly reactive in substitution).
  • Vinylic/aryl: X on sp2 carbon of π system (different behaviour from alkyl halides).
  • Primary/secondary/tertiary refers to degree of the C bearing X (count carbon neighbours).
  • Monohalo alkyl halides form homologous series CnH2n+1X (saturated, open chain).

TOPIC3

NOMENCLATURE AND ISOMERISM (HALOALKANES/ARYL HALIDES: KEY NAMING POINTS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Nomenclature assigns a unique name to a haloalkane/haloarene from its structure (or vice versa).
  • Common system: alkyl group name + halide (chloride/bromide/iodide/fluoride).
  • IUPAC: halogen as prefix (fluoro/chloro/bromo/iodo) on parent hydrocarbon with lowest locants.
  • In benzene di-substitution, o/m/p (common) corresponds to 1,2 / 1,3 / 1,4 (IUPAC).
  • Isomerism: same molecular formula, different connectivity (structural isomers), e.g., C5H11Br has 8 isomers.
  • For classification 1°/2°/3°, check the carbon bearing X, not the chain end or overall branching.

🖼️Diagram

One-glance map: o/m/p ↔ 1,2/1,3/1,4 and geminal vs vicinal dihalides.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🏷️Common: ethyl chloride; IUPAC: chloroethane (halo-substituted alkane).
  • 🔢Benzene dihalides: o- = 1,2; m- = 1,3; p- = 1,4 (do not mix styles).
  • 🧷Geminal: both X on same carbon; Vicinal: X on adjacent carbons.
  • 🧠Common terms: alkylidene (gem) and alkylene (vic); IUPAC uses dihalo + numbering.
  • 🧩Structural isomers of monobromoalkanes (e.g., C5H11Br): choose carbon skeleton, then place Br at non-equivalent positions.

🔎COMMON VS IUPAC: WHAT TO WRITE IN EXAMS

  • Common (alkyl halides): alkyl group + halide; e.g., ethyl chloride.
  • IUPAC: halogen prefix + parent hydrocarbon; e.g., chloroethane.
  • Number parent chain to give lowest set of locants to substituents (including halo).
  • Aryl halide: halogen directly on benzene ring, e.g., chlorobenzene (common and IUPAC same for monosubstituted benzene).
  • Di-substituted benzene: use either o/m/p (common) or 1,2 / 1,3 / 1,4 (IUPAC), not both.

⚙️MUST-KNOW NAME PAIRINGS (HIGH FREQUENCY)

  • ethyl chloride ↔ chloroethane.
  • sec-butyl chloride ↔ 2-chlorobutane.
  • tert-butyl bromide ↔ 2-bromo-2-methylpropane.
  • vinyl chloride ↔ chloroethene (X on sp2 carbon).
  • allyl bromide ↔ 3-bromopropene (X on adjacent sp3 carbon).

  • Write either o/m/p or 1,2 / 1,3 / 1,4 for benzene di-substitution; do not combine.
  • In IUPAC, choose parent chain/ring first, then give halogen prefix with lowest set of locants.
  • Geminal/vicinal distinction is about relative position of halogens, not whether they are same/different halogens.
  • Vinyl: halogen directly on C=C carbon (sp2); Allyl: halogen on adjacent carbon (sp3).
  • For 1°/2°/3° label, count carbon substitution at the carbon attached to X.

TOPIC4

NATURE OF C–X BOND

📖Definition & Introduction

  • C–X bond is polarised because halogen (X) is more electronegative than carbon.
  • Carbon develops partial positive charge: Cδ+; halogen develops partial negative charge: Xδ−.
  • Down the group (F → I), atomic size increases, so C–X bond length increases.
  • Longer C–X bonds are generally weaker, so bond enthalpy decreases from C–F to C–I.
  • Dipole moment depends on both charge separation and bond length; hence it need not vary monotonically down the group.

🖼️Diagram

Down the group: bond length ↑, bond enthalpy ↓; C is δ+ and X is δ−.

🎯Key Concepts

  • Polar bond: Cδ+–Xδ− makes carbon electrophilic in haloalkanes.
  • Bond length order: C–F < C–Cl < C–Br < C–I (size of halogen increases).
  • Bond enthalpy order: C–F > C–Cl > C–Br > C–I (bond weakens as it lengthens).
  • Dipole moment in CH3X: CH3Cl is highest; don’t assume simple trend.
  • Table 6.2 (methyl halides) links C–X bond strength to ease of C–X bond cleavage across R–F to R–I.

🔎 METHYL HALIDE (CH3–X) DATA YOU MUST USE

  • Bond lengths (pm): CH3F 139, CH3Cl 178, CH3Br 193, CH3I 214.
  • Bond enthalpy (kJ mol⁻¹): 452, 351, 293, 234 respectively (F → I: decreases).
  • Dipole moment (D): CH3F 1.847; CH3Cl 1.860; CH3Br 1.830; CH3I 1.636.
  • Exam inference: CH3Cl has slightly higher μ than CH3F; don’t guess trend.

⚙️EXAM LINK: WHY THESE TRENDS MATTER

  • Weaker C–X bond (lower bond enthalpy) usually means easier C–X bond cleavage in reactions.
  • Leaving group ability (same R): R–I > R–Br > R–Cl >> R–F is consistent with weakening C–X bond from R–F to R–I.
  • Polarity makes carbon electrophilic; nucleophiles attack the C bearing X in substitution reactions.
  • For comparisons, always state: size ↑ → bond length ↑ → bond enthalpy ↓ (F → I).

⭐Important Points

  • Always draw polarity correctly: Cδ+–Xδ−; carbon is the electrophilic centre.
  • Bond length order: C–F < C–Cl < C–Br < C–I (must be written exactly).
  • Bond enthalpy decreases F → I; C–F is strongest, C–I is weakest in CH3X.
  • Dipole moment is data-driven: for CH3X, CH3Cl is slightly higher than CH3F.
  • Use Table 6.2 values when asked for “highest/lowest”; do not rely on guessed periodic trends.

TOPIC5

PREPARATION OF HALOALKANES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Preparation of haloalkanes means converting an aliphatic compound into R–X (X = F, Cl, Br, I).
  • Most common lab route: alcohol (R–OH) → haloalkane (R–X) by replacing –OH.
  • Alkanes give R–X by free-radical halogenation, usually as product mixtures.
  • Alkenes give haloalkanes by addition of HX or X2 across C=C.
  • Halogen exchange converts one R–X into another: Finkelstein (I) and Swarts (F).

🖼️Diagram

Four major preparation routes to haloalkanes: from alcohols, alkanes, alkenes, and halogen exchange.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔁Alcohol → R–X: 3° reacts fastest with HX; 1°/2° need stronger conditions/catalyst.
  • 🧂SOCl2 gives R–Cl with gaseous by-products SO2 + HCl → purer product.
  • 🧯Alkane halogenation is radical: many isomers + polyhalogenation → low selectivity.
  • 📍Alkene + HX: major product usually follows Markovnikov orientation; anti-Markovnikov occurs only in special cases (peroxide effect with HBr).
  • 🧪Br2/CCl4 decolourises with alkenes (addition → vic-dibromide).
  • ⚖️Finkelstein driven by NaCl/NaBr precipitation in dry acetone.

🔎1) FROM ALCOHOLS (R–OH → R–X): HIGH-YIELD LAB METHODS

  • With HX: R–OH + HX → R–X + H2O (HCl/HBr/HI).
  • HCl: 1°/2° alcohols need ZnCl2 (Lucas conditions); 3° reacts with conc. HCl at room temp.
  • Reactivity (same HX): 3° > 2° > 1° (due to ease of C–O bond cleavage).
  • With SOCl2: R–OH → R–Cl + SO2(g) + HCl(g) (gases escape → purer R–Cl).
  • With phosphorus halides: PCl3/PCl5/PBr3/PI3 convert alcohols to R–Cl/R–Br/R–I (PBr3, PI3 often generated in situ from red P + Br2/I2).

⚙️2) FROM ALKANES, ALKENES & HALOGEN EXCHANGE (EXAM FAVOURITES)

  • Alkanes (radical): R–H + Cl2/Br2 hν/Δ → mixture of mono- and polyhaloalkanes.
  • Why mixture: substitution can occur at different non-equivalent H sites; further substitution gives polyhalo products.
  • Monochloro isomer count: equals number of distinct H-types (non-equivalent H environments) in the alkane.
  • Alkenes + HX: addition across C=C gives haloalkane; major product follows Markovnikov orientation under normal conditions.
  • Alkenes + Br2 (CCl4): reddish-brown Br2 decolourises; product is vic-dibromide.

  • For HCl: ZnCl2 is required mainly for 1° and 2° alcohols; 3° works with conc. HCl at room temp.
  • For R–Br: remember constant boiling HBr (48%) as the standard reagent.
  • For R–I: use NaI/KI + 95% H3PO4 (better yields than direct HI in many lab settings).
  • PBr3/PI3 are often written as formed in situ from red P + Br2/I2.
  • Free radical halogenation gives mixtures; low selectivity is a key limitation in synthesis questions.

TOPIC6

PREPARATION OF HALOARENES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Haloarenes (aryl halides) are prepared mainly by (i) electrophilic substitution on arenes and (ii) substitution via aryl diazonium salts.
  • In EAS, Cl/Br require Lewis acid catalysts (Fe/FeCl3) to generate the electrophile and activate halogenation.
  • Iodination is reversible; an oxidising agent removes HI to drive equilibrium towards iodoarene.
  • Fluoroarenes are not prepared by direct fluorination (too reactive; poor selectivity).
  • From aromatic amines, diazotisation in cold aqueous mineral acid forms diazonium salts that undergo halide substitution.

🖼️Diagram

One-page map: EAS (Cl/Br) and diazonium route (Cl/Br via Cu(I), I via KI).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🟦EAS: Ar–H + X2 (Lewis acid) → Ar–X + HX; X = Cl, Br
  • 🟨Ortho/para products form in substituted arenes; o- and p-isomers often separable by melting-point difference
  • 🟧Iodination needs oxidant: HI is oxidised back to I2 (drives forward, reduces reversibility)
  • 🟩Diazotisation: Ar–NH2 + NaNO2 + HX (cold) → Ar–N2+X−
  • 🟪Sandmeyer: Ar–N2+ + CuCl/CuBr → Ar–Cl/Ar–Br; with KI gives Ar–I (no Cu needed)

🔎ROUTE 1: ELECTROPHILIC SUBSTITUTION ON ARENES (EAS)

  • Chlorination/bromination: benzene + Cl2/Br2 in presence of Fe or FeCl3.
  • Catalyst role: generates effective electrophile (Cl+/Br+-like species) for ring substitution.
  • In substituted arenes (e.g., toluene): mixture of ortho and para halo derivatives common.
  • Practical note: o- and p-isomers may be separated due to large melting-point difference (p often higher).
  • Iodination: reversible; use oxidant (HNO3 or HIO4) to oxidise HI and drive forward.

⚙️ROUTE 2: FROM AROMATIC AMINES VIA DIAZOTISATION + SUBSTITUTION

  • Step A (diazotisation): primary aromatic amine + NaNO2 in cold aqueous mineral acid → Ar–N2+X−.
  • Reason for cold: diazonium salts are stable only at low temperature; warm conditions cause side reactions/decomposition.
  • Step B (Sandmeyer): diazonium salt + CuCl gives Ar–Cl; + CuBr gives Ar–Br.
  • For iodine: diazonium salt + KI (Cu(I) salt not required) → Ar–I.
  • Exam phrasing: always write freshly prepared diazonium salt and cold conditions.

TOPIC7

PHYSICAL PROPERTIES TRENDS (HALOALKANES & HALOARENES)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Physical properties of haloalkanes/haloarenes follow trends in polarity, molar mass, and packing.
  • Most pure haloalkanes are generally colourless; many are volatile with sweet smell.
  • Intermolecular forces: dipole–dipole + London (van der Waals) → higher b.p. than similar hydrocarbons.
  • Solubility: very slightly soluble in water but dissolve well in organic solvents (energy + H-bond arguments).
  • Density rises with carbon number, number of halogens, and atomic mass of halogen.

🖼️Diagram

Remember: b.p. mainly tracks van der Waals; m.p. often tracks symmetry/packing.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🎨Many alkyl bromides and iodides develop colour on light due to slow decomposition releasing Br2/I2.
  • 🌡️For same R: b.p. order R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F (↑polarizability/van der Waals).
  • 🧱Branching lowers b.p. (↓surface area → weaker dispersion forces).
  • 📦p-Dihalobenzenes have higher m.p. than o-/m- due to symmetry → better crystal packing.
  • 💧Low water solubility: breaking water H-bonds costs more energy than haloalkane–water attractions release.
  • ⚖️Many bromo/iodo and polychloro compounds are denser than water (ρ > 1 g mL−1).

🔎COLOUR, ODOUR & PHYSICAL STATE

  • Pure haloalkanes are generally colourless; coloured samples often indicate impurities/decomposition.
  • Many alkyl bromides and iodides develop colour on light exposure (release of Br2/I2).
  • Many low-molar-mass haloalkanes are volatile and may have a sweet odour.
  • Room-temperature states: CH3Cl, CH3Br, C2H5Cl and some chlorofluoromethanes are gases; higher members are liquids/solids.

⚙️b.p./m.p. TRENDS: HALOGEN, SIZE & BRANCHING + SPECIAL p-DIHALOBENZENES

  • Compared to hydrocarbons of similar molar mass: haloalkanes have higher b.p. (dipole–dipole + dispersion).
  • Same alkyl group: b.p. order R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F due to ↑size/↑polarizability → stronger London forces.
  • With increasing molecular size/electron count in a series: b.p. increases (stronger dispersion).
  • Isomeric haloalkanes: more branching → lower b.p. (smaller surface area reduces van der Waals contact).
  • Isomeric dihalobenzenes: b.p. nearly same, but p-isomer has higher m.p. (symmetry → tight lattice packing).

⭐Important Points

  • Write exact b.p. trend (same R): R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F (polarizability dominates).
  • Boiling points of haloalkanes are higher than comparable hydrocarbons due to dipole–dipole + van der Waals.
  • Isomeric haloalkanes: branching decreases b.p. (surface area argument).
  • Dihalobenzenes: b.p. almost same, but p-isomer m.p. highest (symmetry/packing).
  • Water solubility: very slightly soluble; reason must mention breaking water H-bonds and weak solute–solvent forces.

TOPIC8

NUCLEOPHILIC SUBSTITUTION

Definition & Introduction
  • Nucleophilic substitution: an electron-rich nucleophile (Nu) replaces halogen X in R–X to form R–Nu.
  • In haloalkanes, the C bonded to X is δ+; it is the electrophilic centre attacked by Nu.
  • The halogen departs as X−; hence halide ion is the leaving group.
  • General change: R–X → R–Nu (bond making C–Nu and bond breaking C–X).
  • Ambident nucleophiles have two attack sites (e.g., CN−, NO2−), giving different products.
  • Counter-ion / bonding nature (more ionic vs more covalent) can control which atom attacks (C vs N; O vs N).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🎯Nu is electron rich (often negative/with lone pair); it targets C(δ+) of R–X.
  • 🚪Leaving group is halide ion: X leaves as X− after taking the bonding pair.
  • 🔁Product class depends on Nu: HO−→alcohol, RO−→ether, NH3→amine, CN−→nitrile.
  • 🧷Ambident Nu: same anion, two linkage options; reagent decides major product (KCN vs AgCN; KNO2 vs AgNO2).
  • ⚖️Generally, K salts behave more ionic in solution (free anion). Ag salts show higher covalent/coordination effects, changing the attacking atom in ambident nucleophiles.

🔎COMMON NUCLEOPHILES → TYPICAL PRODUCTS (R–X → R–Nu)

  • NaOH/KOH (HO−): gives alcohol ROH.
  • H2O: gives ROH (hydrolysis; slower than HO−).
  • NaOR′ (R′O−): gives ether ROR′.
  • NaI (I−): gives alkyl iodide R–I (halogen exchange type substitution).
  • NH3: gives primary amine RNH2.

⚙️AMBIDENT NUCLEOPHILES: PRODUCT CONTROL (CN−, NO2−)

  • Ambident = two different donor atoms; product depends on which atom bonds to carbon.
  • CN−: C-attack → RCN (nitrile); N-attack → RNC (isocyanide).
  • KCN (more ionic in solution): gives free CN−; C-attack predominates → RCN major.
  • AgCN (higher covalent/coordination character): Ag+ binds/coordinates more with C end, so N-attack predominates → RNC major.
  • NO2−: O-attack → R–O–N=O (alkyl nitrite); N-attack → R–NO2 (nitroalkane).

⚖️AMBIDENT NUCLEOPHILES: REAGENT → MAJOR PRODUCT

Reagent Major product Key exam reasoning
KCN + R–X RCN (nitrile) KCN is ionic in solution → free CN−; C-attack predominates
AgCN + R–X RNC (isocyanide) AgCN has higher covalent character; Ag+ binds/coordinates more with C end, so N-end attacks → RNC major
KNO2 + R–X R–O–N=O (alkyl nitrite) Ionic nitrite in solution; O-attack predominates
AgNO2 + R–X R–NO2 (nitroalkane) Higher covalent character; N-attack predominates

⭐Important Points

  • Always label: substrate = R–X, nucleophile = Nu, leaving group = X−.
  • Key transformation: R–X → R–Nu; new bond is C–Nu, broken bond is C–X.
  • In product prediction, state both: nucleophile used and class of product (alcohol/ether/amine/nitrile/alkyl iodide).
  • Ambident nucleophiles give two linkage isomers; reagent decides major product.
  • For KCN vs AgCN and KNO2 vs AgNO2, mention relative ionic vs covalent/coordination character explicitly.

💡Memory Technique

KCN → C-attack → RCN (think: K with C for cyanide). AgCN → N-attack → RNC (Ag shifts linkage to N). KNO2 → O-attack → R–ONO (K with O). AgNO2 → N-attack → R–NO2 (Ag with N).
TOPIC9
One-step bimolecular substitution with backside attack → Walden inversion; steric hindrance controls rate

SN2 MECHANISM AND INVERSION

📖Definition & Introduction

  • SN2 is a one-step nucleophilic substitution where C–Nu forms and C–X breaks simultaneously.
  • It is bimolecular: rate depends on both substrate (alkyl halide) and nucleophile concentrations.
  • Attack occurs only by backside approach (opposite the leaving group) due to orbital overlap and repulsion factors.
  • The reaction passes through a single transition state (no intermediate) with partial bonds to Nu and X.
  • Backside attack causes inversion of configuration at the stereocentre (Walden inversion).

🖼️Diagram

SN2: nucleophile attacks from the side opposite X, giving inversion at C*.

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⚡Rate law: Rate = k[R–X][Nu⁻] (second order overall).
  • 🧩Geometry: transition state has five groups around C (3 substituents + Nu + X, all partial bonds).
  • 🔄Stereochemistry: inversion like an umbrella turning inside out.
  • 🚧Steric control: methyl > 1° > 2° >> 3° for SN2 reactivity.
  • 🧠Do not use carbocation stability to explain SN2 (no carbocation forms).

🔎ONE-STEP MECHANISM (HOW IT PROCEEDS)

  • Step is concerted: bond making and bond breaking occur in the same event.
  • Nu attacks the electrophilic carbon (Cδ+) from backside of the C–X bond.
  • Transition state: Nu and X are both partially bonded to carbon (high-energy, not isolable).
  • As Nu approaches, the three substituents at carbon become nearly planar (umbrella-flip moment).
  • Leaving group departs as X⁻ while the C–Nu bond forms (simultaneous bond changes).

⚙️STEREOCHEMISTRY: WALDEN INVERSION

  • If the reacting carbon is chiral, SN2 gives inversion of configuration (often essentially complete).
  • Backside approach forces the tetrahedral arrangement to flip: umbrella turning inside out.
  • Product has opposite spatial arrangement at that carbon compared to reactant (inversion, not retention).

⚖️SN2 REACTIVITY: STERIC HINDRANCE TREND

Basis Methyl / 1° (favoured) 2° (slower) 3° (very slow / no SN2)
Crowding at reacting carbon Minimum; Nu approaches easily Moderate; approach partially blocked Maximum; backside approach blocked

Key reason to write Steric hindrance least Steric hindrance increases Backside attack not possible
Common trap Do not invoke carbocation ideas Do not mix with SN1 logic Carbocation stability is irrelevant for SN2

⭐Important Points

  • SN2 is concerted: no carbocation, so no rearrangement intermediate.
  • Rate law: Rate = k[R–X][Nu⁻] (depends on both nucleophile and substrate).
  • Backside attack gives Walden inversion at a chiral reacting centre.
  • Transition state: carbon has five attached groups (two partial bonds + three substituents); substituents are nearly planar.
  • Reactivity controlled mainly by steric hindrance: methyl > 1° > 2° >> 3°.

💡Memory Technique

“SN2 = 'Backside 2-body, Umbrella Flip'”
Single step; Nucleophile + substrate = 2 bodies in the rate law. Backside attack → imagine umbrella in wind → inversion. More crowding near C → slower: Me > 1° > 2° >> 3°.

📌Quick Recap

TOPIC10

SN1 MECHANISM AND FACTORS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • SN1 is nucleophilic substitution where the rate-determining step is unimolecular ionisation of R–X.
  • It proceeds in two steps: slow C–X cleavage → carbocation, then fast nucleophile attack.
  • Rate depends only on [alkyl halide]; nucleophile concentration does not affect rate.
  • Favoured by polar protic solvents (H2O, ROH, CH3COOH) that stabilise ions by solvation.
  • Faster for substrates giving more stable carbocations: 3° > 2° > 1°, and allylic/benzylic are highly reactive.

🖼️Diagram

SN1: slow ionisation forms carbocation; solvent stabilises ions; fast nucleophile capture gives product.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🐢Slow step: ionisation R–X → R+ + X−; often reversible
  • ⚡Fast step: nucleophile attacks planar carbocation → substitution product
  • 💧Polar protic solvents solvate X− and R+ → lower barrier for ionisation
  • 🏛️Carbocation stability controls SN1: 3° > 2° > 1°; allylic/benzylic stabilised by resonance
  • 🚪Leaving group ability (same R): RI > RBr > RCl >> RF (I− leaves easiest)
  • 🔁SN1 vs SN2: SN1 follows carbocation stability; SN2 follows steric hindrance

🔎TWO-STEP MECHANISM (WHAT TO WRITE IN EXAM)

  • Step 1 (slow, RDS): C–X bond breaks → R+ + X− (often reversible).
  • Step 2 (fast): Nu attacks carbocation → R–Nu; deprotonation if Nu is neutral (H2O/ROH).
  • Carbocation is sp2, planar; attack can occur from either face → often racemisation in chiral cases.
  • Energy barrier mainly for step 1; anything stabilising ions speeds up reaction.

⚙️FACTORS CONTROLLING SN1 RATE (FAST → SLOW)

  • Carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1° (inductive + hyperconjugation effects).
  • Resonance stabilisation: allylic and benzylic halides react very fast (positive charge delocalised).
  • Solvent: polar protic solvents (H2O, alcohol, acetic acid) stabilise R+ and X− by solvation.
  • Leaving group: RI > RBr > RCl >> RF; weaker C–X bond and more stable X− leave faster.
  • Substrate note: aryl/vinylic halides do not undergo SN1 (unstable phenyl/vinyl cations).

⚖️SN1 vs SN2 (HIGH-YIELD COMPARISON)

Substrate preference 3° > 2° > 1° (carbocation stability); allylic/benzylic often very fast CH3 > 1° > 2° >> 3° (steric hindrance)

⭐Important Points

  • Always state: Step 1 slow (ionisation) controls rate; hence Rate ∝ [R–X].
  • Polar protic solvents speed SN1 by solvation of both R+ and X−.
  • Carbocation stability is the key substrate factor: 3° > 2° > 1°; allylic/benzylic are exceptionally fast.
  • Leaving group ability: RI > RBr > RCl >> RF; iodide is best leaving group.
  • For butyl bromide isomers (as substrates): SN1 reactivity tert-butyl > sec-butyl > isobutyl > n-butyl; SN2 reactivity n-butyl > isobutyl > sec-butyl >> tert-butyl.

💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: Slow Now = 1 molecule”
SN1: Slow step is Now only 1 reactant (R–X) breaking. Carbocation loves 3° and resonance (allyl/benzyl). Leaving group: think I goes out first: I > Br > Cl >> F.

STEREOCHEMISTRY AND OUTCOMES (HALOALKANES)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Optical activity is rotation of plane polarised light by a chiral compound’s solution.
  • Plane polarised light is obtained by passing ordinary light through a Nicol prism.
  • Angle and direction of rotation are measured using a polarimeter.
  • Chiral objects are non-superimposable on their mirror images; achiral are superimposable.
  • An asymmetric carbon (stereocentre) is a tetrahedral C attached to four different groups.

🖼️Diagram

Big picture: chirality causes optical activity; mechanism controls retention/inversion/racemisation.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔦Dextrorotatory: rotates right (clockwise) → +; Laevorotatory: left (anticlockwise) → −.
  • 🪞Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images; they rotate light equally but oppositely.
  • ⚖️Racemic mixture (±) or dl: equimolar (+) and (−) → net rotation zero (externally inactive).
  • 🔁SN2: one-step backside attack → inversion of configuration (Walden inversion).
  • 🧩SN1: planar carbocation intermediate → attack from both sides → racemisation (often partial).

🔎CHIRALITY: QUICK TESTS (EXAM-USEFUL)

  • Mirror-image test: draw mirror image and attempt superimposition; if not possible → chiral.
  • Asymmetric carbon test: a tetrahedral C with four different groups usually implies chirality.
  • Symmetry check: presence of plane of symmetry often makes molecule achiral (optically inactive).
  • Note: absence of asymmetric carbon can still allow chirality in some cases; symmetry test is final.

⚙️STEREOCHEMICAL OUTCOMES IN SUBSTITUTION

  • Retention: configuration unchanged at stereocentre (rare; needs special pathway).
  • Inversion: configuration flips due to backside attack (characteristic of SN2).
  • Racemisation: formation of both enantiomers from a chiral substrate, due to planar intermediate (SN1).
  • SN1 often gives partial racemisation because ion pairs/solvent cage can hinder one face.

Why does a chiral substrate often give racemisation in SN1 substitution, and why is it sometimes not exactly 50:50?
Answer:
In SN1, the slow step forms a planar carbocation intermediate (sp2).

  • Racemic mixture (±) has zero net rotation due to cancellation of equal and opposite rotations.
  • SN2 is stereospecific: a single enantiomer gives a single inverted enantiomer.

TOPIC12

ELIMINATION & METAL REACTIONS OF HALOALKANES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • β-Elimination (dehydrohalogenation): removal of HX from a haloalkane to form an alkene, commonly using alcoholic KOH + heat.
  • In β-elimination, H is removed from a β-carbon (adjacent carbon) while X leaves from the α-carbon (carbon bearing halogen).
  • Major alkene often follows Zaitsev (Saytzeff) rule: the more substituted (more stable) alkene predominates; bulky bases can increase Hofmann product.
  • Reactions with metals convert R–X into new bonds: RMgX (Grignard reagent) and R–R (Wurtz coupling).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 1alc. KOH + Δ is the standard condition for dehydrohalogenation of haloalkanes.
  • 2α-carbon = carbon bonded to X; β-carbon = adjacent carbon supplying β-H.
  • 3E2 requires a suitable β-H; anti-periplanar geometry often governs which β-H is removed.
  • 4Zaitsev: more substituted alkene usually major; bulky base (t-BuO−) can shift toward Hofmann (less substituted) alkene.
  • 5Grignard: R–X + Mg (dry ether) → RMgX; any moisture/protic source destroys RMgX.
  • 6Wurtz: 2R–X + 2Na (dry ether) → R–R + 2NaX; best for symmetrical alkanes using same halide.

🔎β-ELIMINATION (DEHYDROHALOGENATION) + ORIENTATION (ZAITSEV / HOFMANN)

  • Overall change: haloalkane + base (alc.) + Δ → alkene + salt (KX/NaX) + H2O.
  • β-H requirement: at least one β-hydrogen must be present for dehydrohalogenation.
  • Zaitsev rule: major product is usually the more substituted (more stable) alkene.
  • Bulky base effect: bulky bases can increase the fraction of Hofmann (less substituted) alkene.
  • E2 note: stereochemical requirement is often anti-periplanar H and leaving group.

⚙️REACTIONS WITH METALS: GRIGNARD & WURTZ (FROM HALOALKANES)

  • Grignard formation: R–X + Mg → RMgX in dry ether (strictly anhydrous).
  • Moisture sensitivity: RMgX is destroyed by H2O/ROH/acids to give RH.
  • Wurtz reaction: 2R–X + 2Na (dry ether) → R–R + 2NaX.
  • Wurtz gives best results with primary alkyl halides and same halide (symmetrical alkane).
  • General leaving-group reactivity: R–I > R–Br > R–Cl >> R–F (C–F is very strong).

⚖️ELIMINATION (E1/E2) IN HALOALKANES: KEY FACTORS THAT INCREASE ELIMINATION

TOPIC13

REACTIVITY OF HALOARENES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Haloarenes (Ar–X) are aryl halides where X is bonded to an sp2 carbon of an aromatic ring.
  • They are much less reactive than haloalkanes toward nucleophilic substitution at the C–X bond.
  • Reason: the C–X bond is strengthened by resonance (partial double-bond character) and by higher s-character of sp2 carbon.
  • SN1 is disfavoured because formation of an aryl (phenyl) carbocation is extremely unstable.
  • SN2 is disfavoured because backside attack is not feasible at the aryl sp2 carbon and the C–X bond is stronger.
  • In electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS), halogens are o/p-directing but deactivating (−I decreases rate, +R controls orientation).

🖼️Diagram

Haloarenes: substitution at C–X is difficult, but the ring undergoes EAS with o/p orientation and overall deactivation.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧲Resonance: lone pair on X conjugates with ring → partial double-bond character in Ar–X.
  • 📏Stronger/shorter bond: Ar–Cl ≈ 169 pm vs CH3–Cl ≈ 178 pm.
  • 🚫SN1 ruled out: aryl (phenyl) carbocation is extremely unstable; ionisation at aryl carbon is not feasible.
  • 🧱SN2 very slow: aryl sp2 carbon + π-cloud hinders approach; strengthened C–X bond resists displacement.
  • 🔥Phenol formation needs harsh conditions: chlorobenzene → phenol with NaOH, 623 K, 300 atm (then acidification).
  • 🎯Nitro activation (SNAr): −NO2 at o/p stabilises the anionic intermediate (Meisenheimer) → faster; meta gives little activation.

🔎WHY HALOARENES RESIST NUCLEOPHILIC SUBSTITUTION (VS HALOALKANES)

  • Resonance: X lone pair donation gives Ar–X partial double-bond character → bond cleavage becomes difficult.
  • Hybridisation: aryl carbon is sp2 (more s-character) → C–X is stronger/shorter (Ar–Cl 169 pm; CH3–Cl 178 pm).
  • SN1 not feasible: formation of an aryl (phenyl) carbocation is extremely unfavourable.
  • SN2 not feasible: backside attack at the aryl sp2 carbon is not possible like sp3; π-cloud hinders approach.
  • Electronic repulsion: incoming nucleophile experiences repulsion from the electron-rich aromatic π-cloud, reducing effective attack.

⚙️KEY REACTIONS & DIRECTING EFFECTS YOU MUST REMEMBER

  • Chlorobenzene → phenol: requires harsh conditions with NaOH at 623 K, 300 atm, followed by acidification.
  • Nitro-activation (SNAr): o/p −NO2 stabilises the anionic (Meisenheimer) intermediate → substitution becomes faster.
  • Meta −NO2: cannot stabilise the intermediate by resonance at the reacting carbon → little/no activation.
  • EAS on haloarenes: halogenated benzenes undergo halogenation, nitration, sulphonation etc., but slower than benzene (deactivated ring).
  • Friedel–Crafts: haloarenes generally give FC reactions with difficulty due to deactivation; halogens may also complex with Lewis acids.

⚖️HALOALKANES vs HALOARENES (NUCLEOPHILIC SUBSTITUTION)
