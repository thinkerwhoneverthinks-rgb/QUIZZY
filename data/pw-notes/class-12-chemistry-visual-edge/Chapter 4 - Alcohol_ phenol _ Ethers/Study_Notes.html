TOPIC1

FUNCTIONAL GROUP BASICS: ALCOHOLS, PHENOLS & ETHERS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Alcohol: one or more –OH groups directly bonded to an aliphatic carbon (R–OH).
  • Phenol: –OH directly bonded to an aromatic ring carbon (Ar–OH).
  • Ether: oxygen connects two carbon groups: R–O–R′ (alkyl/aryl on both sides).
  • Functional groups often form by substitution: replace H of hydrocarbon by –OH/–OR.
  • Key exam trigger: –OH on aromatic ring carbon ⇒ phenol, not alcohol.

🖼️Diagram


🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔎Classification trigger: check where O is attached—ring carbon (phenol) vs alkyl carbon (alcohol).
  • 🔁Substitution view: hydrocarbon H replaced by –OH gives alcohol/phenol; by –OR gives ether.
  • 🧷Ether naming idea: oxygen-containing substituent is alkoxy (–OR) or aryloxy (–OAr).
  • ⚠️Benzylic/allylic alcohols are still alcohols because –OH is on sp3 carbon, not ring carbon.
  • 🧩Ethers have no O–H bond; alcohols/phenols have O–H bond (important for quick spotting).

🔎SUBSTITUTION-BASED FORMATION (TWO VIEWS)

  • Hydrocarbon view: replace H by –OH ⇒ alcohol/phenol (depending on carbon type).
  • Replace H by –OR or –OAr ⇒ ether (alkoxy/aryloxy substituted hydrocarbon).
  • Alcohol/phenol view: replace H of –OH by R/Ar ⇒ R–O–R′ ether.
  • So ether can be seen as: RO–H where H is substituted by R′.

⚙️IDENTIFICATION TRAPS (HIGH-YIELD)

  • Phenol vs benzylic alcohol: Ar–OH is phenol; Ar–CH2–OH is alcohol.
  • Alcohol vs vinylic: CH2=CH–OH has –OH on a vinylic (sp2) carbon; it is a vinylic alcohol (alcohol class), not a phenol.
  • Ether vs alcohol/phenol: absence of O–H and presence of R–O–R′ indicates ether.
  • Count carbon substituents on the C–OH carbon to decide 1°/2°/3° (allylic/benzylic are separate labels).

CLASSIFICATION OF ALCOHOLS, PHENOLS & ETHERS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Alcohol: –OH directly on an aliphatic carbon; Phenol: –OH directly on an aromatic ring carbon; Ether: R–O–R′ linkage.
  • Alcohols are classified by number of –OH (mono/di/tri/polyhydric) and by nature of C bearing –OH.
  • Monohydric alcohols: if –OH is on sp3 carbon → 1°/2°/3°; if on sp2 vinylic carbon → vinylic alcohol.
  • Allylic/benzylic alcohols: –OH on sp3 carbon adjacent to C=C / benzene ring; still can be 1°/2°/3°.
  • Phenols are classified mainly by number of –OH groups on the ring (mono/di/tri-hydric phenols).
  • Ethers are classified by groups attached to O: symmetrical (same) vs unsymmetrical (different).

🖼️Diagram

Flow: identify alcohol vs phenol vs ether, then apply the relevant classification rule.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🎯Exam trigger: –OH on aromatic ring carbon ⇒ phenol, not alcohol.
  • 🧩1°/2°/3° depends on number of carbon groups on the C–OH carbon (not on total chain length).
  • 🧷Allylic/benzylic tells position (next to C=C/Ar), not the 1°/2°/3° class.
  • 🧠Vinylic alcohol: –OH on alkene carbon (sp2 C–OH).
  • ⚖️Symmetrical ether: R = R′; unsymmetrical ether: R ≠ R′.

🔎ALCOHOLS: CLASSIFY IN 2 STEPS

  • Step 1 (–OH count): monohydric (1), dihydric (2), trihydric (3), polyhydric (>3).
  • Step 2A (monohydric, sp3 C–OH): classify as 1°, 2°, 3° using the C bearing –OH.
  • 1° alcohol: C–OH carbon attached to one other carbon (e.g., CH3CH2OH).
  • 2° alcohol: C–OH carbon attached to two carbons (e.g., (CH3)2CHOH).
  • 3° alcohol: C–OH carbon attached to three carbons (e.g., (CH3)3COH).

⚙️PHENOLS & ETHERS: QUICK CLASSIFICATION

  • Phenols: count –OH groups on benzene ring: monohydric (1), dihydric (2), trihydric (3).
  • Ether: oxygen joins two groups (alkyl/aryl): R–O–R′.
  • Symmetrical (simple) ether: both groups same, R = R′ (e.g., C2H5OC2H5).
  • Unsymmetrical (mixed) ether: groups different, R ≠ R′ (e.g., C2H5OCH3; C2H5OC6H5).

NOMENCLATURE RULES: ALCOHOLS, PHENOLS & ETHERS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Nomenclature assigns systematic (IUPAC) and common names to alcohols, phenols, and ethers.
  • Alcohol: –OH on an aliphatic carbon; named mainly with suffix -ol in IUPAC.
  • Phenol: –OH on an aromatic ring carbon; phenol is an accepted IUPAC name.
  • Ether: R–O–R′; IUPAC usually treats it as an alkoxy substituent on a parent hydrocarbon.
  • Key exam idea: for alcohols/phenols, –OH gets numbering priority over alkyl/halo substituents.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧭Alcohol IUPAC: longest chain containing C–OH; number from end nearer –OH; replace -e by -ol.
  • 🔢Polyhydric alcohols: retain “e”; use diol/triol with all –OH locants (e.g., ethane-1,2-diol).
  • 🌀Cyclic alcohols: carbon bearing –OH is position 1; locant “1” is often omitted if unambiguous; then number to give substituents lowest set.
  • ⬡Phenols: use o-/m-/p- for common names (1,2 / 1,3 / 1,4) or numeric locants in IUPAC.
  • 🔤Ethers: common name lists both groups alphabetically + “ether”; IUPAC uses alkoxy + parent.
  • ⚠️Do not mix: cresols = methylphenols (one –OH), but catechol/resorcinol/hydroquinone = benzene diols (two –OH).

🔎ALCOHOLS: COMMON & IUPAC RULES (HIGH YIELD)

  • Common name: alkyl group name + alcohol (methyl alcohol, isopropyl alcohol).
  • IUPAC (monohydric): longest chain containing C–OH; number from end nearer –OH; -e → -ol.
  • Write locant for –OH: propan-1-ol vs propan-2-ol; then name other substituents with locants.
  • Polyhydric: retain “e”; use diol/triol; give all –OH locants (propane-1,2,3-triol).
  • Cyclic: treat –OH carbon as position 1; use cyclo-; then give substituents lowest locants (e.g., 2-methylcyclopentan-1-ol).

⚙️PHENOLS & ETHERS: RING LOCANTS, o/m/p, AND ALKOXYALKANES

  • Phenol is accepted as IUPAC name for C6H5OH; substituted phenols use locants or o/m/p in common names.
  • o- = 1,2; m- = 1,3; p- = 1,4 on benzene (disubstituted) → o-cresol = 2-methylphenol, etc.
  • Benzene diols (IUPAC): benzene-1,2-diol; benzene-1,3-diol; benzene-1,4-diol.
  • Common dihydroxy names to memorise: catechol (1,2), resorcinol (1,3), hydroquinone (1,4).
  • Ethers common name: list both alkyl/aryl groups alphabetically + ether (ethyl methyl ether).

STRUCTURE AND BONDING IN ALCOHOLS, PHENOLS & ETHERS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Structure and bonding describes hybridisation at the attached carbon/oxygen, σ-bonds, and resulting bond angles/lengths in –OH and –O– groups.
  • In alcohols, O is bonded to an sp3 carbon by a σ bond; O is approximately sp3 with 2 lone pairs.
  • In phenols, O is bonded to an sp2 ring carbon; O lone pair conjugates with the aromatic π system (resonance).
  • In ethers, O has 2 σ bonds + 2 lone pairs; electron-pair geometry around O is approximately tetrahedral.
  • Bond angles deviate from 109°28′ due to lone pair repulsion and/or bulky group repulsions; bond lengths reflect bond order and hybridisation.

🖼️Diagram

Angles: alcohol ∠C–O–H is slightly < 109°28′ (lone pairs); ether ∠C–O–C is slightly > 109°28′ (bulky groups). Phenol C–O is shorter (136 pm) due to resonance + sp2 carbon.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩Alcohol: C(sp3)–O(sp3) σ overlap; O has 2 lone pairs.
  • 📐Alcohol angle: ∠C–O–H is slightly < 109°28′ due to lone-pair repulsion on O.
  • 🌀Phenol: C(sp2)–O bond shows conjugation/resonance → partial double-bond character.
  • 📏Bond lengths to remember: phenol C–O = 136 pm; ether C–O ≈ 141 pm (≈ alcohol).
  • 🔺Ether angle: ∠C–O–C is slightly > 109°28′ because two bulky R groups repel.

🔎WHY ALCOHOL ∠C–O–H IS SLIGHTLY LESS THAN TETRAHEDRAL

  • O has 4 electron pairs (2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs) → tetrahedral electron-pair geometry.
  • Lone pairs occupy more space than bond pairs → LP–LP and LP–BP repulsions compress ∠C–O–H.
  • Hence ∠C–O–H is slightly < 109°28′ (not exactly tetrahedral).
  • Hybridisation label: oxygen is commonly described as sp3 (approx.) in alcohols.

⚙️PHENOL VS ALCOHOL: WHY C–O IS SHORTER IN PHENOL

  • Phenol has O attached to sp2 carbon of the aromatic ring (higher s-character at C → shorter σ bond).
  • O lone pair overlaps with ring π system → conjugation/resonance gives partial double-bond character to C–O.
  • Result: C–O bond length in phenol = 136 pm, slightly less than in methanol.
  • Exam line to write: both reasons—resonance + sp2 carbon—must be stated.

PREPARATION OF ALCOHOLS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Preparation of alcohols means converting alkenes/carbonyls/Grignard adducts into R–OH using specific reagents.
  • From alkenes: hydration gives alcohol by adding H and OH across C=C with regiochemistry rules.
  • From carbonyls: reduction converts C=O to C–OH (aldehyde → 1°; ketone → 2°).
  • From carboxylic acids/esters: strong reduction (LiAlH4) gives 1° alcohols.
  • From Grignard reagents: carbonyl addition gives alkoxide; hydrolysis gives the alcohol.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧭Acid hydration: Markovnikov OH via carbocation (rearrangements possible).
  • 🧷Hydroboration–oxidation: anti-Markovnikov OH; no carbocation; usually no rearrangement.
  • 🧯NaBH4: reduces aldehydes/ketones; generally not acids/esters.
  • ⚡LiAlH4: strong; reduces aldehydes/ketones/acids/esters to alcohols (then H2O work-up).
  • 🔩Grignard (RMgX): adds R− to C=O; product class depends on carbonyl type.
  • 🎯HCHO + RMgX always gives 1° alcohol (after hydrolysis).

🔎1) FROM ALKENES: HYDRATION RULES

  • Acid-catalysed hydration: alkene + H2O, dilute H2SO4/H+ → alcohol.
  • Regioselectivity: Markovnikov; OH goes to more substituted carbon (via stable carbocation).
  • Mechanism order: protonation → carbocation → H2O attack → deprotonation.
  • Key risk: rearrangement (1,2-hydride/alkyl shift) if a more stable carbocation can form.
  • Hydroboration–oxidation: (i) BH3/THF (ii) H2O2, NaOH → alcohol.

⚙️2) FROM CARBONYLS & GRIGNARD: PRODUCT PREDICTION

  • Aldehyde (R–CHO) + [H] → 1° alcohol (R–CH2OH).
  • Ketone (R2C=O) + [H] → 2° alcohol (R2CHOH).
  • Reagents: H2/Pt, Pd, Ni (catalytic hydrogenation) OR NaBH4 OR LiAlH4.
  • RCOOH/esters → 1° alcohol mainly with LiAlH4, then H2O work-up.
  • Grignard: RMgX adds to C=O to give alkoxide; then H3O+ gives alcohol.

PHYSICAL PROPERTIES & H-BONDING (ALCOHOLS/PHENOLS/ETHERS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Intermolecular hydrogen bonding is attraction between a donor X–H (here O–H) and a lone-pair-bearing atom (here O) of another molecule.
  • Alcohols/phenols form strong O–H···O H-bonds with each other and with water.
  • Ethers have O lone pairs (acceptor) but no O–H; they cannot donate H-bonds.
  • Alkyl chain length ↑ ⇒ dispersion (van der Waals) ↑ ⇒ usually b.p. increases.
  • Branching ↑ ⇒ surface area ↓ ⇒ dispersion ↓ ⇒ usually b.p. decreases.
  • Water solubility depends on balance: H-bonding to water (↑) vs hydrophobic alkyl/aryl part (↓).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔗Alcohol/phenol molecules form H-bond networks ⇒ unusually high boiling points vs similar-mass alkanes/ethers.
  • 🌡️In a homologous alcohol series: chain length ↑ ⇒ b.p. ↑ (dispersion adds on top of H-bonding).
  • 🌿Isomer effect: more branching in alcohols ⇒ b.p. ↓ (weaker dispersion due to compact shape).
  • 💧Lower alcohols are often miscible with water; solubility falls sharply as alkyl size increases.
  • 🧲Intermolecular force strength (typical): H-bonding > dipole–dipole > dispersion.

🔎BOILING POINT: WHAT CHANGES IT?

  • Alcohols/phenols: strong intermolecular H-bonds + dispersion ⇒ high b.p.
  • Homologous series: more carbons ⇒ stronger dispersion ⇒ b.p. rises.
  • Branching: compact molecules have less surface contact ⇒ weaker dispersion ⇒ b.p. falls.
  • Functional group at similar molar mass (typical): alcohol/phenol (H-bond) > aldehyde/ketone (strong dipole–dipole) > ether (dipole–dipole, but no H-bond donation) > alkane (dispersion only).
  • Ethers: no O–H, so no H-bond donation; b.p. is much lower than corresponding alcohols and often nearer to hydrocarbons of similar mass.

⚙️WATER SOLUBILITY: WHY "LOWER ALCOHOLS MISCIBLE"?

  • Alcohol/phenol can H-bond with water (O accepts; O–H donates) ⇒ hydration favoured.
  • As alkyl/aryl part grows, hydrophobic effect dominates ⇒ solubility decreases.
  • Branching usually increases solubility vs straight-chain isomer (smaller hydrophobic surface).
  • Ethers can accept H-bonds from water, so some are moderately soluble; generally less than corresponding alcohols.
  • Phenols: can H-bond with water, but aromatic ring reduces solubility compared to small alcohols.

ACIDITY AND O–H CLEAVAGE (ALCOHOLS & PHENOLS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • O–H bond cleavage (in acidity context) is deprotonation of R/Ar–OH to give RO− (alkoxide) or ArO− (phenoxide).
  • Alcohols and phenols act as Brønsted acids (donate H+) to sufficiently strong bases or reactive metals.
  • Their acidity is judged by stability of the conjugate base: more stable RO−/ArO− ⇒ stronger acid.
  • Phenols are more acidic than alcohols because phenoxide is resonance-stabilised (charge delocalisation).
  • Alkyl groups (+I) increase electron density on O, destabilise RO− and decrease acidity of alcohols.
  • Lower pKa means stronger acid; phenol (10.0) < ethanol (15.9).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔪Acidic site is O–H hydrogen; cleavage gives RO−/ArO− by H+ transfer.
  • ⚡With active alkali metals (Na/K): 2ROH + 2Na → 2RONa + H2 (gas) (similarly for phenols).
  • 🧂Key distinction: phenol reacts with aq. NaOH to form sodium phenoxide; most alcohols do not.
  • 🧠Acidity ∝ conjugate-base stabilisation: resonance and electron-withdrawing groups (−I/−M) increase phenol acidity.
  • 📉Alcohol acidity order: CH3OH > C2H5OH > (CH3)2CHOH > (CH3)3COH.
  • 📌pKa set to remember (typical): ethanol 15.9; phenol 10.0; p-nitrophenol 7.1; o-nitrophenol 7.2; m-nitrophenol 8.3.

🔎METAL TEST & BASE REACTIONS (EVIDENCE OF ACIDITY)

  • With reactive alkali metals (Na/K): H2 evolution confirms acidic O–H hydrogen (though alcohols are weak acids).
  • General: 2R–OH + 2Na → 2R–ONa + H2 and 2Ar–OH + 2Na → 2Ar–ONa + H2.
  • Phenol + aq. NaOH → sodium phenoxide + H2O (acid–base reaction goes forward).
  • Most simple alcohols do not react appreciably with aq. NaOH (too weak as acids).

⚙️WHY ACIDITY CHANGES: +I, RESONANCE, SUBSTITUENTS

  • Alcohols: alkyl groups show +I effect → destabilise RO− → acidity decreases with substitution.
  • Phenols: phenoxide has resonance delocalisation of negative charge into ring → much greater stability than alkoxide.
  • Nitro (−NO2) is strongly electron-withdrawing (−I, −M): stabilises phenoxide → lower pKa.
  • Ortho/para nitro generally increase acidity more than meta because resonance interaction operates at o/p, while meta shows mainly −I.
  • Alkyl (e.g., −CH3) is electron-releasing: cresols are slightly less acidic than phenol.

ALCOHOL REACTIONS VIA C–O CLEAVAGE

📖Definition & Introduction

  • C–O cleavage reactions of alcohols occur after protonation, making H2O a good leaving group.
  • Key outcomes: substitution (R–OH → R–X) and elimination (R–OH → alkene + H2O).
  • Reactivity depends strongly on carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1° for SN1/E1 routes.
  • Lucas test uses rate of formation of insoluble alkyl chloride (turbidity) to classify 1°/2°/3° alcohols.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔗Protonation converts poor leaving group –OH into good leaving group H2O.
  • ⚡In SN1/E1, the slow step is often C–O cleavage to form carbocation (RDS).
  • 🧫Lucas reagent: conc. HCl + anhyd. ZnCl2; turbidity = immiscible alkyl chloride formation.
  • 🧰PBr3 converts R–OH to R–Br via substitution; works best for 1°/2° alcohols (SN2), minimizing rearrangement.
  • 🔥Acid-catalysed dehydration: conc. H2SO4/H3PO4 + heat; usually gives Zaitsev alkene as major.
  • 🧭Rearrangements possible when carbocations form (3°/2° under strong acid): hydride/alkyl shift may change product.

🔎 1) SUBSTITUTION: R–OH → R–X (HX, Lucas, PBr3)

  • General: R–OH + HX → R–X + H2O (after protonation; C–O bond breaks as water leaves).
  • Mechanistic trend: 3° often SN1; 1° often SN2; 2° can be SN1/SN2 depending on conditions.
  • Lucas reagent: conc. HCl + anhyd. ZnCl2; ZnCl2 complexes with oxygen and increases leaving tendency.
  • Observation basis: alcohol is more miscible; alkyl chloride formed is often immiscible → turbidity/second layer.
  • Lucas test order (RT): 3° immediate; 2° in minutes; 1° no turbidity at RT.

⚙️2) ELIMINATION: ACID-CATALYSED DEHYDRATION (R–OH → Alkene)

  • Conditions: conc. H2SO4 or H3PO4, heat; ethanol → ethene at 443 K.
  • Mechanism idea: protonation of –OH, then C–O cleavage (water leaves), then β-H removal to give alkene; acid is regenerated.
  • Ease: 3° > 2° > 1° (carbocation formation easier for more stable carbocations).
  • Regiochemistry: usually Zaitsev alkene is major (more substituted alkene favoured).
  • Driving the reaction: remove alkene/water as formed to shift equilibrium to products.

PHENOL REACTIONS (EAS & NAMED REACTIONS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Phenol (C6H5OH) mainly reacts on the ring via electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS).
  • The −OH group activates the ring by +R (lone-pair donation), increasing electron density at ortho/para positions.
  • Hence phenol undergoes EAS faster than benzene, often even without Lewis acid catalysts.
  • Several named reactions of phenol give key industrial products: salicylic acid and salicylaldehyde.
  • Phenol also shows special reactions: Zn dust reduction (deoxygenation) and oxidation to quinone.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🎯−OH = strong activator; o/p-directing by resonance; meta is not favoured in EAS.
  • 🧊Dilute HNO3, 298 K gives o- and p-nitrophenol; conditions control product distribution.
  • 💨Steam distillation separates o-nitrophenol (intramolecular H-bond → more volatile) from p-isomer.
  • ⚪With bromine water, phenol gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white ppt) without catalyst.
  • 🧷Kolbe–Schmitt: sodium phenoxide + CO2 (pressure, heat) → salicylic acid (o-hydroxybenzoic acid).
  • 🧫Reimer–Tiemann: CHCl3/NaOH generates :CCl2 → o-formylation → salicylaldehyde.

🔎ELECTROPHILIC AROMATIC SUBSTITUTION (EAS) IN PHENOL: KEY REACTIONS

  • Reason for activation: lone pair on O donates by resonance (+R), stabilising σ-complex.
  • Directive effect: o/p positions become electron-rich; hence o- and p-products dominate.
  • Nitration (mild): phenol + dil. HNO3 at 298 K → o-nitrophenol + p-nitrophenol.
  • Steam distillation: o-nitrophenol is steam volatile due to intramolecular H-bonding; p-isomer forms intermolecular H-bonds.
  • Nitration (strong): conc. HNO3/H2SO4 can give 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid).

⚙️NAMED REACTIONS + SPECIAL TRANSFORMATIONS

  • Kolbe–Schmitt: C6H5ONa + CO2 (pressure, heat) → sodium salicylate; acidification → salicylic acid (o-hydroxybenzoic acid).
  • Why ortho major (Kolbe–Schmitt): −O− strongly activates ring; carboxylation occurs mainly at o-position under these conditions.
  • Reimer–Tiemann: phenol + CHCl3/aq. NaOH (then H+) → o-hydroxybenzaldehyde (salicylaldehyde).
  • Key intermediate (Reimer–Tiemann): CHCl3 + OH− generates :CCl2 (dichlorocarbene), the electrophile.
  • Zn dust reduction: C6H5OH + Zn → C6H6 (benzene) + ZnO; rare case of C–O cleavage in phenols.

A mixture of o-nitrophenol and p-nitrophenol is obtained by nitration of phenol with dilute HNO3 at 298 K. How will you separate them, and why does the method work?
Answer:
Use steam distillation on the mixture.
o-Nitrophenol forms intramolecular H-bonding → reduced intermolecular association → becomes steam volatile.
p-Nitrophenol forms intermolecular H-bonds → higher association → not steam volatile under same conditions.
Distillate contains mainly o-nitrophenol; residue is enriched in p-nitrophenol.
✓  Separate by steam distillation: o-nitrophenol distils, p-nitrophenol remains due to stronger intermolecular H-bonding.

  • −OH is o/p-directing due to resonance donation; phenol is far more reactive than benzene in EAS.

  • Bromine water test: phenol → 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white ppt) (common board/JEE observation).
  • Steam distillation logic: intramolecular vs intermolecular H-bonding (o- vs p-nitrophenol).
  • Kolbe–Schmitt gives salicylic acid (industrial relevance; precursor to aspirin via acetylation).

ETHERS: PREPARATION AND REACTIONS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Ethers are compounds with the functional group R–O–R′ (alkyl/aryl groups linked via oxygen).
  • They are symmetrical (R = R) or unsymmetrical (R ≠ R′).
  • Key preparations: acid-catalysed dehydration of alcohols and Williamson ether synthesis.
  • Key reaction: C–O bond cleavage with excess HI/HBr to give alkyl halides (and phenol from aryl ethers).
  • Physical behavior: ethers are weakly polar, have boiling points close to (often slightly higher than) alkanes of similar molar mass but much lower than isomeric alcohols, and H-bond with water only (cannot self H-bond).

🖼️Diagram

One-page map: dehydration (413 K), Williamson (SN2), and HX cleavage rules.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔥Dehydration at 413 K: 2 ROH → ROR + H2O (best with 1° alcohols for ethoxyethane).
  • 🔁Alcohol dehydration to ethers is bimolecular (two alcohol molecules participate) and acid-catalysed.
  • 🧷Williamson: RO⁻Na⁺ + R′–X → R–O–R′ + NaX via SN2.
  • 🚫SN2 scope: CH3X, 1° X best; 2° slows; 3° gives elimination (E2) with alkoxide.
  • 💧Ethers accept H-bonds but do not donate; lower ethers are moderately soluble in water.
  • ✂️Cleavage with excess HI/HBr: protonate O then C–O breaks by SN1/SN2 depending on substrate.

🔎1) PREPARATION BY DEHYDRATION OF ALCOHOLS (413 K)

  • Overall: 2 ROH conc. H2SO4, 413 K → ROR + H2O (acid-catalysed).
  • Best for making symmetrical ethers like ethoxyethane from ethanol.
  • Uses 1° alcohols; 2°/3° tend to give alkenes more (dehydration to alkene favored at higher T).
  • Mechanistic idea: protonation makes H2O leaving group; second ROH attacks (bimolecular).
  • Limitation: mixture for unsymmetrical ether if two different alcohols are used together.

⚙️2) WILLIAMSON ETHER SYNTHESIS (SN2): SCOPE, LIMITATIONS, STRATEGY

  • Step-1: prepare sodium alkoxide (RO⁻Na⁺) from alcohol + Na/NaH (dry conditions).
  • Step-2 (key): RO⁻ attacks R′–X by backside SN2 → ether; inversion at reacting carbon.
  • Best electrophiles: CH3X and 1° alkyl halides/tosylates (fast SN2, high yield).
  • With 2° halide: competing E2 increases; with 3° halide: elimination major, ether poor.
  • Make t-butyl ethyl ether: use t-BuO⁻ + C2H5X (1°), not C2H5O⁻ + t-BuX.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
You must prepare t-butyl ethyl ether by Williamson synthesis. Which pair gives best yield: (A) C2H5ONa + (CH3)3C–Br, or (B) (CH3)3CONa + C2H5–Br? Explain.
Answer:
Williamson requires SN2 on the alkyl halide; SN2 is fastest for 1° halides and fails for 3° halides.
(A) uses tert-butyl bromide (3°); alkoxide acts as strong base → E2 elimination (isobutene major), ether poor.
(B) uses ethyl bromide (1°); tert-butoxide can do SN2 on a 1° carbon → ether forms efficiently.
✓  (B) (CH3)3CONa + C2H5Br gives best yield; (A) mainly gives alkene by elimination.
2
Predict the major products with excess HI: (i) ethoxyethane (C2H5–O–C2H5), (ii) anisole (C6H5–O–CH3). Justify the bond that breaks.
Answer:
(i) Dialkyl ether: O is protonated, then I⁻ attacks by SN2 (both sides are 1°). With excess HI, the alcohol intermediate is further converted to iodide.
So ethoxyethane → first gives C2H5I + C2H5OH; then C2H5OH + HI → C2H5I + H2O.
(ii) Anisole: cleavage occurs at alkyl–O bond, not aryl–O, because the aryl carbon cannot undergo SN2 and aryl cation is unstable.
Thus anisole + HI → phenol (C6H5OH) + CH3I.
✓  (i) With excess HI, final organic product is 2 C2H5I (and H2O forms). (ii) C6H5OH + CH3I.

⭐Important Points

  • At 413 K with conc. H2SO4, 1° alcohols preferentially give ethers (e.g., ethanol → ethoxyethane).
  • Dehydration for ethers is mainly useful for symmetrical ethers; mixed alcohols give mixtures.
  • Williamson is an SN2 reaction: choose methyl/1° electrophile for high yield.
  • Do not use 3° alkyl halides in Williamson: strong base (alkoxide) causes elimination.
  • Ethers have lower b.p. than isomeric alcohols; boiling points are close to (often slightly higher than) alkanes of similar molar mass (no self H-bonding).
