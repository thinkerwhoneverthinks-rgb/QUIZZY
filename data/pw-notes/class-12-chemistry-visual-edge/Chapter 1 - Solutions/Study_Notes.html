SOLUTIONS AND COMPONENTS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Solution: a homogeneous mixture of two or more components; properties are uniform throughout.
  • Homogeneous means same composition at every point; no visible boundaries between components.
  • Solvent: component present in the largest amount; it determines the physical state (gas/liquid/solid) of the solution.
  • Solute(s): one or more component(s) other than the solvent; may be solid/liquid/gas.
  • Binary solution: solution with exactly two components (one solvent + one solute).
  • Solution properties depend strongly on composition; tiny amounts can matter (e.g., fluoride in water).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔍State of solution is named by the solvent, not by the solute.
  • 🧊Liquid solutions: solvent is liquid; solute may be gas, liquid, or solid.
  • 🧱Solid solutions include alloys; composition changes material properties.
  • 💧Trace composition is often expressed in ppm (e.g., fluoride levels in water).
  • 🏥Intravenous injections are solutions with salt concentration matched to blood plasma.

🔎CLASSIFICATION BY PHYSICAL STATE (SOLVENT-BASED)

  • Gaseous solution: solvent is gas; solute may be gas/liquid/solid (e.g., O2 in N2, air-like homogeneous mixture).
  • Liquid solution: solvent is liquid; solute may be gas/liquid/solid (e.g., O2 in water; ethanol in water; glucose in water).
  • Solid solution: solvent is solid; solute may be gas/liquid/solid (e.g., Cu in Au; H in Pd; amalgam: Hg with Na).
  • Exam trap: classify the solution by the solvent phase, not the solute.

⚙️EVERYDAY RELEVANCE: COMPOSITION MATTERS

  • Alloys: brass (Cu+Zn), bronze (Cu+Sn), German silver (Cu+Zn+Ni) differ by composition and hence properties.
  • Fluoride in water: around 1 ppm helps prevent tooth decay; around 1.5 ppm may cause mottling.
  • At high fluoride concentration, it is poisonous (e.g., NaF has been used in rat poison).
  • IV injections: prepared with salt concentration matched to blood plasma to avoid harm.

TYPES OF SOLUTIONS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Solution: homogeneous mixture of two or more components with uniform composition throughout.
  • Solvent: component present in larger amount; it decides the physical state of the solution.
  • Solute: component(s) other than solvent; usually present in smaller amount(s).
  • Each component (solute/solvent) can be solid, liquid, or gas → different types possible.
  • Classification of types is done by the state of solvent, not by the state of solute.
  • Binary solution: only two components (commonly asked for examples).

🖼️Diagram

Classification is based on solvent: solvent state fixes the solution’s physical state.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧭Rule: Solution state = solvent state (gas solvent → gaseous solution, etc.).
  • 🧩For each solvent state (gas/liquid/solid), solute may be gas or liquid or solid.
  • ⚠️Exam trap: do not name solution as “solid solution” just because solute is solid.
  • 📌Write examples as “solute in solvent” (e.g., O2 in water).

🔎MASTER CLASSIFICATION (SOLVENT-BASED)

  • Gaseous solutions: solvent is gas; solute can be gas/liquid/solid.
  • Liquid solutions: solvent is liquid; solute can be gas/liquid/solid.
  • Solid solutions: solvent is solid; solute can be gas/liquid/solid.
  • Write type as: (state of solute) in (state of solvent), then name by solvent state.

⚙️STANDARD EXAMPLES (MUST REMEMBER)

  • Gas in gas: O2 in N2 (air-like mixture) → gaseous solution.
  • Liquid in gas: chloroform in N2 (vapour mixed with nitrogen) → gaseous solution.
  • Solid in gas: camphor in N2 (vapour in nitrogen) → gaseous solution.
  • Gas in liquid: O2 dissolved in water → liquid solution.
  • Liquid in liquid: ethanol dissolved in water → liquid solution.

TOPIC3

CONCENTRATION EXPRESSIONS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Concentration = amount of solute present in a specified amount of solution (or solvent) using a defined scale.
  • Qualitative terms: dilute, concentrated are vague; exams prefer numerical (quantitative) expressions.
  • Mass-based terms (w/w, ppm by mass, m) use masses; volume-based terms (v/v, w/V, M) use solution volume.
  • Mole fraction x uses moles; sum of all mole fractions in a mixture is 1.
  • Temperature dependence: M changes with temperature (volume changes); m, x, mass %, ppm do not.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔎Always read denominator: some use solution, some use solvent.
  • ⚖️w/w and molality use mass; use kg for molality.
  • 🧊Molarity is volume-based → changes on heating/cooling; molality stays same.
  • 🧮Mole fraction needs moles; never equate mass % with mole fraction.
  • 🚰For dilute aqueous solutions: 1 ppm ≈ 1 mg L−1 (density ≈ 1 g mL−1).

🔎PERCENT & TRACE SCALES (w/w, v/v, w/V, ppm)

  • Mass % (w/w) = mass of solute in 100 g of solution.
  • Volume % (v/v) = volume of component in 100 mL of solution (not solvent).
  • w/V % = grams of solute in 100 mL of solution (medicine/pharmacy).
  • ppm = (parts of component/total parts) × 106; specify mass/volume basis if needed.
  • For dilute aqueous solutions: 1 ppm ≈ 1 mg L−1 since 1 L water ≈ 1 kg.

⚙️MOLE-BASED SCALES (x, M, m) + TEMPERATURE TRAPS

  • Mole fraction: xi = niΣn; for binary, xA+xB=1.
  • Molarity: M = moles of solutevolume of solution (L) → changes with temperature.
  • Molality: m = moles of solutemass of solvent (kg) → temperature independent.
  • Exam trigger words: “in 1 L solution” → molarity; “in 1 kg solvent” → molality.
  • Dilute solution shortcut: for gas dissolved in water, nsolute ≪ nwater often simplifies x algebra.

SOLUBILITY AND SATURATION

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Solubility = maximum amount of solute that can dissolve in a specified amount of solvent at specified T (and pressure where relevant).
  • At the solubility limit, the solution is saturated: dissolved solute is in equilibrium with undissolved solute (for a solid solute) at fixed T, p.
  • Unsaturated solution contains less solute than its solubility at the same conditions; more can dissolve.
  • Like dissolves like: polar solutes dissolve better in polar solvents; non-polar in non-polar.
  • Saturation is not “very concentrated”; it means maximum at equilibrium at stated T, p.

🖼️Diagram

At saturation, opposing processes continue but cancel in rate: equilibrium is dynamic, not static.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧲Polarity match matters: similar intermolecular forces favour dissolution.
  • ⚖️At saturation (equilibrium): rate of dissolution = rate of crystallisation (dynamic equilibrium).
  • 🧊Crystallisation = solute particles leaving solution to form solid again.
  • 🌡️Temperature effect depends on enthalpy: many solids have solubility ↑ with T if dissolution is endothermic; for gases, solubility generally ↓ as T rises (gas dissolution is treated as exothermic).
  • 🔁Adding more solute to a saturated solution does not increase dissolved amount at the same T, p; excess remains undissolved.
  • 🫧For gases in liquids: higher pressure of the gas above the liquid increases gas solubility (qualitatively, more gas dissolves until a new equilibrium).

🔎LIKE DISSOLVES LIKE (POLARITY LOGIC)

  • Polar solvent (e.g., water) stabilises ions/polar molecules via ion–dipole, dipole–dipole, and hydrogen bonding interactions.
  • Non-polar solvent (e.g., benzene) stabilises non-polar solutes mainly via dispersion forces.
  • Examples: NaCl, sugar dissolve in water; naphthalene dissolves in benzene but not in water.
  • If solute–solvent attractions are weak compared to solute–solute, solubility is low.

⚙️SATURATION + LE CHATELIER (TEMPERATURE & PRESSURE EFFECTS)

  • Think of dissolution as an equilibrium: solute (separate phase) ⇌ solute in solution.
  • For many solids in liquids: if dissolution is endothermic (ΔHsol > 0), solubility increases with T; if exothermic (ΔHsol < 0), solubility decreases with T.
  • Pressure has negligible effect on solubility of solids in liquids (both nearly incompressible).
  • For gases in liquids: solubility increases with increase of gas pressure above the solution; solubility generally decreases as temperature increases.

FACTORS AFFECTING SOLUBILITY (SOLIDS IN LIQUIDS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Solubility of a solid in a liquid is the concentration of solute in a saturated solution at a specified temperature (pressure usually irrelevant).
  • In a saturated solution, rate of dissolution = rate of crystallisation (dynamic equilibrium).
  • Changing temperature shifts this equilibrium according to Le Chatelier’s principle.
  • If dissolution is endothermic (ΔHsol > 0), solubility generally increases with temperature.
  • If dissolution is exothermic (ΔHsol < 0), solubility generally decreases with temperature.
  • Pressure has no significant effect on solubility of solids in liquids because solids and liquids are nearly incompressible.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ⚖️Nearly saturated solution: tiny T change noticeably shifts dissolution ⇌ crystallisation equilibrium.
  • 🔥Treat heat as a reagent: endothermic dissolution needs heat; exothermic dissolution produces heat.
  • 🧠Le Chatelier: system shifts to oppose the temperature change.
  • 🧱Pressure effect is negligible: condensed phases (solid/liquid) show minimal volume change with pressure.

🔎TEMPERATURE EFFECT (LE CHATELIER LOGIC)

  • Write dissolution as: Solid + Solvent ⇌ Solute in solution (saturated state).
  • If ΔHsol > 0, heat is effectively on reactant side; T ↑ drives more dissolving.
  • If ΔHsol < 0, heat is effectively on product side; T ↑ favours crystallisation.
  • Hence solubility–temperature curves can slope upward or downward depending on ΔHsol.

⚙️WHY PRESSURE EFFECT IS NEGLIGIBLE (SOLIDS IN LIQUIDS)

  • Pressure changes matter when volume changes significantly between states.
  • Solids and liquids are highly incompressible → volume changes on pressurising are tiny.
  • Dissolving a solid into a liquid does not create a gas phase; hence pressure cannot “push” solute in.
  • So even large pressure changes cause no significant change in solubility of solids in liquids.

  • Always link temperature trend to sign of ΔHsol, not to “solid vs gas” blindly.
  • Use nearly saturated solution for Le Chatelier arguments (equilibrium present).

  • Pressure effect for solids in liquids is negligible due to incompressibility of condensed phases.

GAS SOLUBILITY & HENRY'S LAW

📖Definition & Introduction

  • At constant T, solubility of a gas in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressure above the solution (Henry’s law).
  • For gas–liquid equilibrium: gas dissolves and escapes simultaneously; at equilibrium their rates are equal.
  • If solubility is measured as mole fraction x of gas in solution: p = KHx.
  • At fixed p, x = p/KH; hence larger KH means lower solubility.
  • For most gases in water, KH increases with temperature, so gas solubility decreases on heating.
🎯Key Concepts
  • EQUIDynamic equilibrium: rate of dissolution = rate of escape (no net change in dissolved gas).
  • PRESPressure effect: increasing gas pressure increases collisions at surface → higher solubility (new equilibrium).
  • HENRHenry form (mole fraction basis): x = pKH.
  • TEMPHeating lowers gas solubility: gas dissolution is like condensation and is generally exothermic.
  • APPLSoft drinks: CO2 dissolved under high pressure; on opening, p drops and bubbles form.
  • APPLBends: at high pressure more N2 dissolves in blood; rapid ascent releases bubbles.

🔎DYNAMIC EQUILIBRIUM VIEW (WHY PRESSURE INCREASES SOLUBILITY)

  • At equilibrium: gas molecules enter solution and escape back at equal rates.
  • Increase p by compression → more molecules per unit volume above liquid.
  • More surface collisions per second → dissolution rate increases temporarily.
  • New equilibrium forms with larger dissolved gas concentration (higher x).

⚙️TEMPERATURE TREND + EXAM APPLICATIONS

  • For most gases: dissolution is exothermic; heating shifts equilibrium to gas phase → solubility decreases.
  • KH generally increases with T (e.g., N2, O2 in water) so x decreases at fixed p.
  • Soft drinks: bottle sealed at high p(CO2); opening lowers p → CO2 escapes as bubbles.
  • Bends: on rapid ascent, pressure drops fast → dissolved N2 forms bubbles blocking capillaries.
  • Anoxia: high altitude lowers p(O2) → reduced O2 in blood/tissues.

RAOULT’S LAW: IDEAL BINARY VOLATILE SOLUTIONS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Ideal binary volatile solution obeys Raoult’s law for both components at a fixed T.
  • Partial vapour pressure: pi is proportional to liquid mole fraction xi.
  • Total vapour pressure follows Dalton: ptotal = p1 + p2.
  • Vapour-phase composition: yi = pi/ptotal (so vapour can differ from liquid).
  • More volatile component has larger pure vapour pressure (pi°) and for 0<x<1 gives y > x.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📌Raoult: p1=x1p1°, p2=x2p2° (same T).
  • ➕Dalton: ptotal=p1+p2=x1p1°+x2p2°.
  • 📈ptotal vs x is a straight line between p1° and p2° for ideal solutions.
  • 🌫️Vapour composition: y1=x1p1°x1p1°+x2p2°, similarly for 2.
  • ⚡If p2° > p1°, component 2 is more volatile and for 0<x2<1, y2 > x2.

🔎WORKING MAP (x → p → y)

  • Step 1: Find liquid mole fractions: x1+x2=1.
  • Step 2: Raoult: p1=x1p1°, p2=x2p2°.
  • Step 3: Dalton: ptotal=p1+p2.
  • Step 4: Vapour composition: yi=pi/ptotal; compare y with x.

⚙️KEY CONSEQUENCES EXAMINERS LOVE

  • Linear law: ptotal=p1°+(p2°−p1°)x2.
  • If p2°>p1°, then as x2 increases, ptotal increases.
  • Vapour is enriched in more volatile component: if p2°>p1°, then for 0<x2<1, y2>x2.
  • At x2=0: ptotal=p1°; at x2=1: ptotal=p2°.

VAPOUR PRESSURE LOWERING BY NON-VOLATILE SOLUTE

📖Definition & Introduction

  • In a solution containing a non-volatile solute, the vapour pressure is due only to the solvent and is lower than that of the pure solvent at the same T.
  • Lowering occurs because the solvent’s mole fraction (and its surface fraction) decreases on adding solute.
  • For an ideal solution, solvent vapour pressure follows Raoult’s law: p1 = x1p1°.
  • Vapour-pressure lowering depends on composition (mole fraction). For many dilute ideal solutions it behaves as a colligative-type effect.
  • Typical case: solid non-volatile solute in liquid solvent (e.g., glucose/urea in water). For electrolytes, the number of solute particles differs due to dissociation; use mole fractions of species present.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ConcNon-volatile solute: its vapour pressure is negligible; total vapour pressure comes from solvent only.
  • RaouAdding solute decreases x1 of solvent ⇒ p1 decreases linearly for ideal solutions.
  • SurfSolute reduces the fraction of solvent molecules at the surface, reducing the solvent’s tendency to escape.
  • AmouFor ideal dilute solutions of non-electrolytes, same number of solute particles gives similar relative lowering at the same T.
  • EquiAt equilibrium: evaporation rate = condensation rate; equilibrium is re-established at a lower solvent vapour pressure.

🔎WHY VAPOUR PRESSURE DECREASES (SURFACE + EQUILIBRIUM VIEW)

  • In pure solvent, the surface is only solvent molecules, so evaporation is maximum at a given T.
  • After dissolving a non-volatile solute, the surface contains solute + solvent; solvent surface fraction decreases.
  • At equilibrium, solvent vapour pressure adjusts so that evaporation rate equals condensation rate at a lower p1.
  • Non-volatile solute has negligible vapour pressure and does not add to the vapour phase.

⚙️RAOULT’S LAW FOR SOLVENT (NON-VOLATILE SOLUTE CASE)

  • General Raoult: vapour pressure of each volatile component is proportional to its liquid-phase mole fraction (ideal solution).
  • Here only solvent is volatile ⇒ total vapour pressure p = p1.
  • Thus p1 = x1p1°; since x1 < 1, p1 < p1°.
  • Vapour-pressure lowering: Δp = p1° − p1 depends on composition via mole fraction.

IDEAL AND NON-IDEAL SOLUTIONS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Ideal solution: obeys Raoult’s law for each component over the entire composition range (at a given temperature).
  • For an ideal solution: ΔHmix = 0 (no heat absorbed/evolved on mixing).
  • For an ideal solution: ΔVmix = 0 (no volume change on mixing).
  • Non-ideal solution: does not obey Raoult’s law over the entire composition range.
  • Non-ideal solutions show positive or negative deviation depending on the relative strength of A–B interactions compared to A–A and B–B.
  • Deviation criterion (at same composition, same T): pactual > pRaoult (positive), pactual < pRaoult (negative).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📏Raoult’s law idea: at fixed T, each volatile component’s partial vapour pressure depends on its mole fraction in the liquid.
  • 🧲Ideality requires A–A ≈ B–B ≈ A–B so average attractions do not change on mixing.
  • 🔥Positive deviation: A–B weaker → higher escaping tendency → vapour pressure higher than Raoult prediction.
  • 🧘Negative deviation: A–B stronger → lower escaping tendency → vapour pressure lower than Raoult prediction.
  • 🧩Ethanol–acetone: acetone disrupts ethanol H-bond network → positive deviation.
  • 🔗Chloroform–acetone: specific H-bonding strengthens A–B → negative deviation.

🔎WHY IDEAL SOLUTIONS HAVE ΔHmix = 0 AND ΔVmix = 0

  • In pure liquids: attractions are A–A in A and B–B in B.
  • On mixing: new attractions A–B appear along with A–A and B–B.
  • If A–B ≈ A–A ≈ B–B, replacing contacts gives no net energy change → ΔHmix = 0.
  • Similar sizes and packing keep volume nearly additive → ΔVmix = 0.
  • Nearly ideal examples: n-hexane + n-heptane, benzene + toluene, bromoethane + chloroethane.

⚙️DEVIATIONS FROM RAOULT’S LAW: WHAT CHANGES AND WHY

  • Positive deviation: A–B weaker than A–A and B–B → molecules escape easier → p increases vs Raoult prediction.
  • Microscopic cause: disruption of strong like–like interactions (often H-bonding) on mixing.
  • Example: ethanol + acetone (acetone breaks some ethanol H-bonds) → positive deviation.
  • Negative deviation: A–B stronger than A–A and B–B → molecules held tighter → p decreases vs Raoult prediction.
  • Example: chloroform + acetone (specific H-bonding) → negative deviation.

AZEOTROPES (CONSTANT-BOILING MIXTURES)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Azeotrope: a binary liquid mixture that boils at constant temperature with same composition in liquid and vapour at that pressure (x = y).
  • At the azeotropic composition, the vapour formed is not richer in any one component than the liquid.
  • Hence fractional distillation cannot separate beyond the azeotropic composition at a given pressure.
  • Azeotropes arise from large deviations from Raoult’s law in non-ideal solutions.
  • They correspond to an extremum (minimum or maximum) in boiling point versus composition (at fixed pressure).
🎯Key Concepts
  • ♨️Azeotrope = constant-boiling mixture at one specific composition (at fixed pressure).
  • 🧩At azeotrope: xA = yA (liquid composition equals vapour composition).
  • 📉Positive deviation (A–B weaker) → minimum-boiling azeotrope can form.
  • 📈Negative deviation (A–B stronger) → maximum-boiling azeotrope can form.
  • 🧯Distillation: vapour usually enriches the more volatile component, except at azeotrope.

🔎WHY AZEOTROPES FORM (LINK TO RAOULT’S LAW)

  • Non-ideal solutions deviate because A–B interactions differ from A–A and B–B interactions.
  • Positive deviation: A–B weaker → vapour pressure higher than Raoult prediction.
  • Higher vapour pressure implies lower boiling temperature → can give a minimum in boiling point.
  • Negative deviation: A–B stronger → vapour pressure lower than ideal → boiling temperature higher.
  • At the extremum, the mixture boils at constant composition: x = y (azeotrope).

⚙️TYPES + STANDARD EXAMPLES TO MEMORISE

  • Minimum-boiling azeotrope (positive deviation): boils at lower T than either pure component.
  • Example (1 atm): ethanol–water azeotrope at about 95% (v/v) ethanol.
  • Maximum-boiling azeotrope (negative deviation): boils at higher T than either pure component.
  • Example (1 atm): nitric acid–water azeotrope at about 68% (w/w) HNO3.
  • Implication: simple/fractional distillation cannot produce ethanol above ~95% (v/v) from aqueous ethanol at 1 atm.

COLLIGATIVE PROPERTIES OVERVIEW

📖 Definition & Introduction

  • Colligative properties are solution properties that depend only on number of solute particles, not their chemical nature.
  • Applicable mainly for dilute solutions; for electrolytes, particle number changes and colligative effects are corrected using the van’t Hoff factor (i).
  • For a non-volatile, non-electrolyte solute in a volatile solvent, vapour pressure of solution is lower than pure solvent.
  • Key measurable: relative lowering of vapour pressure (RLVP) Δp/p1°, which equals solute mole fraction x2 for ideal solutions with non-volatile solute.
  • RLVP can be used to determine molar mass of an unknown solute from vapour-pressure measurements.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔢Colligative effect depends on total particles in solution (electrolytes require van’t Hoff factor correction).
  • 🌫️Raoult’s law for solvent (1): p1 = x1p1° (ideal solutions).
  • ⬇️Relative lowering (non-volatile solute): Δp/p1° = x2.
  • ⚖️Exact mole fraction: x2 = n2/(n1+n2); dilute: n2 ≪ n1 ⇒ x2 ≈ n2/n1.
  • 🧾Molar mass from RLVP (dilute): M2 ≈ (w2M1p1°)/(w1Δp).

🔎FOUR COLLIGATIVE PROPERTIES (LIST ONLY)

  • Relative lowering of vapour pressure (RLVP): depends on number of solute particles via x2.
  • Elevation of boiling point (ΔTb): solution boils at higher temperature than pure solvent.
  • Depression of freezing point (ΔTf): solution freezes at lower temperature than pure solvent.
  • Osmotic pressure (π): pressure needed to stop solvent flow through semipermeable membrane.

⚙️WHEN Δp/p1° WORKS BEST (ASSUMPTIONS)

  • Solute must be non-volatile so vapour pressure comes only from solvent.
  • Solution should be ideal and sufficiently dilute for Raoult’s law.
  • For electrolytes/association, use van’t Hoff factor correction; otherwise RLVP gives wrong particle count.
  • Dilute simplification: n2 ≪ n1 ⇒ x2 ≈ n2/n1.

⭐Important Points

  • Colligative properties depend on number of solute particles (concentration), not chemical identity.

TOPIC12

BOILING POINT ELEVATION, FREEZING POINT DEPRESSION & OSMOSIS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Colligative properties are solution properties that depend only on solute particle count, not nature.
  • Non-volatile solute lowers vapour pressure, causing boiling point elevation and freezing point depression.
  • Boiling occurs when vapour pressure equals external pressure; lowered vapour pressure needs higher T.
  • Freezing occurs when chemical potentials of solid and liquid match; solute stabilises liquid, lowering Tf.
  • Osmosis: solvent flows through a semipermeable membrane from dilute to concentrated solution.
  • Osmotic pressure Π is the pressure needed to stop osmosis; it obeys van’t Hoff equation for dilute solutions.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔥Elevation: ΔTb = Kbm (ebullioscopic constant Kb)
  • ❄️Depression: ΔTf = Kfm (cryoscopic constant Kf)
  • 🧫Molality m = moles solute per kg solvent; best for colligative properties (T-independent)
  • 🧵Kb = RTb2M11000ΔHvap, Kf = RTf2M11000ΔHfus
  • 💧van’t Hoff: Π = CRT = nRTV; for dilute solutions it is an ideal-gas-like analogy for solute particles in solution

🔎BOILING & FREEZING: WHAT CHANGES AND WHY

  • Adding non-volatile solute lowers solvent vapour pressure at same T.
  • To reach 1 atm vapour pressure, solution needs higher T ⇒ ΔTb > 0.
  • Freezing point depression means Tf(solution) decreases; define ΔTf = Tf0 − Tf(solution) > 0.
  • For dilute solutions: colligative changes are proportional to m (molality).
  • If solute dissociates/associates: use van’t Hoff factor i ⇒ ΔTb = iKbm, ΔTf = iKfm.

⚙️OSMOTIC PRESSURE: MOLAR MASS FROM Π

  • For dilute solutions: ΠV = nsoluteRT (analogy with ideal gas).
  • If w g solute of molar mass M in volume V L: n = wM.
  • Then Π = wRTMV ⇒ M = wRTΠV.
  • Use T in K; V in L; Π in atm with R = 0.0821 L atm mol−1 K−1.
