Electrochemistry: Scope and Importance

TOPIC1

SCOPE OF ELECTROCHEMISTRY

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Electrochemistry studies interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy via redox processes.
  • It explains electricity generation from spontaneous chemical reactions in electrochemical (galvanic) cells.
  • It enables non-spontaneous chemical transformations by supplying electrical energy (electrolysis).
  • It provides electrochemical methods for large-scale manufacture of important chemicals and metals.
  • It underpins devices like batteries and fuel cells, supporting energy-efficient and less-polluting technologies.
  • Many biological processes have an electrochemical basis, e.g. nerve impulse transmission and cell-to-cell communication.

🖼️Diagram

Two core directions: galvanic cells generate electricity; electrolysis uses electricity to drive chemistry.
 

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔋Spontaneous redox in a cell produces electrical work: electrons flow through an external circuit.
  • 🧪Electrolysis is the reverse: electricity forces a reaction that won’t occur on its own.
  • 🏭Industrial electrochemistry: extraction/refining of metals; manufacture of NaOH, Cl2, F2.
  • 🚗Fuel cells convert chemical energy directly to electricity, often with lower emissions than combustion.
  • 🌿Electrochemical routes can be energy-efficient and less polluting, aiding eco-friendly technologies.
  • 🧠Bioelectrochemistry: ion flows and membrane potentials enable nerve signals and cell communication.

🔎MAJOR AREAS WHERE ELECTROCHEMISTRY IS USED

  • Power sources: batteries (portable electronics) and fuel cells (clean power).
  • Industrial synthesis: chlor-alkali (NaOH, Cl2); production of highly reactive elements like F2.
  • Metallurgy: electro-extraction, electrorefining, and electroplating (controlled deposition).
  • Corrosion control: coatings and sacrificial protection to reduce metal loss.
  • Analytical methods: electrochemical measurements for monitoring composition and concentration.

⚙️WHY IT MATTERS (TECH + ENVIRONMENT + LIFE)

  • Direct redox-to-electricity conversion can avoid major heat losses of combustion-based engines.
  • Electrochemical routes can reduce pollutants by avoiding some harsh chemical oxidants/reductants.
  • Key enabler for electrification: storage (batteries) and conversion (fuel cells).
  • Biology uses ion gradients and electron-transfer steps to transmit signals and sustain metabolism.

  • Two pillars: spontaneous reactions generate electricity; non-spontaneous reactions need electricity (electrolysis).
  • Electrochemical methods are crucial for large-scale production of NaOH, Cl2, F2, and metals.
  • Batteries and fuel cells convert chemical energy to electrical energy in practical devices.

TOPIC2

DANIELL CELL BASICS (Zn–Cu GALVANIC CELL)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • A Daniell cell is a galvanic cell: Zn|Zn2+ || Cu2+|Cu that converts energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.
  • Zinc electrode in ZnSO4 acts as anode (oxidation); copper electrode in CuSO4 acts as cathode (reduction).
  • Salt bridge completes the circuit internally by ion migration and maintains electrical neutrality of both half-cells.
  • Overall reaction: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s) (electrons cancel).
  • Under standard conditions (unity activities; dilute solutions ≈ 1 mol dm−3), the Daniell cell has E°cell = 1.10 V at 298 K.

🖼️Diagram

Daniell cell set-up: Zn anode in ZnSO4, Cu cathode in CuSO4, salt bridge between solutions.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧱Cell notation: Zn(s) | Zn2+(aq) || Cu2+(aq) | Cu(s) (anode left, cathode right).
  • ⚡Electron flow in external wire: Zn → Cu; conventional current: Cu → Zn.
  • 🔁Half-reactions: anode Zn → Zn2+ + 2e⁻; cathode Cu2+ + 2e⁻ → Cu.
  • 🧂Salt bridge: anions migrate to anode side (to balance Zn2+ formation); cations to cathode side (to balance Cu2+ removal).
  • 📏Standard means activity = 1; for dilute solutions, a ≈ concentration, so 1 mol dm−3 is used in exams.
  • 🧠Do not multiply electrode potentials by stoichiometric coefficients; E is intensive.

🔎CONSTRUCTION & WORKING (WHAT HAPPENS WHERE?)

  • Anode (Zn): Zn atoms ionise → Zn2+ enters solution; electrons left on Zn make it negative.
  • Cathode (Cu): Cu2+ ions gain electrons and deposit as Cu(s) on the copper rod.
  • External circuit carries electrons; internal circuit (salt bridge) carries ions to prevent charge build-up.
  • Salt bridge electrolytes are usually inert (e.g., KNO3, KCl) to avoid side reactions.

⚙️STANDARD CELL POTENTIAL & ACTIVITY NOTE

  • Use standard reduction potentials: E°(Cu2+/Cu) = +0.34 V; E°(Zn2+/Zn) = −0.76 V.
  • By convention: E°cell = E°right − E°left = 0.34 − (−0.76) = 1.10 V.
  • Standard condition means a(Cu2+) = a(Zn2+) = 1; pure solids have a = 1.
  • In dilute aqueous solutions, a ≈ concentration, so exams often use 1 mol dm−3 as “unity”.

⭐Important Points

  • Daniell cell notation: Zn|Zn2+ || Cu2+|Cu (anode left, cathode right).
  • Overall reaction: Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) → Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s); n = 2 electrons transferred.
  • Electron flow: Zn → Cu; current is opposite direction (Cu → Zn).
  • E° values are reduction potentials; for Daniell: E°cell = 1.10 V at 298 K.
  • Standard condition is unity activity; for dilute solutions, activity ≈ molar concentration.

TOPIC3

GALVANIC VS ELECTROLYTIC (OPPOSING VOLTAGE CASE: DANIELL CELL)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Galvanic (voltaic) cell: converts energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.
  • Electrolytic cell: uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction.
  • For Daniell cell (Zn|Zn2+ || Cu2+|Cu), E° ≈ 1.10 V under standard conditions.
  • Applying an external opposing potential Eext can slow, stop, or reverse the net cell reaction.
  • Keep directions consistent: electron flow, conventional current, oxidation, reduction.

🖼️Diagram

In forward (galvanic) Daniell operation: electrons Zn→Cu; conventional current Cu→Zn; Zn oxidises and Cu2+ reduces.
 

🎯Key Concepts

  • Opposing source for Daniell: Eext < 1.10 V → forward (galvanic); Eext = 1.10 V → stop; Eext > 1.10 V → reverse (electrolytic).
  • Electron flow is from negative → positive electrode; conventional current is opposite.
  • Anode = oxidation, cathode = reduction in both types; only electrode sign (±) may change.
  • If opposing Eext equals the cell emf, net driving force is zero ⇒ I = 0 and no net reaction.

🔎THREE REGIMES WITH OPPOSING EXTERNAL VOLTAGE (Eext) FOR DANIELL (E° ≈ 1.10 V)

  • Eext < 1.10 V (opposing): spontaneous forward reaction continues (galvanic mode).
  • Electron flow: Zn → Cu; conventional current: Cu → Zn.
  • At Zn: oxidation, Zn(s) → Zn2+ + 2e− (Zn dissolves).
  • At Cu: reduction, Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu(s) (Cu deposits).
  • Eext = 1.10 V (opposing): driving force cancels ⇒ I = 0; no net reaction.

⚙️DIRECTION FIXING IN REVERSE (ELECTROLYTIC MODE)

  • Eext > 1.10 V (opposing): net reaction is forced in reverse (electrolytic mode).
  • Electron flow: Cu → Zn; conventional current: Zn → Cu.
  • At Zn: reduction, Zn2+ + 2e− → Zn(s) (Zn deposits).
  • At Cu: oxidation, Cu(s) → Cu2+ + 2e− (Cu dissolves).
  • Anode/cathode are defined by oxidation/reduction, not by the metal name.

⚖️GALVANIC (VOLTAIC) VS ELECTROLYTIC: WHAT CHANGES? (DANIELL + OPPOSING SOURCE)

⭐Important Points

  • For the Daniell cell under standard conditions, compare opposing Eext with 1.10 V to decide forward/stop/reverse.
  • Eext = 1.10 V (opposing) ⇒ I = 0; no electron flow and no net chemical change.
  • Electron flow is always opposite to conventional current in the external wire.
  • Anode/cathode are defined by oxidation/reduction; their sign (±) depends on cell mode.
  • In forced reverse for Daniell: Zn plates at Zn electrode; Cu dissolves at Cu electrode.

TOPIC4

HALF-CELLS AND ELECTRODE POTENTIAL

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Half-cell: a redox couple consisting of an electrode in contact with an electrolyte containing its oxidised/reduced forms.
  • Example half-cells: Zn(s)|Zn2+(aq) and Cu2+(aq)|Cu(s); each can undergo oxidation or reduction depending on the other half-cell paired with it.
  • At the electrode–electrolyte interface, charge separation develops due to competing deposition and ionisation tendencies.
  • Electrode potential is the potential difference between the electrode and the adjacent solution at equilibrium.
  • Standard electrode potential (E°) is the electrode potential when all species in the half-cell have unit activity (standard state): solutes at activity 1 (often approximated as 1 M in dilute solutions), gases at 1 bar, and pure solids/liquids at activity 1.
  • A single half-cell potential cannot be measured directly; only the difference between two half-cells (cell emf) is measurable.

🖼️Diagram

Two half-cells: competing ionisation vs deposition at each interface creates an electrode potential (electron-flow direction shown for a Daniell-type galvanic setup).

🎯Key Concepts

  • A galvanic cell = two half-cells connected externally (wire) and internally (typically via a salt bridge when the solutions are different).
  • Each half-cell is a redox couple: Ox + ne− ⇌ Red (tabulated as reduction potentials).
  • At equilibrium, deposition tendency = ionisation tendency ⇒ fixed interface potential forms.
  • Deposition tendency: Mn+ + ne− → M(s) (removes electrons from metal surface).
  • Ionisation tendency: M(s) → Mn+ + ne− (leaves electrons on metal).
  • More positive E° ⇒ higher tendency of Ox to gain electrons (stronger oxidising agent).

🔎ELECTRODE–ELECTROLYTE INTERFACE: WHAT CREATES THE POTENTIAL?

  • If ionisation dominates: M(s) → Mn+ + ne−; metal gains electrons ⇒ electrode becomes negative w.r.t. solution.
  • If deposition dominates: Mn+ + ne− → M(s); electrons removed from metal surface ⇒ electrode becomes positive w.r.t. solution.
  • Equilibrium forms a thin electrical double layer (charge separation) at the interface.
  • This interfacial potential is the electrode potential; its sign depends on which tendency is stronger.

⚙️STANDARD ELECTRODE POTENTIAL (E°): REFERENCE AND MEANING

  • E° is defined for a half-reaction written as reduction under unit activity (standard state).
  • Reference: Standard Hydrogen Electrode Pt|H2(1 bar)|H+(unit activity) has E° = 0.00 V by convention.
  • If a half-cell shows E° > 0, its oxidised form is reduced more readily than H+ under standard conditions (it acts as a stronger oxidising agent than H+/H2).
  • If a half-cell shows E° < 0, its reduced form is a stronger reducing agent than H2; it tends to get oxidised (and can reduce H+ to H2 under standard conditions).
  • Key data (298 K): Cu2+/Cu = +0.34 V, Zn2+/Zn = −0.76 V.

⭐Important Points

  • A half-cell is an electrode + electrolyte containing the corresponding redox couple.
  • Electrode potential arises from equilibrium charge separation at the electrode–solution interface.
  • Standard electrode potentials are tabulated as reduction potentials under unit activity (standard state).
  • SHE provides the zero reference; sign of E° indicates ease of reduction vs H+/H2 under standard conditions.
  • For metals: higher ionisation tendency generally corresponds to more negative E° (e.g., Zn).

TOPIC5

CELL EMF AND NOTATION

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Cell potential is the potential difference between two electrodes of a galvanic cell (in V).
  • EMF is the cell potential under open-circuit condition: I = 0 (no current drawn).
  • In a galvanic cell: anode = oxidation (negative electrode); cathode = reduction (positive electrode).
  • Electrons flow externally from negative → positive electrode (anode → cathode); conventional current is opposite.
  • A single half-cell potential cannot be measured alone; only potential difference between two half-cells is measurable.
  • Cell notation convention (galvanic): anode on left, cathode on right; emf computed as Eright − Eleft (using reduction potentials).

🖼️Diagram

Galvanic cell conventions: anode (left, −) → cathode (right, +); electrons flow left → right; || denotes salt bridge.
 

🎯Key Concepts

  • EMF is measured when the circuit is open: I = 0, so there is no internal drop (Ir).
  • In notation, left = anode, right = cathode (for a galvanic cell as written).
  • Use | for phase boundary (electrode|electrolyte) and || for salt bridge/liquid junction.
  • Electron flow is from negative to positive; do not confuse with conventional current.
  • Cell emf from tabulated values uses reduction potentials: right − left.

🔎CELL REPRESENTATION: RULES YOU MUST FOLLOW

  • Write anode on left (oxidation), cathode on right (reduction) for a galvanic cell.
  • Use | between different phases: metal electrode | its ionic solution (phase boundary).
  • Use || for a salt bridge/liquid junction when the two half-cells have different electrolytes.
  • Within a phase, separate species by comma: e.g. Pt | Fe3+, Fe2+ (aq).
  • Include an inert electrode (Pt/graphite) when no solid conductor participates in the redox couple.

⚙️SIGN + EMF CALCULATION IDEA (RIGHT − LEFT)

  • For the cell as written, emf is Ecell = Eright − Eleft (use reduction potentials).
  • Equivalent: Ecell = Ecathode − Eanode only when cathode is written on right and anode on left.
  • If you swap sides in notation, the sign of Ecell changes and the spontaneous direction reverses.
  • Do not multiply electrode potentials by stoichiometric coefficients (potentials are intensive).

  • In a galvanic cell: anode is negative, cathode is positive; oxidation at anode, reduction at cathode.
  • Cell notation (galvanic): left anode, right cathode; compute Ecell as right − left (reduction potentials).
  • Symbols: | = phase boundary; || = salt bridge/liquid junction (when needed).

TOPIC6

SHE AND STANDARD POTENTIALS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) is a reference half-cell assigned E° = 0.00 V by convention.
  • SHE provides a fixed comparison point because single half-cell potentials cannot be measured directly.
  • Notation: Pt(s) | H2(g, 1 bar) | H+(aq, 1 M) under standard conditions.
  • Pt electrode is coated with Pt black to provide inert, catalytic, high-area surface for H2/H+ equilibrium.
  • By coupling any half-cell to SHE and measuring cell emf (open-circuit) at 298 K under standard conditions, we assign that half-cell’s standard reduction potential E°.

🖼️Diagram

SHE coupled to an unknown half-cell; measured emf gives the unknown half-cell E° (as reduction potential) under standard conditions.

🎯Key Concepts

  • Only potential difference is measurable; absolute half-cell potential is not.
  • Standard conditions for SHE: P(H2) = 1 bar, [H+] = 1 M, pure Pt surface.
  • SHE uses inert Pt; Pt does not take part in overall redox, only conducts electrons.
  • SHE half-reaction (reduction form): H+ + e− → ½H2.
  • With SHE on left (anode) under standard conditions: E°cell = E°right − E°left; since E°left=0, measured emf (open-circuit) equals E° of the right half-cell (as reduction potential).
  • Example: E°(Cu2+/Cu) = +0.34 V vs SHE.

🔎CONSTRUCTION & STANDARD CONDITIONS (SHE)

  • Electrode: Pt foil/wire coated with platinum black (inert, catalytic, large surface area).
  • Solution: acidic aqueous solution with [H+] = 1 M (activity ≈ 1 in dilute solutions).
  • Gas: pure H2 bubbled at electrode surface at 1 bar pressure.
  • Half-reaction (reduction): H+(aq) + e− → ½H2(g).
  • Assigned value: E°(H+/H2) = 0.00 V (reference by convention).

⚙️USING SHE TO MEASURE STANDARD REDUCTION POTENTIALS

  • Build a cell with SHE on left and the test half-cell on the right; keep all species at standard conditions.
  • Measure emf (open-circuit); only differences are measurable, not single half-cell potentials.
  • Use convention: E°cell = E°right − E°left.
  • Since E°left = 0.00 V for SHE, E°cell = E° of the right half-cell (as a reduction potential).
  • If measured emf is positive, right half-cell has E° > 0 (more easily reduced than H+); if negative, right half-cell has E° < 0 (less easily reduced than H+).

⚖️INTERPRETING E° SIGN vs SHE (0.00 V)

⭐Important Points

  • SHE is a reference electrode; its potential is defined as 0.00 V (not an experimentally absolute value).
  • Write SHE as Pt(s)|H2(g, 1 bar)|H+(aq, 1 M); Pt is inert, Pt black is catalytic.
  • Half-cell potentials are tabulated as standard reduction potentials (oxidised + e− → reduced).
  • When SHE is on the left and conditions are standard, measured open-circuit emf equals E° of the right half-cell (as reduction potential).
  • E° sign vs SHE: +ve means more easily reduced than H+; −ve means less easily reduced than H+.

TOPIC7

USING E° TABLES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Standard electrode potential (E°) is the electrode potential of a half-cell written as a reduction reaction under standard-state conditions (unity activities) at a specified temperature (commonly 298 K).
  • An E° table lists half-reactions as: Oxidised form + ne− → Reduced form with E° (V) measured relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE = 0.00 V by convention).
  • More positive E° ⇒ greater tendency to be reduced ⇒ the oxidised form is a stronger oxidising agent.
  • More negative E° ⇒ the reduced form is a stronger reducing agent (more readily oxidised in the reverse direction).
  • For a cell written with anode on left and cathode on right: E°cell = E°right − E°left; spontaneity under standard conditions requires E°cell > 0.

🖼️Diagram

How to read an E° table: higher E° ⇒ stronger oxidiser (Ox form); lower E° ⇒ stronger reducer (Red form).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧾Tables give reduction potentials only; do not mix oxidation and reduction values without changing sign.
  • ⬆️Higher in table (more +E°): oxidised form has higher tendency to gain e− ⇒ stronger oxidiser.
  • ⬇️Lower in table (more −E°): reduced form is more easily oxidised ⇒ stronger reducer.
  • 🔁Reverse a half-reaction ⇒ change sign of E°; multiplying the equation does not change E°.
  • 🧭E° > 0 (vs SHE): reduction easier than H+/H2. E° < 0: reduction harder than H+/H2.
  • 📌Only a difference of two half-cell potentials is measurable; single electrode potentials are defined relative to SHE.

🔎SIGN CONVENTIONS (E° > 0 vs E° < 0)

  • E° is tabulated for reduction: Ox + ne− → Red.
  • E° > 0 (vs SHE): the Ox form is reduced more readily than H+.
  • E° < 0 (vs SHE): the Ox form is reduced less readily than H+; the Red form is a stronger reducing agent than H2.
  • For stability language used in many texts: E° > 0 ⇒ reduced form relatively more stable than H2; E° < 0 ⇒ H2 relatively more stable.

⚙️COMMON PITFALLS WHILE USING E° TABLES

  • To write oxidation, reverse the half-reaction and change sign of E°.
  • Balance electrons by multiplying equations, but never multiply E°.
  • Use only reduction potentials in: E°cell = right − left (anode left, cathode right).
  • Do not claim to measure a single half-cell potential directly; only cell emf is measurable.
  • In common aqueous tables: strongest oxidiser among listed couples is F2 (largest +E°); strongest reducer is Li(s) (most −E°).

⚖️Reading trends from an E° (standard reduction potential) table

⭐Important Points

  • E° tables are reduction tables: entries are Ox + ne− → Red (V vs SHE).
  • Stronger oxidiser ⇒ more positive E° (oxidised form). Stronger reducer ⇒ more negative E° (reduced form).
  • Reverse a half-reaction ⇒ sign of E° changes; multiplying coefficients does not change E°.
  • Cell convention: anode on left, cathode on right; use E°cell = E°right − E°left.
  • E°cell > 0 indicates a spontaneous cell reaction under standard conditions.

TOPIC8

NERNST EQUATION

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Nernst equation relates potential at any conditions to standard potential using Q: E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q.
  • Q is the reaction quotient written from the balanced redox reaction using activities of products/reactants (dilute solutions: activity ≈ molar concentration).
  • n is electrons transferred in the balanced reaction used to write Q (overall for a cell).
  • Pure solids and pure liquids have activity = 1, so they do not appear in Q.
  • At 298 K: E = E° − (0.0592/n) log Q (log base 10).
  • For a cell: Ecell = Eright − Eleft and also Ecell = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln Q.

🖼️Diagram

Nernst connects E with composition: half-cell uses ion activity; cell uses overall Q and overall n.
 

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧪For Mn+/M: Mn+ + ne− → M(s); Q = a(M(s))/a(Mn+) = 1/a(Mn+) ≈ 1/[Mn+] in dilute solutions.
  • 🔁Cell Nernst uses overall reaction Q; do not mix half-cell Q values inconsistently.
  • 🧮298 K shortcut: (RT/F)·2.303 = 0.0592 V; hence 0.0592/n.
  • 🔋Daniell cell (Zn|Zn2+||Cu2+|Cu): Ecell = E°cell − (RT/2F) ln([Zn2+]/[Cu2+]).
  • 🧷If the balanced reaction has coefficients, raise activities (or concentrations) to powers in Q (e.g., [Ag+]2).

🔎DERIVATION SKETCH (WHY THE FORM LOOKS LIKE THIS)

  • From thermodynamics: ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q.
  • Electrochemical link: ΔG = −nFE and ΔG° = −nFE°.
  • Eliminate ΔG, ΔG° ⇒ E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q.
  • At 298 K: ln Q = 2.303 log Q ⇒ factor 0.0592/n.

⚙️APPLYING TO COMMON CELLS (READY-TO-USE Q)

  • Single electrode: Mn+ + ne− → M(s); Q = 1/a(Mn+) ≈ 1/[Mn+].
  • Daniell cell: Zn(s)+Cu2+→Zn2++Cu(s); Q = [Zn2+]/[Cu2+], n=2.
  • Ni|Ni2+||Ag+|Ag: Ni(s)+2Ag+→Ni2++2Ag(s); Q=[Ni2+]/[Ag+]2, n=2.
  • Always balance electrons first, then write Q with correct powers.

E°CELL, Kc AND ΔG

📖Definition & Introduction

  • For a cell reaction, ΔrG = −nFEcell; at equilibrium Ecell = 0.
  • Standard link: ΔrG° = −nFE°cell and also ΔrG° = −RT ln K (here K is the activity-based equilibrium constant).
  • Combining gives E°cell = (RT/nF) ln K = (2.303RT/nF) log K.
  • At 298 K: E°cell = (0.0592/n) log K (log base 10).
  • In dilute aqueous solutions, exams often approximate K by Kc (using molar concentrations).
  • Ecell is intensive; ΔrG is extensive (scales with reaction amount).

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧭At equilibrium: Ecell = 0 but usually E°cell ≠ 0.
  • 🔁Nernst: Ecell = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln Q; at equilibrium Q = K.
  • 🧮Never multiply E° values by stoichiometric coefficients; only n changes.
  • 📌For Daniell cell Zn|Zn²⁺||Cu²⁺|Cu, n = 2 and E°cell = 1.10 V.
  • ⚖️Units: 1 V = 1 J C−1; ΔG (J mol−1) = −nFE.

🔎MINI-DERIVATION (EXAM-READY)

  • Start with Nernst: Ecell = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln Q.
  • At equilibrium, Ecell = 0 and Q = K.
  • So 0 = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln K ⇒ E°cell = (RT/nF) ln K.
  • Convert to log: E°cell = (2.303RT/nF) log K.
  • At 298 K: 2.303RT/F = 0.0592 V ⇒ E°cell = (0.0592/n) log K.

⚙️SIGN + SCALING (INTENSIVE VS EXTENSIVE)

  • If Ecell > 0 (galvanic), then ΔrG < 0: reaction is spontaneous.
  • If Ecell = 0, then ΔrG = 0: equilibrium (no net reaction).
  • If Ecell < 0, then Δr>G > 0: non-spontaneous (needs external voltage).
  • Multiply balanced equation by m: n becomes mn, so ΔG scales by m, but E does not.

TOPIC10

RESISTANCE AND CONDUCTIVITY BASICS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Resistance R is opposition offered by a conductor to flow of current; unit Ω.
  • Resistivity ρ is a material property in R=ρlA; unit Ω m (or Ω cm).
  • Conductance G is inverse of resistance: G=1R; unit S (= Ω−1).
  • Conductivity κ is inverse of resistivity: κ=1ρ; unit S m−1 (or S cm−1).
  • Dimensional dependence (same material, same temperature): R ∝ l/A (longer wire → more R, thicker wire → less R).
  • Classification by conductivity: conductors (high κ), insulators (low κ), semiconductors (intermediate). Superconductors have ρ ≈ 0 (idealised) below a critical temperature.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📏R depends on dimensions (l, A); ρ depends on material and temperature.
  • 🔁Inverse pairs: G ↔ R, and κ ↔ ρ.
  • 🧪A uniform conducting column (wire/solution) of length l and area A follows R=ρlA.
  • 🔌Ideal superconductor: ρ=0 ⇒ R=0 and κ→∞ (below critical temperature).

🔎 DERIVATION: R = ρl/A AND LINKS TO κ, G

  • For same material and temperature: experimentally R ∝ l.
  • For same material and temperature: experimentally R ∝ 1A.
  • Combine: R ∝ l/A ⇒ R = ρlA, where ρ depends on material and temperature.
  • Define conductivity: κ = 1ρ ⇒ R = lκA.
  • Conductance: G = 1R ⇒ G = κAl.

⚙️UNITS + MATERIAL CLASSIFICATION (EXAM-FAVOURITE)

  • Resistivity: Ω m; also Ω cm. Conversion: 1 Ω m = 100 Ω cm; 1 Ω cm = 0.01 Ω m.
  • Conductivity: S m−1; also S cm−1. Conversion: 1 S cm−1 = 100 S m−1.
  • Conductor: very high κ (metals). Insulator: extremely low κ (glass, rubber).
  • Semiconductor: intermediate κ (Si, Ge); strongly temperature-dependent.
  • Superconductor (idealised): below critical temperature, ρ ≈ 0 ⇒ R ≈ 0 for a specimen.

TOPIC11

CONDUCTANCE IN SOLUTIONS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Electrolytic (ionic) conductance is current flow in solution due to migration of ions under an electric field.
  • Metallic (electronic) conductance is current flow in metals due to drift of electrons; conductor composition unchanged.
  • Resistance R opposes current; conductance G = 1/R (unit S = Ω−1).
  • Resistivity ρ is a material property in Ω m; conductivity κ = 1/ρ in S m−1.
  • Very pure water conducts slightly due to self-ionisation: [H+] ≈ [OH−] ≈ 10−7 M (κ ≈ 5.5×10−6 S m−1 at 298 K).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔩Geometry effect: R ∝ lA; hence G ∝ Al.
  • 🌡️Temperature trend: metals → G decreases with T; ionic solutions → κ increases with T.
  • 🧪Ionic conductivity depends on: ion concentration, charge, size/solvation, solvent viscosity, temperature.
  • 🔁AC source is used to avoid electrode polarisation and electrolysis that occur with prolonged DC through solutions.
  • 📏Cell constant Kcell = lA (depends only on cell geometry); find it using standard KCl.

🔎FACTORS AFFECTING IONIC CONDUCTIVITY (κ)

  • More ions in solution (higher concentration) generally increases κ for strong electrolytes.
  • Higher ionic charge and smaller hydrated radius increase mobility → higher κ.
  • Stronger solvation increases effective size → lowers ionic mobility and κ.
  • Higher solvent viscosity lowers ionic mobility → lowers κ.
  • Temperature increases mobility and ionisation (weak electrolytes) → κ increases.

⚙️MEASURING κ: WHY AC + CONDUCTIVITY CELL + BRIDGE

  • DC through solution causes electrolysis and electrode polarisation; measured R drifts with time.
  • AC reverses direction rapidly → prevents net electrolysis; gives stable resistance reading.
  • Conductivity cell uses Pt electrodes coated with Pt black to reduce polarisation and increase effective area.
  • After finding cell constant Kcell, compute κ from measured R using κ = Kcell/R.

TOPIC12

MOLAR CONDUCTIVITY AND DILUTION

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Molar conductivity Λm = conductivity contributed by ions produced by 1 mol electrolyte in solution.
  • Relation: Λm = κc, with c in mol m−3 (SI).
  • Units: κ in S m−1 ⇒ Λm in S m2 mol−1.
  • On dilution: κ decreases (fewer ions per unit volume), but Λm increases (higher ionic mobility; and higher dissociation for weak electrolytes).
  • Limiting molar conductivity Λm°: value of Λm as c → 0 (infinite dilution).

🎯Key Concepts

  • TRENDilution trend (dilute solutions): κ ↓ with dilution; Λm ↑ and approaches Λm°.
  • STROStrong electrolytes: inter-ionic interactions reduce mobility; dilution reduces these effects, so Λm rises slightly.
  • LAWDebye–Hückel–Onsager form (strong, dilute): Λm = Λm° − A√c.
  • KOHLKohlrausch law: at infinite dilution, ions migrate independently; Λm° is additive of ionic contributions.
  • WEAKWeak electrolytes: degree of dissociation α = ΛmΛm°; combine with Ostwald dilution law for K.

🔎UNITS + CONVERSION (EXAM-SAFE)

  • If c is in mol m−3, then Λm = κc gives SI unit S m2 mol−1.
  • If concentration is given in mol L−1 (M), convert: 1 M = 1000 mol m−3.
  • Unit bridge: 1 S m2 mol−1 = 104 S cm2 mol−1.
  • Common shortcut (when κ is in S cm−1 and c in mol L−1): Λm(S cm2 mol−1) = 1000 × κ(S cm−1)c(mol L−1).

⚙️LIMITING MOLAR CONDUCTIVITY, √c LAW & WEAK ELECTROLYTES

  • Limiting value: Λm° = limc→0 Λm; strong electrolytes approach it smoothly in dilute solutions.
  • Strong electrolytes (dilute): Λm = Λm° − A√c; plot Λm vs √c is ~linear; intercept = Λm°.
  • Kohlrausch law: Λm°(AxBy) = xλ°A + yλ°B (independent ionic migration).
  • Weak electrolytes: Λm rises sharply on dilution due to increased dissociation; use α = ΛmΛm°.
  • Ostwald dilution law: K = cα21 − α; for very weak, 1 − α ≈ 1 ⇒ α ≈ √(Kc).

TOPIC13

ELECTROLYSIS AND FARADAY LAWS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Electrolysis is the use of electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction in an electrolytic cell.
  • In an electrolytic cell: cathode is negative (reduction) and anode is positive (oxidation).
  • Electrons are supplied to the cathode by the power source; electrons are removed from the anode by the power source.
  • Example (Cu/CuSO4): Cu2+ is reduced at cathode and Cu is oxidised at anode, maintaining [Cu2+].
  • Faraday’s laws relate the mass deposited/liberated at electrodes to the charge passed through the electrolyte.

🖼️Diagram

Cu/CuSO4 electrolytic cell: cathode (−) plates Cu, anode (+) dissolves Cu.
 

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔋Electrolytic cell needs external DC source; reaction proceeds opposite to spontaneous direction.
  • ➖Cathode (−): cations migrate and get reduced; anode (+): anions/metal get oxidised.
  • 🧪Cu/CuSO4 with Cu electrodes: cathode plating and anode dissolution keep Cu2+ nearly constant.
  • 🏭Industrial: electrorefining Cu; electrowinning reactive metals (Na, Mg, Al) from molten salts/oxide melts.
  • 📏Equivalent weight E = Mn; links molar mass to electrons exchanged.

🔎 ELECTRODE REACTIONS ( Cu/ CuSO4, Cu ELECTRODES)

  • Cathode (reduction): Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu(s) (copper deposits).
  • Anode (oxidation): Cu(s) → Cu2+ + 2e− (copper dissolves).
  • Overall: Cu is transferred from anode to cathode; electrolyte composition changes little ideally.
  • Migration: Cu2+ moves toward cathode; SO42− toward anode (to maintain electroneutrality).

⚙️INDUSTRIAL APPLICATIONS (WHY ELECTROLYSIS MATTERS)

  • Electrorefining of Cu: impure Cu anode, pure Cu cathode; Cu dissolves then plates as high purity.
  • Less reactive metals (e.g., Cu, Ag) can be electrorefined from aqueous electrolytes using suitable ions.
  • Electrowinning of very reactive metals needs molten (fused) salts, not aqueous solutions.
  • Na, Mg: electrolysis of fused chlorides (no water to be reduced instead).
  • Al: electrolysis of Al2O3 dissolved in cryolite (Na3AlF6) to lower melting point and improve conductivity.
