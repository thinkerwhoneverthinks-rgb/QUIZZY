TOPIC1

AMINES BASICS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Amines are derivatives of ammonia (NH3) in which one or more H atoms are replaced by alkyl (R–) and/or aryl (Ar–) groups.
  • In amines, nitrogen is trivalent and carries one lone pair; the geometry is typically trigonal pyramidal due to the lone pair.
  • Classification depends on the number of carbon groups attached to nitrogen: 1°, 2°, 3°; further alkylation can give quaternary ammonium salts (R4N+X−).
  • Amines are widespread in nature: found in proteins (via amino acids), and present in many vitamins, alkaloids and hormones.
  • They are important industrially as intermediates for drugs, dyestuffs and polymers; primary aromatic amines can form diazonium salts, key dye intermediates.

🎯Key Concepts

  • coreCore idea: start from NH3, replace H by R/Ar to get 1°, 2°, 3° amines; further alkylation gives R4N+.
  • lpLone pair on N makes amines nucleophilic and basic; they form ammonium salts with acids.
  • trapExam trap: 1°/2°/3° refers to number of carbon groups attached to nitrogen, not the degree of any carbon atom.
  • bioBiology link: many biomolecules contain amino/amine functionality (amino acids, alkaloids, some hormones).
  • appQuaternary ammonium salts (R4N+) are common surfactants.
  • dyePrimary aromatic amines form diazonium salts (Ar–N2+X−), key dye intermediates.

🔎REAL-LIFE OCCURRENCE & IMPORTANCE (EXAM RELEVANCE)

  • Proteins contain amino groups (via amino acids); amine functionality is common in biomolecules.
  • Vitamins and alkaloids often contain nitrogen and show basic (amine-like) behaviour.
  • Important hormones/bioregulators may contain amine functionality (e.g., adrenaline).
  • Industrial use: key intermediates for medicines, fibres, polymers and dyestuffs.

⚙️MUST-KNOW EXAMPLES TO QUOTE IN ANSWERS

  • Adrenaline and ephedrine: biologically active; contain a secondary amine (–NH–) group.
  • Novocain: synthetic amino compound; local anaesthetic used in dentistry.
  • Benadryl (diphenhydramine): antihistaminic; contains a tertiary amino group.
  • Quaternary ammonium salts: widely used as surfactants (R4N+X−).
  • Diazonium salts: formed from primary aromatic amines; crucial intermediates for many dyes.

1
Classify each as 1°, 2°, 3° amine or quaternary ammonium: (i) C6H5NH2 (ii) (CH3)2NH (iii) (C2H5)3N (iv) (CH3)4N+Cl−
Answer:
Step 1: Count the number of carbon groups (alkyl/aryl) directly attached to N.
(i) C6H5NH2: one aryl group on N ⇒ 1° arylamine (aniline/benzenamine).
(ii) (CH3)2NH: two alkyl groups + one N–H ⇒ 2° amine.
(iii) (C2H5)3N: three alkyl groups, no N–H ⇒ 3° amine.
(iv) (CH3)4N+Cl−: four alkyl groups; N is positively charged ⇒ quaternary ammonium salt.
✓  (i) 1° arylamine (ii) 2° amine (iii) 3° amine (iv) quaternary ammonium salt
2
Why is aniline (C6H5NH2) a weaker base than aliphatic amines in general?
Answer:
Basicity depends on the availability of the N lone pair to accept H+.
In aniline, the lone pair on N is delocalised into the benzene ring by resonance.
Delocalisation reduces electron density on N and lowers its tendency to protonate.
Therefore, aromatic amines like aniline are generally less basic than aliphatic amines (where alkyl +I effect increases electron density on N).
✓  Aniline is weaker because its N lone pair is resonance-delocalised into the ring, making it less available for protonation.

⭐Important Points

  • Definition: amines are NH3 derivatives with H replaced by alkyl/aryl groups.
  • Classification basis: number of carbon groups on N (1°, 2°, 3°); R4N+X− are quaternary ammonium salts.
  • Structure: N is trivalent with one lone pair; geometry typically pyramidal.
  • Occurrence: commonly cited in proteins, vitamins, alkaloids and hormones.
  • Applications: surfactants (quaternary ammonium salts) and dyestuffs via diazonium salts from primary aromatic amines.

STRUCTURE OF AMINES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Amines are ammonia derivatives where N is trivalent and has one lone pair.
  • Nitrogen in typical amines is sp3-hybridised, like NH3.
  • Molecular shape is trigonal pyramidal (not planar) due to the lone pair.
  • Three sp3 orbitals form σ-bonds by overlap with H(1s) or C(sp3) orbitals.
  • The fourth sp3 orbital contains the unshared electron pair (lone pair).
  • Bond angle at N (C–N–E, E = C or H) is < 109.5° because lone pair–bond pair repulsion compresses angles.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧠Lone pair occupies more space than a bond pair ⇒ compresses ∠ at N below 109.5°.
  • 🧩4 electron pairs around N (3 bond pairs + 1 lone pair) ⇒ tetrahedral electron geometry.
  • 📐Observed example: trimethylamine, ∠C–N–C ≈ 108° (pyramidal).
  • 🔗σ-bonds: N(sp3)–H(1s) in RNH2; N(sp3)–C in alkyl/aryl amines.
  • 🧭Trigonal pyramidal: N at apex, three substituents form a base triangle.

🔎BONDING & HYBRIDISATION (WHY sp3?)

  • N forms 3 σ-bonds (to H/C) and retains 1 lone pair ⇒ 4 electron domains.
  • Four domains arrange tetrahedrally ⇒ N uses four sp3 hybrid orbitals.
  • Bond formation: N(sp3) overlaps with H(1s) or C(sp3) to give σ-bonds.
  • The fourth sp3 orbital remains non-bonding (contains lone pair).

⚙️GEOMETRY & BOND ANGLE (LONE PAIR EFFECT)

  • Electron-pair geometry: tetrahedral; molecular geometry: trigonal pyramidal.
  • Repulsion order: LP–LP > LP–BP > BP–BP ⇒ lone pair pushes bond pairs closer.
  • Hence ∠ at N is less than 109.5° (ideal tetrahedral).
  • Typical value: trimethylamine has ∠C–N–C ≈ 108°.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Why are amines (like methylamine) not planar at nitrogen, even though there are three substituents?
Answer:
Nitrogen has three σ-bonds plus one lone pair ⇒ 4 electron domains.
Four electron domains arrange approximately tetrahedrally using sp3 hybrid orbitals.
With one position occupied by a lone pair, the visible atoms form a trigonal pyramidal arrangement (not trigonal planar).
✓  Amines are trigonal pyramidal because N is sp3-hybridised with one lone pair.
2
Explain why the bond angle C–N–C in trimethylamine is about 108° and not 109.5°.
Answer:
109.5° is the ideal tetrahedral angle when all four electron pairs are bond pairs.
In amines, one of the four sp3 orbitals contains a lone pair.
A lone pair repels bond pairs more strongly (LP–BP > BP–BP), squeezing the three σ-bonds closer.
Therefore the observed ∠C–N–C becomes slightly smaller, about 108° in (CH3)3N.
✓  Lone pair–bond pair repulsion compresses the tetrahedral angle, giving ≈108° in trimethylamine.

⭐Important Points

  • In amines, N is trivalent and always has one lone pair (neutral amines).
  • Hybridisation at N in typical amines: sp3 (three bonding + one nonbonding orbital).
  • Shape is trigonal pyramidal; do not call it trigonal planar.
  • Bond angle at N is < 109.5°; example to quote: trimethylamine ≈ 108°.
  • σ-bonds form by overlap of N(sp3) with H(1s) or C orbital (usually C sp3 in alkyl).

💡Memory Technique

““3 + 1 ⇒ PYRAMID””
Count around N: 3 bonds + 1 lone pair.Four domains ⇒ sp3 and tetrahedral electron arrangement.One corner is hidden (lone pair) ⇒ visible atoms make a pyramid and angle < 109.5°.

CLASSIFICATION OF AMINES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Amines are derivatives of NH3 in which 1, 2 or 3 H are replaced by alkyl/aryl groups.
  • Classification as primary/secondary/tertiary depends on the number of carbon groups attached to nitrogen.
  • Primary (1°): RNH2 or ArNH2 (one carbon group on N).
  • Secondary (2°): R2NH or R–NH–R′ (two carbon groups on N).
  • Tertiary (3°): R3N (three carbon groups on N; no N–H bond).
  • Also classified as simple (all groups same) or mixed (different groups on N).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔢Degree = number of C-containing groups directly bonded to N (not the carbon degree).
  • 🧷1° has two N–H, 2° has one N–H, 3° has zero N–H.
  • 🧩Simple: identical groups on N; Mixed: at least two different groups.
  • ⚠️Exam trap: “primary/secondary/tertiary” refers to N substitution, not the carbon skeleton.
  • 🏷️R = alkyl; Ar = aryl (phenyl etc.). Aryl amines: –NH2 directly on benzene (aniline).

🔎HOW TO CLASSIFY ANY AMINE (EXAM METHOD)

  • Step 1: Locate the nitrogen and list atoms directly attached to it (ignore atoms further away).
  • Step 2: Count carbon groups attached to N: 1 → 1°, 2 → 2°, 3 → 3°.
  • Step 3: Check N–H bonds: 1° has 2 N–H, 2° has 1 N–H, 3° has 0 N–H.
  • Step 4: Decide simple vs mixed: identical substituents = simple; otherwise mixed.
  • Aryl case: ArNH2 (aniline type) is still 1° because only one carbon group bonds to N.

⚙️COMMON EXAM TRAPS (JEE/NEET)

  • Trap: Calling (CH3)3N “tertiary” because carbon is 3°—wrong; degree is at N only.
  • Trap: Confusing mixed with “mixture”; mixed means different groups on same N.
  • Trap: R–NH–R′ is 2° even if one group is aryl; count attachments, not type.
  • Trap: Cyclic amines are classified by attachments to N; ring size doesn’t change degree.
  • Trap: Quaternary ammonium salts (R4N+) are not tertiary amines; they are separate.

1
Classify: (i) C6H5NH2 (ii) C6H5NHCH3 (iii) C6H5N(CH3)2 as 1°/2°/3° and simple/mixed.
Answer:

2
Exam trap check: Is tert-butylamine, (CH3)3C–NH2, a tertiary amine? Justify.
Answer:
Degree of amine depends on how many carbon groups are attached to N, not on whether the carbon attached is 1°/2°/3°.
In (CH3)3C–NH2, N is attached to only one carbon group (tert-butyl) and has two H.
Therefore it is a primary (1°) amine even though the attached carbon is tertiary.
✓  No. tert-Butylamine is a 1° amine; “tertiary” refers to substitution at N, not the carbon.

⭐Important Points

  • 1°/2°/3° amines: classify by number of carbon substituents on N (1, 2, 3).
  • General representations: RNH2, R2NH, R3N; aryl analogs also follow same degree rule.
  • Simple vs mixed is independent of degree; both can exist for 2° and 3°.
  • Check N–H count quickly: 1° has 2, 2° has 1, 3° has 0; helps in reaction-type questions later.
  • Do not confuse tertiary amine with quaternary ammonium salt (R4N+).

NOMENCLATURE OF AMINES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Amines are named in common and IUPAC systems based on groups attached to nitrogen.
  • Aliphatic amines: use alkylamine (common) or alkanamine (IUPAC).
  • Polyamines: locate each –NH2 on the carbon chain and use di-, tri- etc.
  • Secondary/tertiary IUPAC names use N- (or N,N-) to mark substituents on nitrogen.
  • Arylamines: aniline is common name; IUPAC: benzenamine (also accepted: aniline).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩Primary/secondary/tertiary amine depends on number of carbon groups on N, not carbon substitution.
  • 🏷️Common names: list alkyl groups + amine; identical groups use di-/tri-.
  • 📌IUPAC (amines as principal group): pick the parent chain/ring and use suffix -amine with proper locants.
  • 🔤Use N- locant only for substituents directly bonded to nitrogen (not carbon numbering).
  • 🧷Aryl amines: ring is parent; substituents numbered on ring (o/p common; 2/4 IUPAC).

🔎COMMON (TRIVIAL) NAMING: ALIPHATIC AMINES

  • Primary: alkyl + amine as one word: CH3NH2 = methylamine.
  • If alkyl is straight-chain, prefixes like n-, iso- may appear (common usage).
  • Secondary/tertiary: list alkyl groups; identical groups use di-, tri-.
  • Example: (CH3)2NH = dimethylamine; (CH3)3N = trimethylamine.

⚙️IUPAC RULES: ALKANAMINES, POLYAMINES & N-LOCANTS

  • Primary aliphatic amines (alkanamines): take parent alkane name and replace the final -e by -amine: CH3NH2 = methanamine.
  • Locate –NH2 by numbering the parent chain: CH3CH2CH2NH2 = propan-1-amine; CH3CH(NH2)CH3 = propan-2-amine.
  • Polyamines: give positions + di-/tri-; retain e in parent hydrocarbon: H2NCH2CH2NH2 = ethane-1,2-diamine.
  • Secondary/tertiary: choose a parent chain giving the base name as an alkanamine; groups on nitrogen are written as N- substituents.
  • Example: CH3NHCH2CH3 = N-methylethanamine; (CH3)3N = N,N-dimethylmethanamine.

  • For polyamines, use positions + diamine/triamine and retain e in parent hydrocarbon (e.g., ethane-1,2-diamine).
  • Use N- only for groups directly attached to nitrogen; do not use carbon locants for N-substituents.
  • Propan-1-amine vs propan-2-amine is a frequent exam ask (n-propylamine vs isopropylamine).
  • Aniline is a common name and also an accepted IUPAC name; systematic name is benzenamine.

PREPARATION OF AMINES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Preparation of amines = converting nitro compounds, halides, nitriles or amides into 1°/2°/3° amines using suitable reagents.
  • Most common routes: reduction (nitro/nitrile/amide) and ammonolysis (alkyl/benzyl halides).
  • Selectivity matters: ammonolysis often gives mixtures due to further N-alkylation.
  • Nitrile reduction converts –C≡N into –CH2NH2 (primary amine); this is used for ascent of amine series (one-carbon-longer amine from a suitable one-carbon-shorter precursor).
  • Free amines are often obtained by basification of their ammonium salts (liberation from salts).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔻Nitro → amine via H2/Ni, Pd, Pt or metal + acid; Fe/HCl commonly preferred.
  • 🔁Ammonolysis: R–X + NH3 gives 1° amine first, but further alkylation → 2°, 3°, R4N+.
  • 🧷Conditions for ammonolysis: ethanolic NH3, sealed tube, about 373 K.
  • ⚡Halide reactivity in substitution: RI > RBr > RCl.
  • ➕Nitrile reduction (LiAlH4 or H2/cat.) yields only 1° amine: R–CH2NH2.
  • 🧱Amide reduction with LiAlH4: RCONH2 → RCH2NH2 (carbonyl O removed).

🔎1) REDUCTION ROUTES (HIGH-YIELD, OFTEN CLEANER)

  • Nitro compounds → amines: catalytic hydrogenation H2/Ni, Pd, Pt or metals in acidic medium.
  • Fe/HCl preferred: FeCl2 formed gets hydrolysed, regenerating HCl; only small initial HCl needed.
  • Nitriles → 1° amines: R–C≡N + (LiAlH4 or H2/cat.) → R–CH2NH2.
  • Key structural change: the nitrile carbon becomes the –CH2– group attached to –NH2 (basis of ascent of amine series).
  • Amides → amines: RCONH2 (or substituted) + LiAlH4 → RCH2NH2 (or substituted amine).

⚙️2) AMMONOLYSIS OF ALKYL/BENZYL HALIDES (MIXTURE ISSUE)

  • Core reaction: R–X + NH3 (ethanolic, sealed tube, ~373 K) → R–NH2 (as salt) + HX.
  • Further alkylation: R–NH2 is nucleophilic; reacts again with R–X → R2NH → R3N → R4N+X−.
  • Disadvantage: mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° amines and quaternary ammonium salt forms.
  • To favour 1° amine: take large excess NH3 so R–X meets NH3 more often than the amine.
  • Free amine isolation: treat ammonium salt with strong base to liberate the amine.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Why does ammonolysis of an alkyl halide with NH3 usually give a mixture of amines? How can the major product be made 1° amine?
Answer:
In ammonolysis, the first formed 1° amine (RNH2) has a lone pair and remains a good nucleophile.
So it can attack remaining R–X to form 2° amine; this can continue to 3° amine and finally R4N+X− (successive N-alkylation).
To favour 1° amine, use large excess NH3, so collisions of R–X with NH3 are more probable than with RNH2/higher amines.
✓  Mixture occurs due to successive N-alkylation; excess NH3 suppresses further alkylation and makes 1° amine major.
2
Choose the best preparation route and justify: (a) Convert benzonitrile (C6H5–C≡N) to benzylamine. (b) Convert nitrobenzene to aniline using a preferred reducing system.
Answer:
(a) Use reduction of nitrile: C6H5–C≡N → C6H5–CH2NH2 with LiAlH4 (or H2/cat.); it gives a primary amine because the nitrile group reduces to –CH2NH2.
(b) Use Fe/HCl to reduce nitrobenzene: practical and economical; HCl is regenerated because FeCl2 formed gets hydrolysed, so small initial HCl is sufficient.
✓  (a) LiAlH4 reduction of benzonitrile gives benzylamine. (b) Reduce nitrobenzene with Fe/HCl to obtain aniline (commonly preferred).

⭐Important Points

  • Catalytic hydrogenation of nitro compounds uses finely divided Ni/Pd/Pt with H2.
  • Fe/HCl is preferred in many nitro reductions because FeCl2 hydrolysis regenerates HCl (only a small initial amount is needed).
  • Ammonolysis conditions: ethanolic NH3, sealed tube, about 373 K.
  • Ammonolysis gives mixture; use excess NH3 to suppress multiple N-alkylation.
  • Reactivity of halides in substitution: RI > RBr > RCl.

GABRIEL & HOFMANN METHODS (AMINES)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Gabriel phthalimide synthesis prepares primary aliphatic amines (RNH2) using phthalimide as N-source.
  • Key feature: selective 1° amine formation (avoids over-alkylation common in ammonolysis).
  • Limitation: does not give aryl amines from aryl halides (aryl halides resist required substitution).
  • Hofmann bromamide degradation converts an amide (RCONH2) to a 1° amine (RNH2) using Br2/NaOH.
  • Key rule: product amine has one carbon less than the parent amide (carbonyl carbon is lost).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🎯Gabriel: phthalimide → K-phthalimide (nucleophile) → N-alkyl phthalimide → hydrolysis → RNH2
  • 🔒Selectivity reason: N is protected in phthalimide; only one alkyl group is introduced.
  • ⛔Gabriel fails for aryl halides: chlorobenzene/bromobenzene generally do not undergo this SN substitution.
  • 🔁Hofmann: rearrangement (migration) from carbonyl carbon to N under Br2/NaOH.
  • ➖Hofmann carbon-loss rule: amide with n carbons → amine with (n−1) carbons.

🔎GABRIEL PHTHALIMIDE SYNTHESIS (WORKING + LIMITATION)

  • Step 1 (nucleophile): phthalimide + ethanolic KOH → potassium phthalimide (phthalimide anion).
  • Step 2 (alkylation): phthalimide anion + R–X (alkyl halide, heat) → N-alkyl phthalimide (SN2).
  • Step 3 (hydrolysis): alkaline hydrolysis of N-alkyl phthalimide → RNH2 (primary amine) + phthalate salt.
  • Best for: primary aliphatic amines from primary alkyl halides (clean 1° product).
  • Not for: aryl halides (e.g., chlorobenzene) → do not undergo required substitution, so aniline cannot be made by Gabriel from aryl halide.

⚙️HOFMANN BROMAMIDE DEGRADATION (REARRANGEMENT + CARBON-LOSS RULE)

  • Reagents: Br2 + NaOH (aqueous/ethanolic) on amide RCONH2.
  • Key event: migration (R group shifts from carbonyl carbon to nitrogen) → rearrangement.
  • Overall outcome: RCONH2 → RNH2 (primary amine).
  • One-carbon-less rule: carbonyl carbon is removed; amide chain length decreases by 1.
  • Exam use: to find starting amide, add one carbon to the amine product skeleton and attach CONH2.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Why does Gabriel phthalimide synthesis give mainly primary aliphatic amines, unlike ammonolysis of alkyl halides which gives mixtures?
Answer:
In Gabriel, N is present in phthalimide; after deprotonation it forms a single nucleophilic anion that undergoes one N-alkylation to give N-alkyl phthalimide.
The nitrogen in N-alkyl phthalimide is part of an imide system and does not behave like a free amine for repeated alkylations under the same conditions.
After hydrolysis, the product released is RNH2; hence over-alkylation to 2°/3° is avoided.
✓  Gabriel uses a protected nitrogen source (phthalimide), so only one alkyl group is introduced before hydrolysis releases RNH2 selectively.
2
An amine CH3CH2NH2 is obtained by Hofmann bromamide reaction. Identify the starting amide.
Answer:
Product is ethanamine with 2 carbons in the chain.
Hofmann rule: amide has one extra carbon (the carbonyl carbon).
So starting amide must have 3 carbons: CH3CH2CONH2.
✓  Starting amide: propanamide (CH3CH2CONH2).

⭐Important Points

  • Gabriel is the go-to method for pure 1° aliphatic amines from alkyl halides.
  • Write the sequence clearly: phthalimide → K-phthalimide → N-alkyl phthalimide → hydrolysis → RNH2.
  • State limitation explicitly: aryl halides do not undergo required substitution; do not propose Gabriel for aniline from chlorobenzene.
  • Hofmann uses Br2/NaOH on amides and involves rearrangement (migration).
  • Hofmann carbon-loss: amide with n carbons (including C=O carbon) gives amine with n−1 carbons.

PHYSICAL PROPERTIES OF AMINES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Physical properties of amines depend mainly on intermolecular forces: polarity and hydrogen bonding.
  • Lower aliphatic amines are gases with fishy odour; higher members become liquids then solids.
  • Lower amines are water-soluble due to H-bonding with water; solubility decreases with molar mass.
  • 1° and 2° amines show intermolecular association (self H-bonding); 3° amines do not (no N–H).
  • At similar molar mass: alcohols have higher b.p. than amines; alkanes have the lowest b.p.

  • 👃Fishy smell is characteristic of many lower aliphatic amines (volatile bases).
  • 🧊State trend: small RNH2 gases → C3+ 1° amines mostly liquids → higher solids.
  • 🎨Aniline is usually colourless but becomes coloured on storage due to atmospheric oxidation.
  • 💧Water solubility: amines H-bond with water (N lone pair acceptor; 1°/2° also N–H donor).
  • 📉Solubility decreases as hydrophobic alkyl part grows with molar mass; higher amines ≈ insoluble.
  • 🧷Self H-bonding: 1° (2 N–H) > 2° (1 N–H) > 3° (0 N–H).

🔎STATE, ODOUR & STORAGE BEHAVIOUR

  • Lower aliphatic amines are gases with fishy odour (high volatility).
  • Primary amines with ≥ 3 carbons are generally liquids; still higher members are solids.
  • Aryl amines (e.g., aniline) are usually colourless but turn coloured on storage (air oxidation).

⚙️SOLUBILITY & H-BONDING LOGIC (EXAM USE)

  • Amines are soluble in organic solvents like alcohol, ether, benzene due to similar intermolecular forces.
  • In water: N lone pair accepts H-bond from H2O; 1°/2° can also donate via N–H.
  • As molar mass increases, nonpolar alkyl part dominates ⇒ water solubility decreases sharply.
  • Alcohols form stronger H-bonds than amines because O (3.5) is more electronegative than N (3.0).

TOPIC8

BASICITY OF AMINES: Kb & pKb

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Amines are bases because N has a lone pair that can accept H+ (Lewis base).
  • In water: amine + H2O ⇌ conjugate acid (ammonium ion) + OH−.
  • With mineral acids, amines form ammonium salts (generally water-soluble).
  • Ammonium salts regenerate the free amine on treatment with strong base (e.g., NaOH).
  • Kb measures base strength; pKb = −log Kb; lower pKb ⇒ stronger base.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧠Lewis basicity: N lone pair donation to H+ gives substituted ammonium ion.
  • 🔁Salt formation is reversible: acid makes salt; base liberates the amine again.
  • 📉Kb ↑ ⇒ pKb ↓ ⇒ basic strength ↑ (compare bases using pKb carefully).
  • ⚖️Aliphatic amines are generally stronger than NH3 due to +I effect of alkyl groups.
  • 🌿Aniline is much weaker (pKb 9.38) because the lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring (resonance).

🔎SALT FORMATION & REGENERATION (EXAM CORE)

  • Salt formation: RNH2 + HCl → RNH3+Cl− (ammonium salt).
  • Regeneration: RNH3+Cl− + NaOH → RNH2 + NaCl + H2O.
  • Ammonium salts: generally water-soluble and often insoluble in ether (useful in separations).
  • Reason: ionic salts interact strongly with water via ion–dipole hydration.

⚙️BASICITY TRENDS + KEY pKb DATA (AQUEOUS)

  • Aliphatic amines: +I effect increases electron density on N → generally stronger than NH3 (pKb 4.75).
  • Aqueous basicity depends on +I effect, solvation of RnNH4−n+, and steric hindrance.
  • Aromatic amines: lone pair delocalised by resonance → less available for protonation → weak bases.
  • Must-know: aniline pKb = 9.38 (very weak compared to aliphatic amines).
  • Typical aqueous orders: (CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > (CH3)3N > NH3 ; and (C2H5)2NH > (C2H5)3N > C2H5NH2 > NH3.

1

Solution:
Use pKb = −log(Kb).
So log(Kb) = −9.38 ⇒ Kb = 10−9.38.
10−9.38 = 10−9 × 10−0.38.
Using 10−0.38 ≈ 0.416 (rounded), Kb ≈ 0.416 × 10−9 = 4.16 × 10−10.
✓  Kb(aniline) ≈ 4.16 × 10−10 (approx.).
2

Kb(base) = 10−pKb.
Ratio = 10−3.38 / 10−4.75 = 10(−3.38 + 4.75) = 101.37.
101.37 = 10 × 100.37; 100.37 ≈ 2.34 (rounded).
So ratio ≈ 10 × 2.34 = 23.4.
✓  Methanamine is ≈ 23.4 times more basic than NH3 in water (by Kb ratio).

⭐Important Points

  • State both: proton acceptance and OH− formation in water.
  • Use pKb correctly: smaller pKb ⇒ stronger base.
  • Amines + acids form ammonium salts; salts + NaOH regenerate amines (asked in separation logic).
  • Aliphatic amines: generally stronger than NH3 due to +I effect; aqueous pKb often ~3 to 4.22 (many common examples).
  • Aniline is weak mainly due to resonance delocalisation; pKb 9.38.

STRUCTURE–BASICITY EFFECTS (AMINES)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Basicity of an amine is its tendency to accept H+ (form ammonium ion) using N lone pair.
  • Stronger base ⇔ more available lone pair and/or more stable conjugate acid (RnNH4−n+).
  • In gas phase: mainly intrinsic electron donation and +I stabilisation of the cation.
  • In aqueous phase: hydration/solvation and steric hindrance strongly modify the order.
  • Aromatic amines (aniline) are weak bases because lone pair is resonance-delocalised into the ring.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • ➕+I effect: alkyl groups push electron density to N → increases basicity (esp. gas phase).
  • 💧Solvation: better hydration of RNH3+/R2NH2+ stabilises conjugate acid → increases basicity in water.
  • 🚧Steric hindrance: bulky groups shield ammonium ion and reduce hydration → lowers aqueous basicity.
  • 🌀Resonance in aniline: lone pair participates in ring conjugation → less available for protonation.
  • ⚖️In water, compare using Kb/pKb: larger Kb (smaller pKb) ⇒ stronger base.

🔎WHAT CONTROLS BASICITY?

  • Intrinsic availability of lone pair: higher electron density on N → stronger base.
  • Conjugate-acid stability: more stable RnNH4−n+ → stronger corresponding base.
  • Hydration stabilises ammonium ions via H-bonding with water; poor hydration weakens base in water.
  • Steric crowding around N reduces approach of H+ and hydration of the cation.
  • Aryl resonance delocalises the lone pair into the ring, decreasing basicity sharply.

⚙️ALKYLAMINES: GAS PHASE vs AQUEOUS PHASE

  • Gas phase order (standard): R3N > R2NH > RNH2 > NH3 (maximum +I dispersion).
  • Aqueous reality: basicity depends on +I and hydration of the conjugate acid; bulky groups reduce hydration.
  • Methyl-substituted (water): (CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > (CH3)3N > NH3.
  • Ethyl-substituted (water): (C2H5)2NH > (C2H5)3N > C2H5NH2 > NH3.
  • Key message: in water, tertiary amines can drop due to poorer solvation and steric shielding of the ammonium ion.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Arrange in decreasing basic strength in water: (CH3)3N, (CH3)2NH, CH3NH2, NH3. Explain.
Answer:
In water, basicity depends on +I effect and on hydration of the conjugate acid.
Tertiary ammonium ions are less effectively hydrated (bulkier; fewer/less accessible N–H sites) than 1°/2° ammonium ions.
(CH3)2NH has strong +I yet still forms a well-hydrated conjugate acid (CH3)2NH2+.
Therefore methylamines in water follow: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH3.
✓  (CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > (CH3)3N > NH3
2

Answer:
Ethylamine: alkyl group shows +I effect → increases electron density on N → protonation is favoured.
Aniline: N lone pair is delocalised into benzene ring by resonance → less available to bind H+.
On protonation, the N lone pair is no longer available for resonance with the ring; hence resonance stabilisation present in aniline is lost in anilinium ion, making aniline relatively less eager to accept H+.
On aniline, −NO2 is strong −R/−I → decreases basicity; −OCH3 is +R (dominant) → increases basicity.
✓  Ethylamine is stronger due to +I; aniline is weaker due to resonance delocalisation. −NO2: basicity ↓, −OCH3: basicity ↑.

⭐Important Points

  • Always compare aqueous basicity using pKb: lower pKb ⇒ stronger base.
  • Gas-phase order for alkylamines: 3° > 2° > 1° > NH3 (no hydration).
  • In water, hydration of the conjugate acid can override +I, especially against bulky 3° bases.
  • Methyl vs ethyl series show different aqueous orders; quote the correct one for the given groups.
  • Aniline is weak mainly due to lone-pair resonance; not merely because “phenyl is −I”.

AMINES: KEY REACTIONS AND TESTS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Key reactions/tests of amines use nucleophilicity/basicity of N-lone pair for conversion and identification.
  • Alkylation gives higher substituted amines; excess NH3 favours 1° amine formation in ammonolysis of alkyl halides.
  • Acylation/benzoylation converts 1°/2° amines to amides; a base (pyridine/NaOH) neutralises HCl and drives the reaction.
  • With carboxylic acids at room temperature, amines form ammonium carboxylate salts (not amides).
  • Carbylamine test is specific for 1° amines, giving foul-smelling isocyanides.
  • Nitrous acid and Hinsberg test differentiate 1°/2°/3° amines (diazotisation at 273–278 K for aryl 1°).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧷Amines act as nucleophiles (attack electrophiles) and bases (form salts with acids).
  • 🔁Alkylation tends to over-alkylation: 1° → 2° → 3° → quaternary ammonium salt.
  • 🧊Aryl 1° amines + HNO2 at 273–278 K give diazonium salts (stable only in cold).
  • 🧫Hinsberg: alkali solubility depends on presence/absence of N–H in sulphonamide.
  • 👃Carbylamine test: isocyanide smell = primary amine present.

🔎CORE CONVERSIONS (WRITE THESE EQUATIONS)

  • Alkylation (with alkyl halide): RNH2 + R′X → RNH(R′) + HX (HX usually forms amine salt in situ); further alkylation can give R4N+X−.
  • To favour 1° amine in ammonolysis: use large excess NH3; reactivity RI > RBr > RCl.
  • Acylation (acid chloride/anhydride/ester): 1° or 2° amine → amide; 3° amines do not give amides (no N–H), though they can form salts with acids.
  • Acylation/benzoylation is done with a base (pyridine or aqueous NaOH) to neutralise HCl and push the reaction forward.
  • Benzoylation (Schotten–Baumann): RNH2/R2NH + C6H5COCl (with NaOH/pyridine) → benzoyl amide; used for derivatisation/identification of 1° and 2° amines.

⚙️IDENTIFICATION TESTS (HIGH-YIELD DIFFERENTIATION)

  • Carbylamine (isocyanide) test: 1° amine + CHCl3 + alcoholic KOH (heat) → RNC (foul smell). Commonly written: RNH2 + CHCl3 + 3KOH → RNC + 3KCl + 3H2O.
  • Negative carbylamine test suggests 2°/3° amine (or non-amine).
  • Nitrous acid preparation: NaNO2 + HCl → HNO2 (in situ).
  • 1° aliphatic amine + HNO2 → unstable alkyldiazonium → alcohol + N2↑ (gas evolution).
  • 1° aryl amine + HNO2 at 273–278 K → aryl diazonium salt (prepared cold; used immediately).

TOPIC11

ANILINE SUBSTITUTION CHEMISTRY (EAS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Aniline undergoes very fast electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) because –NH2 donates lone pair by resonance, making ring strongly activating and o/p directing.
  • Over-activation often causes polysubstitution (multiple EAS) instead of clean monosubstitution.
  • In acidic medium, aniline is protonated to anilinium ion (–NH3+), which is deactivating and meta directing.
  • Acetylation (aniline → acetanilide) is a protection strategy: reduces activation yet keeps o/p direction, enabling controlled monosubstitution.
  • Aniline fails Friedel–Crafts due to strong complex/salt formation with AlCl3, which deactivates the ring.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧠–NH2 is o/p directing via +R effect (resonance donation into ring).
  • ⚡Strong activation ⇒ electrophile attacks repeatedly ⇒ 2,4,6 patterns are common.
  • 🧯Protection: aniline → acetanilide (–NHCOCH3) lowers ring activation.
  • 🧂Acidic medium: aniline → anilinium (–NH3+) ⇒ meta directing.
  • 🚫Friedel–Crafts fails: N donates to AlCl3 ⇒ salt/complex; ring becomes deactivated.

🔎BROMINATION: WHY 2,4,6-TRIBROMOANILINE FORMS

  • With bromine water (rt), aniline gives 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (white ppt).
  • Reason: –NH2 strongly activates; after first Br, ring still highly activated → rapid further Br at both ortho and para.
  • Reaction proceeds without Lewis acid (no FeBr3 needed) due to high ring electron density.

⚙️NITRATION & SULPHONATION: ACID EFFECT + PROTECTION STRATEGY

  • Direct nitration of aniline (HNO3/H2SO4) gives mixture; also tarry oxidation products may form.
  • In strong acid, aniline → anilinium ion (–NH3+): ring deactivated; meta becomes significant.
  • For controlled nitration: acetylate (acetic anhydride) → nitration → mainly p-nitroacetanilide → hydrolysis → p-nitroaniline.
  • Sulphonation: aniline + conc H2SO4 → anilinium hydrogensulphate; heating 453–473 K gives sulphanilic acid (p-aminobenzenesulphonic acid) major.

This gives N a positive character (or forms anilinium-like species), which deactivates the aromatic ring toward EAS.

DIAZONIUM SALTS CHEMISTRY

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Diazonium salts have general formula Ar–N2+ X− (diazonium group = –N2+).
  • Naming: arene + diazonium + anion; e.g. C6H5N2+Cl− = benzenediazonium chloride.
  • Prepared from primary aromatic amines by diazotisation using HNO2 generated in situ (NaNO2 + HCl).
  • Arenediazonium salts are stable only in cold aqueous solution at 273–278 K; warm → hydrolysis/decomposition.
  • Key use: substitution (loss of N2) to make Ar–Cl/Br/CN/I/F/OH etc, and azo coupling (formation of –N=N–) for dyes.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧊Cold condition is mandatory: diazotisation and storage only at 273–278 K.
  • 💨Driving force: N2(g) leaves easily → substitution reactions proceed readily.
  • 🔁Two reaction families: displacement of N2 vs azo coupling (no N2 loss in the coupling step).
  • 🧱Aromatic diazonium ions have resonance stabilisation; aliphatic diazonium salts are highly unstable.
  • 🎨Azo coupling with phenol/aniline gives azo dyes (–N=N– link).

🔎DIAZOTISATION (PREPARATION) + STABILITY

  • Make HNO2 in situ: NaNO2 + HCl → HNO2 + NaCl.
  • Overall: ArNH2 + NaNO2 + 2HCl → ArN2+Cl− + NaCl + 2H2O.
  • Temperature control: keep at 273–278 K to prevent decomposition/hydrolysis.
  • Arenediazonium ions: resonance-stabilised → usable in cold solution; alkyldiazonium salts decompose immediately.
  • Handling: many diazonium salts decompose when dry; prepare and use freshly in solution.

⚙️KEY REACTIONS OF ARENEDIAZONIUM SALTS

  • Sandmeyer: ArN2+X− + CuCl/CuBr/CuCN → ArCl/ArBr/ArCN + N2 (copper salt changes accordingly, e.g. CuX formed).
  • Gatterman: ArN2+X− + HCl/HBr in presence of Cu powder → ArCl/ArBr + N2 (X− ends up in inorganic salts).
  • KI gives iodo: ArN2+X− + KI → ArI + KX + N2.
  • Schiemann (fluoro): make ArN2+BF4− using HBF4; on heating → ArF + BF3 + N2.
  • Azo coupling: ArN2+ couples with phenol (alkaline) or aniline (mildly acidic) → Ar–N=N–Ar′ (coloured azo dye).

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Why are arenediazonium salts prepared and kept at 273–278 K, and used immediately?
Answer:
Arenediazonium ions are only briefly stable in cold aqueous solution; warming increases decomposition/hydrolysis.
N2 is an excellent leaving group; loss of N2 becomes rapid on heating, so the salt cannot be stored safely.
Hence reactions are done in an ice bath (273–278 K) and the diazonium solution is used freshly.
✓  Cold stabilises the arenediazonium ion; warming causes fast decomposition (loss of N2), so prepare cold and use immediately.
2
Convert aniline into (i) chlorobenzene, (ii) benzonitrile, (iii) iodobenzene, (iv) fluorobenzene, and (v) an azo dye with phenol.
Answer:
Step 1 (common): aniline → benzenediazonium chloride by NaNO2/HCl, 273–278 K.
(i) Sandmeyer: diazonium salt + CuCl → chlorobenzene + N2.
(ii) Sandmeyer: diazonium salt + CuCN → benzonitrile + N2.
(iii) KI: diazonium salt + KI → iodobenzene + N2 (KX formed).
(iv) Schiemann: form benzenediazonium BF4− using HBF4; heat → fluorobenzene + N2.
(v) Azo coupling: diazonium salt + phenol in alkaline medium → p-hydroxyazobenzene (major).
✓  All conversions proceed via benzenediazonium salt: CuCl/CuCN (Sandmeyer), KI for iodo, HBF4 then heat (Schiemann) for fluoro, and coupling with phenol (alkali) for azo dye.

⭐Important Points

  • Only primary aromatic amines give useful diazonium salts; secondary/tertiary amines do not form them similarly.
  • Maintain 273–278 K during diazotisation; temperature rise reduces yield and increases side reactions.
  • Sandmeyer uses Cu(I) salts (CuCl/CuBr/CuCN); Gatterman uses Cu powder + HCl/HBr.
  • KI is sufficient for iodo substitution; no copper catalyst usually needed.
  • Schiemann is the key route to aryl fluorides: stable ArN2+BF4− then thermal decomposition.
