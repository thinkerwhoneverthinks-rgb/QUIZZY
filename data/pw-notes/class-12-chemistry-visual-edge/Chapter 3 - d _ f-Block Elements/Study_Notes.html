The d- and f-Block Elements

TOPIC1
Where they lie in the periodic table and what is being filled

OVERVIEW AND PLACEMENT (d- AND f-BLOCK)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • d-block elements are Groups 3–12 elements where (n−1)d orbitals are progressively filled.
  • f-block elements are elements where 4f (period 6) and 5f (period 7) orbitals are progressively filled.
  • d-block forms the middle block between s-block (left) and p-block (right) in the periodic table.
  • f-block is displayed as a separate panel below, but it still belongs to periods 6 and 7.
  • Common names: d-block is commonly associated with transition metals (but Group 12 is not transition by IUPAC); f-block corresponds to inner transition metals.

🖼️Diagram

d-block lies between s and p; f-block belongs to periods 6–7 but is shown separately.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📍d-block placement: Groups 3–12, central block between s and p.
  • 🧠d-filling: electrons enter (n−1)d across each long period (3d, 4d, 5d, 6d).
  • 🧱f-filling: electrons enter 4f (lanthanoids) and 5f (actinoids).
  • ↔️Reason for separate f-panel: avoids an excessively wide table; it is a display convenience.
  • 🧪Exam-critical term: transition series (d-block) vs inner transition series (f-block).

🔎TRANSITION vs INNER TRANSITION SERIES (NAMES + MEMBERS)

  • Transition series (d-block): four rows formed by 3d, 4d, 5d, 6d filling.
  • Main d-series ranges: 3d Sc→Zn; 4d Y→Cd; 5d La; then Hf→Hg; 6d Ac; then Rf→Cn.
  • Inner transition series (f-block): two rows formed by 4f and 5f filling.
  • f-series ranges: 4f Ce→Lu (lanthanoids); 5f Th→Lr (actinoids).

⚙️WHY IS THE f-BLOCK SHOWN SEPARATELY?

  • If 4f/5f elements were inserted inline, the table would become too wide for practical display.
  • The separate panel is only a layout choice; these elements are still in period 6 and period 7.
  • Remember: f-block is not an “extra period”; it is a part of the main periods.

⚖️d-BLOCK vs f-BLOCK (PLACEMENT & FILLING)

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Explain why the f-block is shown as a separate panel at the bottom although it belongs to periods 6 and 7.
Answer:
f-block corresponds to progressive filling of 4f (period 6) and 5f (period 7) subshells.
If these 14 + 14 elements were placed in the main body between groups 2 and 3, the periodic table would become excessively wide.
Hence, for convenience of display, they are written separately, but they are not removed from periods 6 and 7.
✓  The f-block is separated only to keep the periodic table compact; it still belongs to periods 6 and 7.
2
A student says: "All Group 12 elements are transition metals because they are in the d-block." Is this statement correct? Justify using the exam definition.
Answer:
Being in Groups 3–12 places an element in the d-block, but transition metal status is decided by the IUPAC definition.
IUPAC: a transition metal has an incomplete d subshell in its atom or in one of its ions.
Zn, Cd and Hg have d10 configuration in the ground state and in common oxidation states, so the d subshell is complete.
✓  Incorrect. Zn, Cd, Hg are d-block elements but are not transition metals by IUPAC due to d10 (complete d subshell).

⭐Important Points

  • d-block = Groups 3–12; electrons enter (n−1)d across long periods.
  • Four d-series: 3d, 4d, 5d, 6d (each corresponds to a long period).
  • f-block = progressive filling of 4f (lanthanoids) and 5f (actinoids).
  • f-block elements are in periods 6 and 7 even when shown separately.
  • Separate f-panel is a formatting convenience, not a change in periodic classification.

S–D–P: s-block left, d-block centre, p-block right. F on the floor: f-block drawn on the bottom to save width (still P6 & P7). Remember: Groups 3–12 = d-block; 4f/5f = f-block.
TOPIC2
Transition metal ≠ just d-block: check incomplete d in atom or ion

TRANSITION METAL DEFINITION (IUPAC) & GROUP 12 EXCEPTION

📖Definition & Introduction

  • IUPAC: A transition metal is a metal with an incomplete d subshell in its neutral atom or in one of its ions.
  • The term “transition” originated because these metals show properties intermediate between s-block and p-block elements.
  • Being in Groups 3–12 (d-block) is a positional idea; “transition metal” is a configuration-based label (IUPAC).
  • If all common ions of an element have d10, it fails the IUPAC condition (no incomplete d subshell).
  • Zn, Cd, Hg are often studied with transition series as end members of 3d/4d/5d rows, but are not transition metals by IUPAC.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📜Keyword test: incomplete d in atom OR ion.
  • 🔍Group 12 exam trap: d-block elements can be non-transition (Zn, Cd, Hg).
  • 🧩Typical transition metals form at least one ion with d1–d9.
  • 🏁Row end members: Sc starts d-filling; Zn ends with d10 and breaks IUPAC condition.

🔎WHY GROUP 12 (Zn, Cd, Hg) ARE EXCLUDED

  • IUPAC demands incomplete d in atom or ion; Group 12 fails both in common chemistry.
  • Zn: [Ar] 3d104s2; Zn2+: 3d10 (still full).
  • Cd: [Kr] 4d105s2; Cd2+: 4d10 (still full).
  • Hg: [Xe] 4f145d106s2; Hg2+: 5d10 (still full).
  • So, despite being d-block, they are not transition metals (no d-vacancies).

⚙️ORIGIN OF THE TERM “TRANSITION” + END-MEMBER IDEA

  • Historically: properties appeared “transitional” between highly electropositive s-block and p-block behaviour.
  • In each d-series, d-filling starts at Group 3 (e.g., Sc: d1) and ends at Group 12 (e.g., Zn: d10).
  • Zn/Cd/Hg are end members of 3d/4d/5d series; taught with d-block trends but excluded by IUPAC definition.
  • Exam phrasing: “All transition metals are d-block, but not all d-block are transition metals.”

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Using IUPAC definition, classify Sc (Z=21) and Zn (Z=30) as transition or not.
Answer:
Step 1: Write ground-state outer configuration.
Sc: 3d14s2; Zn: 3d104s2.
Step 2: Apply IUPAC: incomplete d in atom or any ion.
Sc atom has d1 → incomplete → qualifies.
Step 3: Check Zn common ion.
Zn2+ is 3d10 → still complete, so no ion with incomplete d.
✓  Sc is a transition metal; Zn is not a transition metal (though it is a d-block element).
2
A student says: “Zn, Cd, Hg are transition metals because they are in the d-block.” Identify the mistake and correct it using IUPAC wording.
Answer:
Mistake: Confusing d-block position with transition metal definition.
Correction: State IUPAC: transition metal must have an incomplete d subshell in neutral atom or one ion.
Apply: Zn/Cd/Hg have d10 in ground state and remain d10 in common oxidation state +2, so they fail the condition.
✓  Group 12 (Zn, Cd, Hg) are d-block but not transition metals by IUPAC due to d10 in atom and common ions.

⭐Important Points

  • Write the IUPAC definition verbatim: incomplete d in atom or ion.
  • Group 12 is the classic trap: d-block does not guarantee transition.
  • Use electron configurations to justify: d1–d9 qualifies; persistent d10 does not.
  • Zn/Cd/Hg are “end members” of their series; studied with d-block trends but excluded by IUPAC.
  • For borderline questions, always check a likely ion (often +2, +3) for incomplete d.

💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: “12 is Full””
Group 12 ends each d-row and has d10 (full d). Full d in atom and common ions ⇒ not transition (Zn, Cd, Hg). Remember the ends: Sc starts d-filling, Zn ends with d-full.
TOPIC3
General pattern (n−1)d1–10 ns1–2 and classic exceptions: Cr, Cu, Pd

ELECTRONIC CONFIGURATION PATTERNS (d-BLOCK)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • d-block outer configuration usually follows (n−1)d1–10 ns1–2 as d electrons build across a series.
  • (n−1)d belongs to the penultimate shell; ns is the outermost s orbital for that period.
  • Exceptions arise because ΔE between ns and (n−1)d is very small, so electron shift costs little energy.
  • Extra stability of half-filled d5 and fully filled d10 subshells can outweigh the normal filling order.
  • Exam-safe justification for exceptions: state both small energy gap and d5/d10 stability.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩General outer pattern: (n−1)d1–10 ns1–2 (not always ns2).
  • ⚖️Key reason: ns and (n−1)d energies are close → easy electron promotion/shift.
  • 🧲Stability: exchange energy makes d5 unusually stable (maximum parallel spins).
  • ✅Fully filled stability: d10 has symmetric distribution and extra stability.
  • 🎯High-yield exceptions: Cr: 3d5 4s1, Cu: 3d10 4s1, Pd: 4d10 5s0.

🔎GENERAL OUTER CONFIGURATION (WHAT TO WRITE FIRST)

  • For d-block: electrons enter (n−1)d while ns also participates in valence.
  • Write outer configuration as (n−1)d1–10 ns1–2 (not fixed ns2).
  • In a given series, n is constant; only d count increases across the row.

⚙️CLASSIC EXCEPTIONS (HIGH-YIELD LIST + REASON)

  • Cr (Z=24): expected 3d44s2, actual 3d54s1 (half-filled d stability).
  • Cu (Z=29): expected 3d94s2, actual 3d104s1 (fully filled d stability).
  • Pd (Z=46): notable: 4d105s0 (complete d filling outweighs 5s occupancy).
  • Standard exam line: small ΔE(ns−(n−1)d) + extra stability of d5/d10.

⚖️NORMAL vs EXCEPTIONAL OUTER CONFIGURATIONS

Case What you may predict Actual ground state (learn)
General d-block (n−1)d1–10 ns2 (often assumed) (n−1)d1–10 ns1–2
Cr (3d series) 3d4 4s2 3d5 4s1
Cu (3d series) 3d9 4s2 3d10 4s1
Pd (4d series) 4d8 5s2 (or 4d9 5s1) 4d10 5s0

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Give correct ground-state outer electronic configurations of Cr and Cu, and justify each briefly.
Answer:
Step 1: Write predicted Aufbau forms: Cr ≈ 3d44s2, Cu ≈ 3d94s2.
Step 2: Note ns and (n−1)d are very close in energy in d-block → rearrangement possible.
Step 3: Prefer stable subshells: d5 (half-filled) and d10 (fully filled).
Step 4: Therefore Cr = 3d54s1; Cu = 3d104s1.
✓  Cr: 3d54s1; Cu: 3d104s1, due to small ΔE and d5/d10 stability.
2
Pd is an exception: write its outer configuration and explain why it is plausible in one line.
Answer:
Step 1: In 4d series, ns and (n−1)d levels are very close → shifting electrons between them is feasible.
Step 2: A fully filled d subshell (d10) gains extra stability, so 5s can become empty.
Step 3: Hence Pd ground state outer configuration is 4d105s0.
✓  Pd: 4d105s0; stability of d10 + small ns/(n−1)d energy gap.

⭐Important Points

  • Write d-block as (n−1)d1–10 ns1–2; don’t force ns2 always.
  • State two reasons for anomalies: small ΔE and half/full-filled stability.
  • Must memorise exceptions in 3d series: Cr and Cu only (for this chapter slice).
  • Memorise standout exception in 4d series: Pd = 4d105s0.
  • Use exact wording: half-filled (d5) and fully filled (d10) subshells are extra stable.

💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: Craves 5, Curves to 10, Pd has 0 s”
Cr → wants d5: so 4s gives one electron: 3d54s1. Cu → wants d10: so 4s gives one electron: 3d104s1. Pd → goes d10 s0: remember as “Pd: Pure d, no s”.
TOPIC4
Ground-state outer electronic configurations across 3d, 4d, 5d, 6d series + key anomalies

SERIES-WISE CONFIGURATIONS (d-BLOCK)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Series-wise configuration = writing only the outer electrons as (n−1)dx nsy for d-block atoms.
  • Across a transition series, electrons enter (n−1)d while ns is the outermost shell.
  • General pattern: (n−1)d1–10 ns1–2; small (n−1)d vs ns energy gap causes exceptions.
  • Anomalies arise due to extra stability of half-filled d5 and fully filled d10 subshells.
  • Group 12 (Zn, Cd, Hg, Cn) have (n−1)d10 ns2; d is filled in atom and common ions.

🖼️Diagram

d-series filling: (n−1)d increases; ns may drop (1 or 0) in anomalous cases.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📌Write outer configuration only: ignore core noble gas part unless asked.
  • 🔁Across a series: d electrons increase from 1 to 10; ns is usually 2, sometimes 1 or 0.
  • ⚖️Reason for exceptions: very small ΔE(ns vs (n−1)d) + extra stability (exchange energy) of d5/d10.
  • 🚫Pd is special: 4d10 5s0 (not 4d8 5s2).
  • 🧠Exam trap: “All d-block are transition metals” is false; Group 12 fails IUPAC criterion (common ions are d10).

🔎3d SERIES (Sc → Zn): OUTER CONFIGURATIONS

  • Sc 4s2 3d1; Ti 4s2 3d2; V 4s2 3d3
  • Cr 4s1 3d5 (exception: d5 stability)
  • Mn 4s2 3d5; Fe 4s2 3d6; Co 4s2 3d7; Ni 4s2 3d8
  • Cu 4s1 3d10 (exception: d10 stability)
  • Zn 4s2 3d10 (Group 12 pattern)

⚙️4d, 5d, 6d SERIES + HIGH-YIELD ANOMALIES

  • 4d (Y → Cd) (selected key members): Y 5s24d1; Zr 5s24d2; Nb 5s14d4; Mo 5s14d5; Rh 5s14d8; Pd 5s04d10; Ag 5s14d10; Cd 5s24d10
  • 5d (La; Hf → Hg) (selected key members): La 6s25d1; Hf 6s25d2; Ir 6s25d7; Pt 6s15d9; Au 6s15d10; Hg 6s25d10
  • 6d (Ac; Rf → Cn) (selected key members): Ac 7s26d1; Rf 7s26d2; Ds 7s26d8; Rg 7s16d10; Cn 7s26d10
  • Group 12 signature: Zn/Cd/Hg/Cn = (n−1)d10 ns2 (filled d in atom and common ions).

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Write the ground-state outer configuration of Cr and explain why it differs from the expected configuration.
Answer:
Expected by simple filling: 3d44s2.
Because 3d and 4s energies are very close, one 4s electron shifts to 3d.
This gives a more stable half-filled subshell: 3d54s1 (extra stability due to exchange energy).
✓  Cr: 4s1 3d5 due to small 4s–3d energy gap and extra stability of d5.
2
A student claims: “Zn is a transition metal because it is in the d-block.” Correct the statement using configuration logic.
Answer:
IUPAC: transition metal must have an incomplete d subshell in atom or in any common ion.
Zn atom: 3d104s2 (d is complete).
Common ion Zn2+: 3d10 (still complete).
✓  Zn is d-block but not a transition metal (d10 in atom and common ions).

⭐Important Points

  • Always write as (n−1)d ns for outer configuration; series name fixes n (3d→4s, 4d→5s, 5d→6s, 6d→7s).
  • Key 3d anomalies: Cr 4s13d5, Cu 4s13d10.
  • Key 4d exception: Pd 5s04d10.
  • Key 5d anomalies: Pt 6s15d9, Au 6s15d10.
  • 6d series (as listed): Rg 7s16d10; Group 12 end: Cn 7s26d10.

💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: “5-10 stability steals one s””
If d becomes 5 or 10, one electron may shift from ns to (n−1)d. Recall: Cr, Mo → d5ns1; Cu, Au → d10ns1. Special lock: Pd → d10ns0; Group 12 always d10ns2.
TOPIC5
How partly filled (n−1)d and the small ns↔(n−1)d gap shape oxidation states, colour, complexes, catalysis and magnetism

CHARACTERISTIC d-ORBITAL PROPERTIES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Transition-metal hallmark properties arise mainly from partly filled (n−1)d subshells and the small energy gap between ns and (n−1)d orbitals.
  • (n−1)d orbitals are relatively diffuse and can be strongly influenced by surrounding ligands/atoms, affecting bonding and properties.
  • Small energy gap between ns and (n−1)d enables involvement of both in bonding and ion formation.
  • Different distributions of d electrons give multiple stable electronic arrangements in ions and complexes.
  • Ions with the same dn (n = 1–9) often show similar magnetic and electronic properties.

🖼️Diagram

Root causes (diffuse/partly filled d, small ns–d gap) lead to hallmark transition-metal properties.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧩Directional d orbitals + partly filled subshell ⇒ strong ligand interaction and rich coordination chemistry
  • 🔁Partly filled d and small ns↔(n−1)d gap ⇒ multiple oxidation states often differing by 1
  • 🎨Ligand field splits d levels; d–d transitions can absorb visible light ⇒ coloured ions/complexes
  • 🧲Unpaired d electrons ⇒ paramagnetism; d0 or d10 (usually) ⇒ diamagnetism
  • ⚗️Variable oxidation states + availability of orbitals/sites ⇒ catalytic cycles via adsorption and redox steps
  • ➡️Across a row (esp. 3d): gradual d filling causes small changes in size/chemistry ⇒ horizontal similarity

🔎WHY VARIABLE OXIDATION STATES ARE COMMON

  • Energy gap between ns and (n−1)d is small, so both can participate in bonding/ionisation.
  • Using different counts of ns and d electrons gives multiple oxidation states close in energy.
  • Up to Mn, maximum oxidation state often equals (s + d) valence electrons; later, higher states become less stable.
  • Ends of a series show fewer states: early metals have too few electrons; late metals approach stable d10.

⚙️COLOUR, COMPLEX FORMATION, CATALYSIS & MAGNETISM: ONE-LINE LINKS

  • Colour: ligand field splits d orbitals; absorption can promote d electrons between split levels (common for d1–d9).
  • Complexes: small/high-charge cations + availability of acceptor orbitals allow ligands to donate lone pairs (coordinate bonds).
  • Catalysis: multiple accessible oxidation states + surface/complex adsorption gives lower-energy pathways.
  • Paramagnetism: depends on number of unpaired d electrons; same dn often shows similar qualitative behaviour.

⚖️PARTLY FILLED vs FILLED d-SUBSHELL: PROPERTY CONSEQUENCES

Basis Incomplete d (typical transition ions) Filled d10 (Zn2+, Cd2+, Hg2+)
Oxidation states Often variable; differ by unity (e.g., V(II)→V(V)) Usually fixed: +2 common; little/no variability in common chemistry
Colour Often coloured (ligand-field splitting allows d–d transitions) Usually colourless due to absence of d–d transitions (though charge-transfer colours can occur in some compounds)
Complex formation Strong tendency; many coordination numbers/geometries Can form complexes (often via s/p acceptor orbitals); coordination numbers still vary, but no d–d spectral features
Magnetism Often paramagnetic (unpaired d electrons) Diamagnetic (all electrons paired)
Catalysis Very common (redox flexibility, adsorption, variable oxidation states) Less common as redox catalysts due to stable d10 configuration in common ions

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Explain why transition elements show greater similarity across a row (especially 3d) than s- and p-block elements.
Answer:
Across a transition series, added electrons enter (n−1)d while the outer ns count changes little.
d-electron shielding is not very effective, so size and effective nuclear attraction change gradually.
Hence neighbouring elements have close radii and similar energetics, giving strong horizontal similarity in reactions and complex chemistry.
✓  Gradual (n−1)d filling with small changes in size/energetics produces pronounced across-the-row similarity.
2
Why are many transition-metal ions coloured and paramagnetic, while ions like Zn2+ are usually colourless and diamagnetic?
Answer:
In complexes, colour commonly arises when ligand-field splitting permits d–d transitions in the visible region, which needs a partly filled d subshell.
Paramagnetism requires unpaired electrons; many transition ions have unpaired d electrons.
Zn2+ has d10: no unpaired electrons (diamagnetic) and no d–d transitions (so usually colourless).
✓  Partly filled d enables d–d transitions and often leaves unpaired electrons; d10 lacks both.

⭐Important Points

  • Core cause: partly filled (n−1)d subshells and relatively diffuse d orbitals are strongly affected by ligands and environment.
  • IUPAC: transition metal has incomplete d subshell in the atom or in at least one ion; Zn/Cd/Hg have d10 in atom and common ions, so they do not qualify.
  • Variable oxidation states arise from small ns vs (n−1)d energy gap; oxidation numbers often differ by 1.
  • Colour and magnetism largely track dn (n = 1–9): ligand-field transitions and unpaired electrons are possible.
  • Complex formation is favoured by high charge density and ability to accept lone pairs into suitable orbitals (coordinate bonding).

💡Memory Technique

“Mnemonic: VCCaP + S (from d)”
Variable oxidation states, Colour, Complexes, Catalysis, Paramagnetism All traced to partly filled, ligand-sensitive d orbitals; S = similarity across a row.
TOPIC6
Metallic character, high m.p./b.p., ΔaH trends, and bcc–hcp–ccp lattices with key exceptions

PHYSICAL PROPERTIES AND LATTICES (d-BLOCK)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Physical properties of many d-block metals arise from strong metallic bonding where ns and (in many cases) (n−1)d electrons can participate.
  • They show high tensile strength, ductility and malleability due to non-directional metallic bonds.
  • Generally high melting/boiling points reflect strong metal–metal bonding; strength varies across a series.
  • Enthalpy of atomisation, ΔaH, is the enthalpy to form 1 mol gaseous atoms from a metal; it measures metallic bond strength.
  • Many d-block metals show bcc/hcp/ccp lattices at normal temperature; Zn, Cd, Hg and Mn are key structural exceptions in the given source list.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔩High tensile strength: stronger metallic bonding gives higher resistance to breaking under tension.
  • 🧵Ductility/malleability: layers slide while the electron sea maintains cohesion.
  • 🔥High m.p./b.p.: when more valence electrons (ns and often d) contribute to bonding, metallic bonding strengthens.
  • ⚛️ΔaH trend: often rises to near the middle (many unpaired d electrons) then falls; anomalies exist (e.g., Mn, Tc).
  • 🧱Lattices: bcc/hcp/ccp are common; learn representative metals + structural exceptions (Zn, Cd, Hg, Mn).

🔎WHY TRANSITION METALS ARE HARD & HIGH-MELTING

  • Metallic bonding uses delocalised electrons; in d-block metals, (n−1)d electrons can also contribute.
  • More available bonding electrons ⇒ stronger M–M bonding ⇒ higher tensile strength and higher m.p./b.p.
  • Hardness and low volatility are typical; notable low-m.p. exceptions include Zn, Cd, and Hg (Hg is liquid).
  • Within a series, strongest bonding is often when many unpaired d electrons are available for interaction.

⚙️ENTHALPY OF ATOMISATION (ΔaH): CONCEPT + TRENDS

  • ΔaH: enthalpy to convert M(s) → M(g) for 1 mol atoms; higher ΔaH ⇒ stronger metallic bonding.
  • Illustrative 3d values from the standard table (kJ mol−1): Sc 326, Ti 473, V 515, Mn 281, Ni 430, Zn 126.
  • Maxima near the middle indicate special favourability of one unpaired electron per d orbital (many unpaired d electrons) for strong interatomic interaction.
  • 4d and 5d series generally have higher ΔaH than corresponding 3d elements; this supports more frequent metal–metal bonding in compounds of heavier transition metals.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Explain why transition metals (in general) have higher melting points than s-block metals.
Answer:
Strength of metallic bonding increases when more valence electrons are available for delocalisation.
In many transition metals, both ns and (n−1)d electrons can contribute to metallic bonding.
Stronger interatomic attraction means more energy is required to disrupt the metallic lattice.
Hence m.p./b.p. are generally high for transition metals (with notable low-m.p. exceptions like Zn, Cd, Hg).
✓  Because additional valence electrons (often including d electrons) strengthen metallic bonding, more energy is needed to melt them.
2
Across a transition series, why do ΔaH and melting points typically rise to a maximum near the middle and then fall? Mention key anomalies.
Answer:
From left to middle, the number of unpaired d electrons generally increases; more unpaired electrons favour stronger metal–metal interaction and higher ΔaH.
Near the middle (≈ d5), many metals show strongest bonding; beyond this, pairing increases and the effective bonding contribution often decreases.
Therefore ΔaH and m.p. tend to fall towards the end of the series.
The smooth trend has anomalies; the source highlights Mn (3d) and Tc (4d) as examples.
✓  The mid-series maximum is linked to many unpaired d electrons; anomalies include Mn and Tc.

⭐Important Points

  • Strong metallic bonding in d-block metals often involves (n−1)d electrons along with ns electrons.
  • m.p./b.p. trend in a row: generally increases to near d5 then decreases; Mn and Tc show anomalous values.
  • ΔaH measures metallic bond strength: M(s) → M(g) (1 mol atoms).
  • Transition metals (except Zn, Cd, Hg) are generally hard and have low volatility.
  • Crystal lattices commonly bcc/hcp/ccp; key structural exceptions at normal T in the source: Zn, Cd, Hg and Mn.

“Mnemonic: "Z-C-H + M" are the structure misfits”
Remember structural exceptions at normal T (as marked X in the list) as: Zn, Cd, Hg plus Mn. Hook: "Z-C-H" are group 12 end-members (d10), and "M" (Mn) is the odd one out.
TOPIC7
Small contraction across a series; 4d > 3d, but 5d ≈ 4d due to lanthanoid contraction

ATOMIC AND IONIC SIZE TRENDS (d- AND f-BLOCK)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Across a transition series, atomic and (same-charge) ionic radii show a small overall decrease with atomic number.
  • Cause: nuclear charge increases while added electrons enter (n−1)d; d-electron shielding is poor.
  • The decrease is not dramatic because added (n−1)d electrons partly shield outer ns electrons.
  • From 3d to 4d series, radii generally increase (extra shell).
  • From 4d to 5d series, radii are nearly the same due to lanthanoid contraction (4f filling before 5d).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📉For ions of same charge in one series: ionic radius decreases left → right.
  • 🧲Poor shielding by d electrons → effective nuclear charge felt by outer electrons increases.
  • 📏Atomic radii variation is small across a transition series (much less than s/p blocks).
  • 🧱4d atoms are larger than 3d (higher principal quantum number).
  • 🔁5d ≈ 4d sizes because 4f electrons shield very poorly → lanthanoid contraction.
  • 🧪Classic size-similarity pair (radii): Zr 160 pm, Hf 159 pm.

🔎WHY RADII CONTRACT (WITHIN ONE d-SERIES)

  • Each step: Z increases by 1 but added electron enters (n−1)d subshell.
  • d electrons shield poorly → effective nuclear charge felt by outer electrons increases.
  • So atomic radius decreases slightly and ionic radius (same charge) decreases progressively.
  • Contraction is small because (n−1)d electrons still provide some shielding to outer ns electrons.

⚙️LANTHANOID CONTRACTION (BEFORE 5d) + CONSEQUENCES

  • Before 5d series, 4f orbitals fill (lanthanoids).
  • 4f–4f shielding is very poor → effective nuclear charge increases strongly across 4f filling.
  • Result: regular decrease in atomic radii called lanthanoid contraction.
  • Consequence: expected 5d size increase is compensated → 4d and 5d pairs show similar radii.
  • Example: Zr 160 pm and Hf 159 pm → leads to unusually similar properties.

⚖️SIZE COMPARISON: 3d vs 4d vs 5d

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Explain why ionic radii (for ions of the same charge) generally decrease from left to right across a transition series.
Answer:
Across the series, nuclear charge increases by 1 each step.
The added electron enters (n−1)d, and d electrons shield poorly.
Hence effective nuclear charge on outer electrons increases → electrons are pulled closer → ionic radius decreases.
✓  Poor shielding by (n−1)d electrons makes effective nuclear charge rise across the series, causing progressive ionic-radius decrease (for same charge).
2

Answer:
From 3d to 4d, size increases mainly due to one extra shell (higher n).
Before 5d begins, 4f orbitals fill; 4f electrons show very poor shielding.
Thus effective nuclear charge rises strongly across lanthanoids causing lanthanoid contraction.
This contraction compensates the expected size increase for 5d elements → 5d ≈ 4d.
Data: Zr 160 pm, Hf 159 pm.
✓  Lanthanoid contraction (poor 4f shielding) offsets the normal size increase, giving 4d–5d radii similarity (Zr 160 pm ≈ Hf 159 pm).

⭐Important Points

  • Ionic radius decreases across a series for ions of the same charge (poor d-electron shielding).
  • Atomic radii also decrease across a transition series, but the change is small.
  • 4d radii are larger than 3d for corresponding elements.
  • 5d radii are ~same as 4d due to lanthanoid contraction (4f filling before 5d).
  • Key illustration: Zr 160 pm and Hf 159 pm → strong 4d–5d similarity in properties.

“Mnemonic: "3→4 BIG, 4→5 SAME"”

FIRST TRANSITION (3d) SERIES: DATA TRENDS (Sc–Zn)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • First-series data trends summarise how Sc→Zn show linked changes in configurations, successive ionisation enthalpies ΔiH° and E° values.
  • On ionisation of 3d metals, 4s electrons are removed before 3d because in cations the 4s orbital becomes higher in energy than 3d.
  • Across Sc→Zn, metallic radius contracts slightly overall; 3d shielding is poor so Zeff increases.
  • ΔiH1° shows a small, irregular overall rise; ΔiH2° and ΔiH3° rise more and show breaks due to extra stability of d5 and d10 configurations.
  • E°(M2+/M) is less negative overall; Cu is positive and Mn/Ni/Zn show notable deviations from a smooth trend.
  • Density generally increases from Ti to Cu because atomic mass increases while metallic radius decreases; Zn is lower than Cu because Zn has lower atomic mass and a larger metallic radius than Cu.

TOPIC9
IE1 slight rise, IE2/IE3 sharper rise; key breaks from d5/d10 stability + exchange energy

IONISATION ENTHALPY EXPLANATIONS (d-BLOCK)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Ionisation enthalpy (IE) is the enthalpy needed to remove electron(s) from gaseous atoms/ions stepwise.
  • Across 3d series, IE1 increases only slightly because 3d shielding offsets rising nuclear charge.
  • IE1 is irregular since ionisation changes relative 4s vs 3d energies; also 4s is removed before 3d.
  • IE2 and IE3 rise more because electrons are removed from smaller, more charged ions (mainly d-electrons).
  • Extra stability of d5 and d10 (exchange energy, symmetry) causes breaks in trends.
  • High IE3 for many late 3d metals makes oxidation states > +2 harder unless the compound is strongly stabilised (e.g., by electronegative ligands such as O, F).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🛡️3d electrons shield 4s reasonably → effective nuclear charge on 4s rises slowly → small IE1 rise.
  • 🔁On ionisation, ns electrons are lost before (n−1)d in transition metals.
  • 🧩Exchange energy (Hund’s rule) stabilises parallel-spin distributions; losing it raises IE.
  • ⭐Key stable checkpoints in 3d ions: Mn2+ = d5, Fe3+ = d5, Cu+ = d10, Zn2+ = d10.
  • 📈IE2 and IE3 are more diagnostic than IE1 for tendencies toward +2 and +3 (and higher) oxidation states in compounds.

🔎WHY IE1 INCREASES ONLY SLIGHTLY (AND BECOMES IRREGULAR)

  • Across Sc → Zn, nuclear charge increases but added electrons enter inner 3d subshell.
  • 3d electrons shield 4s so attraction on the 4s electron does not rise steeply.
  • Hence atomic size contracts only modestly → IE1 rises slightly overall.
  • IE1 is irregular because removing one electron can change relative 4s vs 3d energies.
  • Chemically, IE1 is less decisive because common ions are formed after removing two electrons.

⚙️IE2 & IE3: EXCHANGE ENERGY + d5/d10 STABILITY → KEY BREAKS

  • After IE1, ions tend to be 3dn (4s already removed) → IE2/IE3 probe d-electrons.
  • d-electrons shield each other poorly → effective nuclear charge on remaining electrons rises → IE2, IE3 increase.
  • Exchange energy stabilises parallel spins; ionisation that destroys this stability needs extra energy.
  • Break in IE2 around Mn because Mn2+ is d5 (half-filled) and strongly stabilised.
  • Break in IE3 around Fe because Fe3+ is d5; reaching/leaving this stable state affects the IE3 pattern.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Why is the second ionisation enthalpy of Cu unusually high, and what oxidation-state consequence follows?
Answer:
Cu: [Ar]3d104s1; after IE1, Cu+ = [Ar]3d10 (fully filled).
Removing another electron (IE2) breaks the very stable d10 arrangement → higher energy needed.
So Cu+ is relatively stable, and forming Cu2+ is less favoured than expected from a smooth trend; oxidation states higher than +2 are rare and need strong oxidants/ligand stabilisation.
✓  Cu+ has stable d10, so IE2 is high; therefore Cu+ is relatively stabilised (and higher oxidation states are uncommon without strong stabilisation).
2
Explain the break/irregularity in IE2/IE3 trends around Mn and Fe using electronic configurations and exchange energy.
Answer:
Upon forming 2+ and 3+ ions, 4s electrons are already removed, so configurations are mainly 3dn.
Mn2+ is 3d5 (half-filled): maximum parallel spins → high exchange energy → extra stability.
Ionisation that moves away from this stable d5 loses exchange stabilisation → requires more enthalpy, producing a trend break.
Similarly, Fe3+ is 3d5; reaching/leaving d5 affects the IE3 pattern near Fe.
✓  Breaks occur because d5 (Mn2+, Fe3+) has extra exchange-energy stability (Hund’s rule), altering the otherwise rising IE2/IE3 trend.

⭐Important Points

  • State clearly: 4s electrons are removed before 3d in forming transition-metal cations.
  • Across 3d series, IE1 increases slightly due to 3d shielding of the 4s electron.
  • IE1 irregularity: ionisation perturbs relative 4s–3d energies; do not expect a smooth curve.
  • IE2/IE3 rise more because they involve more highly charged, smaller ions and mainly d-electron removal.
  • Key stability causes: d5 (Mn2+, Fe3+) and d10 (Cu+, Zn2+).

TOPIC10
Sc→Zn oxidation states, max near middle, (s+d) rule up to Mn, stability controls incl. π-acceptors

OXIDATION STATE TRENDS (d-BLOCK)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Oxidation state trend in 3d metals: multiple accessible oxidation numbers due to close ns and (n−1)d energies.
  • Variable oxidation states often differ by unity (e.g., V(II)→V(III)→V(IV)→V(V)).
  • Maximum variety occurs near the middle of the series; Mn shows a wide range (commonly +2 to +7).
  • Up to Mn, maximum oxidation state ≈ total (s + d) valence electrons; beyond Mn, very high oxidation states are generally less stable and are mainly seen with strongly electronegative ligands (O, F) and in complexes.
  • Very low oxidation states (even 0) are stabilised in complexes with π-acceptor ligands like CO.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 📌Sc→Zn common oxidation states (typical): Sc(+3); Ti(+4,+3,+2); V(+5 to +2); Cr(+6,+3,+2); Mn(+7 to +2); Fe(+3,+2); Co(+3,+2); Ni mainly +2 (also +3 in some complexes); Cu(+2,+1); Zn(+2).
  • 🧠Middle maximum: more available valence electrons and accessible orbitals → more possible oxidation states.
  • 🧩Ends show fewer states: early members have too few valence electrons; late members have high d-electron count/filled d subshell → higher oxidation states are disfavoured.
  • ⬆️Max oxidation state up to Mn: Ti(IV), V(V), Cr(VI), Mn(VII) commonly as oxo-species (MOx) or oxo-ions.
  • ⬇️After Mn: very high oxidation states are less stable; +2/+3 become more common for Fe/Co; Ni mainly +2; Cu +1/+2; Zn only +2.
  • 🧲Configuration stability matters: d5 and d10 give extra stability, influencing which oxidation states persist.

🔎WHY MAXIMUM VARIETY NEAR THE MIDDLE?

  • Across Sc→Mn, increasing number of valence (s+d) electrons enables multiple oxidation states.
  • Near the middle, several oxidation levels can be stabilised by bonding and electronic factors (including exchange energy).
  • Early members: limited electrons to lose/share, so fewer higher oxidation states are feasible.
  • Late members: high d-electron count/filled d subshell reduces tendency for very high oxidation states; +2/+1 dominate (Cu), +2 only (Zn).

⚙️STABILITY RULES & HIGH/LOW OXIDATION STATES

  • Up to Mn: max oxidation state generally equals total (ns + (n−1)d) electrons.
  • Typical stable high states in oxo-ions: TiO2 (Ti(IV)), VO2+ (V(V)), CrO42− (Cr(VI)), MnO4− (Mn(VII)).
  • After Mn: very high oxidation states are less stable; when present, they are often strong oxidants and prefer reduction to lower states.
  • Down d-block groups 4–10: higher oxidation states stabilise for heavier congeners (distinct from inert pair effect).
  • π-acceptor ligands stabilise low oxidation states via back-bonding: Ni(CO)4, Fe(CO)5 have M in oxidation state 0.

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Explain why Mn shows the maximum number of oxidation states in the 3d series, while Zn shows essentially only +2.
Answer:
Mn has outer configuration 3d54s2, so several valence electrons can participate in bonding, allowing multiple oxidation states up to +7 (notably in oxo-species).
In the middle of the series, multiple oxidation levels are accessible because the energy gap between 4s and 3d is small and several electrons can be removed/used.
Zn has 3d104s2; the d subshell is completely filled and is reluctant to participate in oxidation-state changes.
After losing two 4s electrons, Zn forms Zn2+ (3d10) which is especially stable; higher oxidation would require disturbing the stable d10 configuration.
✓  Mn can access many oxidation levels due to multiple usable valence electrons and stabilisation of high states in oxo-compounds; Zn is stabilised as Zn(II) with d10, so it is essentially only +2.
2
Assign oxidation states of metals in Ni(CO)4 and Fe(CO)5, and state why such low oxidation states are stable.
Answer:
CO is a neutral ligand in oxidation-state counting (ligand charge = 0).
Ni(CO)4 is neutral overall, so oxidation state of Ni = 0.
Fe(CO)5 is neutral overall, so oxidation state of Fe = 0.
CO is a π-acceptor ligand: it accepts electron density by back-bonding, stabilising metals in low/zero oxidation states.
✓  Ni in Ni(CO)4 = 0; Fe in Fe(CO)5 = 0; π-acceptor back-bonding by CO stabilises the low oxidation state.

⭐Important Points

  • Know the oxidation-state pattern Sc→Zn; neighbouring states often differ by 1.
  • Mid-series shows maximum variability; Mn commonly shows +2 to +7.
  • Up to Mn: maximum oxidation state generally equals total (s+d) electrons; typical high states occur in oxo-species.
  • After Mn: very high oxidation states are generally less stable; +2/+3 become dominant for many ions/compounds.
  • Group 6: Mo(VI), W(VI) more stable than Cr(VI); hence Cr(VI) is a strong oxidant in acid.

TOPIC11
Why E°(M2+/M) and E°(M3+/M2+) vary: atomisation + ionisation vs hydration; key anomalies & redox behaviour

ELECTRODE POTENTIAL TRENDS (d-BLOCK)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Standard electrode potential E° is the standard reduction potential of a redox couple in aqueous solution.
  • For metals: E°(M2+/M) corresponds to M2+(aq) + 2e− → M(s).
  • E°(M3+/M2+) corresponds to M3+(aq) + e− → M2+(aq).
  • E° reflects a balance: energy required to form gaseous ions (atomisation + ionisation) vs energy released on hydration (more negative ΔhydH° stabilises aqueous ions).
  • More negative E°(M2+/M) ⇒ metal is more readily oxidised (stronger reducing agent); more positive E° ⇒ nobler metal.

🖼️Diagram

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🔁E°(M2+/M) generally becomes less negative from Ti → Cu; major influence is the rise in (ΔiH°1 + ΔiH°2) across the series.
  • 💧Hydration enthalpy ΔhydH°(M2+) is negative; more negative hydration stabilises M2+(aq) and tends to make reduction potentials less negative.
  • 🧱High ΔaH° (strong metallic bonding) raises the energy cost of forming ions, tending to shift E°(M2+/M) towards more positive values (greater nobility).
  • 🧩Anomalies in E°(M2+/M): Mn and Zn (extra stability of Mn2+ d5, Zn2+ d10), Ni (strong hydration), Cu becomes positive (+0.34 V).
  • 🔥Mn3+ (d4) is a strong oxidising agent because it readily forms stable Mn2+(d5); Cr2+ (d4) is a strong reducing agent because it readily forms more stable Cr3+.

🔎E°(M2+/M): OVERALL TREND + KEY ANOMALIES (Ti → Zn)

  • Overall pattern: from Ti(−1.63) to Cu(+0.34), E°(M2+/M) becomes less negative; Zn then shows a negative value (−0.76).
  • General reason: (ΔiH°1 + ΔiH°2) increases across the series, increasing the cost of forming M2+.
  • Mn anomaly: E°(Mn2+/Mn)=−1.18 is more negative than expected; Mn2+ has extra stability (d5).
  • Ni deviation: E°(Ni2+/Ni)=−0.25 is influenced by strong hydration (very negative ΔhydH° for Ni2+).
  • Zn anomaly: E°(Zn2+/Zn)=−0.76 is more negative than expected; Zn2+ is extra stable (d10).

⚙️E°(M3+/M2+): STABILITY-BASED REDOX BEHAVIOUR

  • E°(M3+/M2+) measures ease of reducing M3+ to M2+; higher E° ⇒ M3+ is a stronger oxidising agent.
  • Mn: E°(Mn3+/Mn2+) = +1.57 V; Mn3+ readily reduces to stable Mn2+(d5) ⇒ strong oxidising agent.
  • Cr: E°(Cr3+/Cr2+) = −0.41 V; Cr2+ readily oxidises to more stable Cr3+ ⇒ Cr2+ is a strong reducing agent.
  • Fe: E°(Fe3+/Fe2+) = +0.77 V; Fe3+(d5) is relatively stable, so the tendency to reduce to Fe2+ is moderate compared to very high-E° cases like Mn3+.
  • Rule of thumb: the redox direction that produces a more stable electronic configuration (often d5 or d10) is favoured.

⚖️Interpreting E° in 3d metals: M2+/M vs M3+/M2+

Half-reaction (as reduction potential) M2+ + 2e− → M(s) M3+ + e− → M2+

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Concept: Why is Cu a 'noble' metal compared to Ti based on E°(M2+/M)?
Answer:
E° is listed as a reduction potential: M2+ + 2e− → M(s).
Cu has E°(Cu2+/Cu) = +0.34 V, which means Cu2+ is comparatively easier to reduce to Cu and Cu(s) is less willing to oxidise.
Ti has E°(Ti2+/Ti) = −1.63 V, meaning Ti(s) is much more willing to oxidise to Ti2+, so Ti is a stronger reducing agent (more reactive metal).
✓  Cu is nobler because its E° is more positive; Ti is more reactive because its E° is much more negative.
2
Concept: Why is Mn3+ a strong oxidising agent whereas Cr2+ is a strong reducing agent, even though both correspond to d4 configurations?
Answer:
For Mn3+/Mn2+, E° is large and positive (+1.57 V), so Mn3+ readily gains an electron (gets reduced).
Reduction produces Mn2+ (d5), which has extra stability; this drives Mn3+ to oxidise other species.
For Cr3+/Cr2+, E° is negative (−0.41 V), so Cr2+ tends to lose an electron (gets oxidised) to form Cr3+.
Oxidation produces Cr3+, which is relatively more stable in aqueous solution than Cr2+, so Cr2+ acts as a reducing agent.
✓  Mn3+ is oxidising because it is easily reduced to stable Mn2+(d5); Cr2+ is reducing because it is easily oxidised to more stable Cr3+.

⭐Important Points

  • E°(M2+/M) is controlled by a competition between (atomisation + ionisation) costs and hydration stabilisation of M2+(aq).
  • Across Ti→Cu, E°(M2+/M) generally becomes less negative; Zn shows a notable negative value again (anomaly).
  • Remember key anomalies in E°(M2+/M): Mn (−1.18), Ni (−0.25), Zn (−0.76), and Cu is positive (+0.34).
  • Cu (+0.34 V) does not liberate H2 from dilute non-oxidising acids; it reacts with oxidising acids instead (application of nobility).
  • E°(M3+/M2+) reflects stability differences: Mn3+ is oxidising; Cr2+ is reducing.

MAGNETISM, COLOUR, COMPLEXES & CATALYSIS (d-BLOCK/TRANSITION ELEMENTS)

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Many transition-metal ions show paramagnetism, colours, complex formation and catalysis due to incomplete d subshells in the atom/ion (IUPAC idea).
  • Exam alert: Zn, Cd, Hg are in the d-block but are not transition metals by IUPAC (d10 in atom and common ions).
  • Magnetism depends on the number of unpaired electrons (n) in the metal/ion.
  • Colour commonly arises from d–d electronic transitions in complexes (visible-light absorption), though charge-transfer can also give intense colours.
  • Complexes are coordination compounds where ligands donate lone pairs to a central metal ion.
  • Catalysis occurs via variable oxidation states and/or adsorption and intermediate complex formation.

🖼️Diagram

Partially filled d orbitals drive magnetism, colour, complex formation and catalytic behaviour.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧲Diamagnetic: all electrons paired; Paramagnetic: ≥1 unpaired electron; Ferromagnetic: domain alignment (Fe, Co, Ni).
  • 📐Spin-only magnetic moment (approx., orbital contribution often quenched in 3d complexes): μso = √(n(n+2)) BM.
  • 🎨Observed colour is complementary to absorbed wavelength in the visible region.
  • 🧪Complex examples: [Fe(CN)6]3−, [Cu(NH3)4]2+. CN− is a strong-field ligand (often low spin for 3d), NH3 is moderate-field.
  • ⚗️Industrial catalysts: V2O5 (Contact process), Fe (Haber), Ni (hydrogenation).
  • 🧱Interstitials: TiC, VH0.56; small atoms (C, H, N, B) in interstices → hardness increases; metallic character largely retained.

🔎1) MAGNETISM: TYPES + SPIN-ONLY IDEA

  • Diamagnetic: all e− paired; weakly repelled; typical for d0, d10 (e.g., Zn2+ is d10).
  • Paramagnetic: unpaired e−; attracted; generally increases with number of unpaired e−.
  • Ferromagnetic: strong permanent magnetism via domain alignment (Fe, Co, Ni; some alloys).
  • Spin-only moment is an approximate measure based on unpaired electrons; orbital contribution is often quenched in many 3d complexes.

⚙️2) COLOUR + COMPLEXES + CATALYSIS + INTERSTITIALS

  • d–d transitions: ligand field splitting allows absorption in visible → coloured complexes (intensity often moderate).
  • Observed colour = complementary of absorbed light (e.g., absorb red → appear greenish).
  • Aquated-ion recall: [Ti(H2O)6]3+ purple; [V(H2O)6]2+ violet; [V(H2O)6]3+ green; [Cu(H2O)6]2+ blue; [Fe(H2O)6]3+ yellow-brown.
  • Complex examples: [Fe(CN)6]3− (coordination no. 6); [Cu(NH3)4]2+ deep blue.
  • Catalysis (industrial): 2SO2 + O2 ⇌ 2SO3 (V2O5); N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 (Fe); hydrogenation of alkenes (Ni).

✏️Solved Q&A

1
Fe3+ (high spin, d5) is paramagnetic. Explain why and state its unpaired electron count.
Answer:
High-spin d5 distributes one electron in each of the five d orbitals before pairing (Hund's rule).
So it has 5 unpaired electrons, hence it is strongly paramagnetic.
✓  High-spin Fe3+ (d5) has 5 unpaired electrons → paramagnetic.
2
A complex has magnetic moment close to 3.9 BM (spin-only region). What unpaired-electron count is most consistent, and what does it indicate?
Answer:
Spin-only moments near 3.8–3.9 BM are characteristic of about 3 unpaired electrons.
This suggests a paramagnetic complex with three unpaired spins (common for several dn cases depending on ligand field).
✓  Most consistent with 3 unpaired electrons → the complex is paramagnetic.

⭐Important Points

  • IUPAC: transition metal has incomplete d subshell in atom or one of its ions; Zn, Cd, Hg are d-block but not transition metals.
  • Magnetism is governed by unpaired electrons; d0/d10 are typically diamagnetic.
  • Spin-only moment is approximate; deviations occur when orbital contribution is not quenched (more in heavier metals).
  • Colour is often due to d–d transitions; d0 and d10 complexes are often colourless unless charge-transfer occurs.
  • Catalysis: variable oxidation states and/or adsorption and intermediate formation explain high catalytic activity.
