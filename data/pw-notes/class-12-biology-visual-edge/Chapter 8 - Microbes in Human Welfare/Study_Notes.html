TOPIC1
Microbes occur everywhere (even extremes) and include cellular + acellular infectious agents

MICROBIAL UBIQUITY AND DIVERSITY

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Microbial ubiquity: presence of microbes in almost all habitats on Earth, including extreme conditions.
  • Microbes are major components of Earth’s biological systems besides plants and animals.
  • They occur in soil, water, air, and in/on humans, animals, and plants (normal flora/commensals too).
  • They also survive in extreme habitats where many organisms cannot: heat, cold, depth, acidity.
  • Microbial diversity includes cellular microbes (prokaryotes/eukaryotes) and acellular infectious agents.

🖼️Diagram

One glance map: microbes are everywhere, including extremes; diversity includes cellular microbes and acellular agents.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🌍Ubiquity: soil, water, air, and inside living bodies.
  • 🔥Extremophiles: thermal vents/geysers may be as high as 100°C in some locations.
  • ❄️Cryo-habitats: microbes occur under metres-thick snow layers.
  • ⛏️Subsurface: microbes found in deep soil where light is absent.
  • 🧪Acidophiles: microbes can persist in highly acidic environments.
  • 🧫Many bacteria and fungi can be cultured; viruses cannot grow on inert media.

🔎WHERE MICROBES OCCUR (UBIQUITY + EXTREMES)

  • Common habitats: soil, water bodies, air/dust, and on surfaces of organisms.
  • Inside organisms: gut, skin, respiratory tract, rhizosphere → many are harmless or beneficial.
  • Thermal vents/geysers: temperatures may be as high as 100°C in some locations.
  • Deep soil/subsurface: low nutrients, low oxygen in pockets; microbes persist and recycle elements.
  • Snow/ice layers: microbes survive low temperature and limited liquid water.

⚙️DIVERSITY: MAJOR MICROBIAL GROUPS (INCLUDING ACELLULAR AGENTS)

  • Protozoa: microscopic animal-like eukaryotes; many are motile and heterotrophic.
  • Bacteria: prokaryotes; shapes include cocci (spherical) and bacilli (rod); some have flagella.
  • Fungi: eukaryotes; include yeasts (unicellular) and moulds (filamentous hyphae).
  • Viruses: obligate intracellular parasites; replicate only inside living cells (host machinery).
  • Viroids: infectious agents smaller/simpler than viruses; consist of small RNA without protein coat (classically plant pathogens).

TOPIC2
How microbiologists grow and see microbes in the lab

CULTURE AND COLONIES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Culture: growth of microbes on a suitable nutritive medium under controlled conditions.
  • A culture is used to increase microbe number for observation, identification, and experiments.
  • Colony: a visible mass of microbial growth on a solid medium formed by multiplication of microbes.
  • A colony often arises from a very small starting population and becomes visible to the naked eye.
  • Bacteria and many fungi can be grown on nutritive media to form colonies (commonly in Petri dishes).

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧪Nutritive medium + controlled conditions → microbial growth (culture).
  • 🧫Solid medium → discrete colonies; liquid medium → turbidity/growth but no colonies.
  • 👀Colonies are visible; individual microbes usually need a microscope.
  • 🍄Many fungi also form colonies on plates, often cottony/spreading.
  • 🔬Cultures are essential tools for studying micro-organisms in the lab.

🔎CULTURE: WHAT IT MEANS IN THE LAB

  • Requires a suitable nutritive medium (food source).
  • Requires controlled conditions (e.g., proper temperature).
  • Purpose: to obtain enough microbes for study (microscopy, tests, research).
  • Bacteria and many fungi are commonly cultured on media.

⚙️COLONIES: WHAT YOU SEE ON A PLATE

  • A colony is a visible mass on a solid medium.
  • Formed by repeated multiplication of microbes at one site on the medium.
  • Often visible with naked eye, unlike individual bacterial cells.
  • Useful for observing growth pattern and for picking growth for further study.

TOPIC3
Everyday food transformations driven by useful microbes

HOUSEHOLD FERMENTATIONS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Household fermentation is the microbial conversion of food substrates (milk, dough/batter, sap) into products with new texture, taste and shelf-life.
  • It is usually carried out by bacteria or yeast under suitable warm conditions; fermentation metabolism commonly occurs in low-oxygen/anaerobic microenvironments.
  • Microbes produce acids, gases and other metabolites that change food properties (souring, curdling, puffing).
  • A small inoculum (starter) introduces a very large number of desired microbes to begin fermentation quickly.
  • Fermented foods can have improved digestibility, flavour and certain vitamins (as described for curd).

🔎CURD (YOGHURT) FROM MILK — LAB

  • Microbes: Lactic acid bacteria (LAB), e.g., Lactobacillus and others.
  • Starter: a little curd added to warm milk provides millions of LAB cells.
  • LAB produce acids → milk proteins coagulate; proteins are partially digested.
  • Benefit (as per NCERT): nutritional quality improves; vitamin B12 content can increase.
  • In stomach: LAB can help check disease-causing microbes.

⚙️FERMENTED DOUGH / BATTER (IDLI, DOSA, BREAD)

  • Idli/dosa batter fermentation is carried out by bacteria (household fermentation).
  • Puffed-up batter/dough is due to CO2 produced during microbial metabolism.
  • Bread is fermented using baker’s yeast: Saccharomyces cerevisiae.
  • CO2 gets trapped in dough → leavening (rise) and porous texture.

CHEESE AND TRADITIONAL FERMENTS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Microbe-based foods are produced when specific microbes grow on food substrates and convert them into products with new taste, texture and aroma (via fermentation and/or ripening).
  • Cheese is among the oldest microbe-made foods; different varieties depend on the microbes used during ripening.
  • Traditional ferments include curd, fermented dough/batters (idli/dosa), bread and drinks like toddy.
  • Microbial metabolism can release gases (especially CO2) that change food structure (e.g., holes or puffing).
  • A selected inoculum (starter) introduces the desired microbes to ensure predictable fermentation.

TOPIC5
Large-scale microbial growth under controlled conditions to make useful products

INDUSTRIAL FERMENTATION AND FERMENTORS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Industrial fermentation = cultivation of selected microbes in large vessels (fermentors) to synthesise commercially valuable products.
  • Fermentors (bioreactors) maintain optimal conditions (nutrients, temperature, pH, aeration, agitation) for high yield.
  • Microbes used are chosen for specific products like ethanol, organic acids, enzymes and drugs.
  • Inoculum (starter culture) is added to sterile medium to begin controlled microbial growth.
  • Product may be obtained from cells (biomass) or from broth (metabolites), followed by downstream processing.

🖼️Diagram

A simplified stirred-tank fermentor showing key control and flow parts.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🏭Fermentor = industrial-scale vessel for growing microbes and harvesting product
  • 🧪Sterile nutrient medium + inoculum + controlled parameters = reproducible yield
  • 🌬️Aeration + agitation are critical for aerobic processes (oxygen supply, mixing)
  • 🧫Aseptic conditions reduce contamination and prevent batch failure
  • 🍺Brewer's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments malted cereals/fruit juices to ethanol
  • 🧴Microbes can produce acids/enzymes: citric acid, acetic acid, pectinases, proteases, lipases

🔎WORKING (INDUSTRIAL FERMENTATION FLOW)

  • Select high-yield microbe + suitable substrate/medium.
  • Sterilise medium and vessel to prevent contamination.
  • Add inoculum (starter) → controlled growth in fermentor.
  • Maintain pH, temperature, aeration and agitation as required.
  • Harvest: separate cells/broth and perform downstream processing to purify product.

⚙️MICROBES AND PRODUCTS (HIGH-YIELD PAIRS)

  • Ethanol: Saccharomyces cerevisiae (brewer's yeast) on malted cereals/fruit juices.
  • Citric acid: Aspergillus niger.
  • Acetic acid: Acetobacter aceti.
  • Butyric acid: Clostridium butylicum.
  • Lactic acid: Lactobacillus.

TOPIC6
Definition of antibiotics + the chance discovery, development, and impact of penicillin

ANTIBIOTICS AND PENICILLIN

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Antibiotics are chemical substances produced by some microbes that kill or inhibit other (disease-causing) microbes.
  • Word origin: anti = against, bio = life; i.e., "against life" (against disease-causing organisms). In relation to humans, antibiotics help by saving patients.
  • Penicillin was the first antibiotic discovered and its discovery was a chance observation in a culture plate.
  • Fleming saw a zone of inhibition where Staphylococci failed to grow near a mould colony.
  • In the classic NCERT account, the mould was identified as Penicillium notatum and the antibacterial substance was named penicillin (named after Penicillium).

🖼️Diagram

Penicillin story: observation of inhibition → identification → medical development and global impact.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🧫Antibiotics show selective toxicity: harm microbes more than host at therapeutic doses.
  • 🦠Kill = bactericidal; retard growth = bacteriostatic (NCERT: kill or inhibit).
  • 🍄Penicillin is a microbial secondary metabolite produced by a fungus (mould).
  • 👨‍🔬Discovery: Alexander Fleming; development for therapy: Ernest Chain and Howard Florey.
  • ⚔️Clinical impact: widely used to treat wounded soldiers in World War II.
  • 🏅Nobel Prize (1945) shared by Fleming, Chain, and Florey for penicillin.

🔎DISCOVERY: FLEMING'S CHANCE OBSERVATION (1928)

  • Fleming was culturing Staphylococci when an unwashed plate developed a mould contaminant.
  • Near the mould colony, bacteria did not grow: a clear inhibition zone formed.
  • In the NCERT account, mould identified as Penicillium notatum; antibacterial substance named penicillin.
  • Key inference: the mould released a diffusible chemical that inhibited bacterial growth.

⚙️DEVELOPMENT + HISTORICAL SIGNIFICANCE

  • Ernest Chain and Howard Florey established penicillin's full therapeutic potential.
  • Penicillin was used extensively to treat American soldiers wounded in World War II.
  • The work transformed treatment of bacterial infections and marked a milestone in modern medicine.
  • Nobel Prize (1945): Fleming, Chain, and Florey shared the award for penicillin.

⚖️Kill vs Inhibit (How antibiotics act)

TOPIC8
Microbes as medicine factories: clot buster, immunosuppressant, cholesterol-lowering drugs

BIOACTIVE MICROBIAL MOLECULES

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Bioactive microbial molecules are therapeutically useful products (often secondary metabolites/enzymes) made by microbes.
  • They are produced naturally by microbes and can be purified as drugs for human therapy.
  • Key exam set: streptokinase, cyclosporin A, and statins (source–use matching).

TOPIC9
Primary (physical) + Secondary (biological) treatment; BOD indicates organic pollution load

SEWAGE TREATMENT AND BOD

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Sewage treatment is the stepwise cleaning of municipal wastewater in an STP to reduce pollution and pathogens.
  • Primary treatment: physical removal of debris and solids by filtration and sedimentation.
  • Secondary treatment: microbial (biological) oxidation of dissolved organic matter using aeration tanks.
  • BOD is the oxygen microbes would consume to oxidise organic matter in water; higher BOD = higher organic pollution.
  • Secondary treatment forms flocs and activated sludge; part is recycled as inoculum, rest goes to digesters.

🖼️Diagram

Flow in an STP: primary separation → aeration/flocs → activated sludge recycling + anaerobic digestion.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🏭STP prevents direct sewage discharge into rivers; reduces polluting potential.
  • 🧹Primary treatment separates primary sludge (settled solids) from effluent (supernatant).
  • 💨Aeration tank: air + agitation promote aerobic microbes that consume organic matter.
  • 🧬Flocs = bacteria + fungal filaments forming mesh-like aggregates.
  • 🔁Activated sludge is recycled back as inoculum to maintain microbial population.
  • 🔥Anaerobic digesters generate biogas (CH4, CO2, H2S) from sludge.

🔎PRIMARY TREATMENT (PHYSICAL)

  • Objective: remove floating and settleable solids before microbial treatment.
  • Filtration/screening: sequential removal of large debris and suspended particles.
  • Sedimentation: grit (soil, small pebbles) settles in tanks.
  • Settled solids = primary sludge; remaining liquid = primary effluent.
  • Primary effluent is sent to secondary (biological) treatment.

⚙️SECONDARY TREATMENT: ACTIVATED SLUDGE PROCESS + DIGESTERS

  • Primary effluent enters aeration tanks; air is pumped and contents are mechanically agitated.
  • Aerobic microbes grow rapidly and form flocs (bacteria + fungal filaments).
  • Flocs oxidise/consume organic matter → BOD decreases markedly.
  • In settling tank, flocs sediment as activated sludge.
  • Small part returns to aeration tank as inoculum; major part to anaerobic sludge digesters.

TOPIC10
Anaerobic digestion of organic waste → methane-rich fuel + useful manure

BIOGAS AND METHANOGENS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Biogas is an inflammable mixture of gases produced by microbial activity during anaerobic decomposition of organic matter; methane (CH4) is predominant.
  • Major gases in biogas: CH4 with CO2 and H2S (in smaller amounts).
  • Methanogens are anaerobic bacteria that grow on cellulosic material and produce large amounts of methane, along with CO2 and H2 during their metabolism.
  • Common methanogen example: Methanobacterium.
  • Methanogens occur in anaerobic sludge digesters (STPs) and in the rumen of cattle, where they aid breakdown of cellulosic food material.

🖼️Diagram

Anaerobic microbes (including methanogens) act on dung slurry to release methane-rich biogas; leftover slurry is manure.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🦠Methanogens = anaerobic, methane-producing bacteria; key contributors to biogas formation.
  • 🐄Rumen methanogens help cattle digest cellulose; humans lack such microbial digestion.
  • 🧪Anaerobic sludge digesters in STPs release biogas (mainly CH4, with CO2 and H2S).
  • 🛢️Gobar (cattle dung) is methanogen-rich; biogas from dung is called gobar gas.
  • 🏡Biogas is piped for cooking/lighting; spent slurry is used as fertiliser.

🔎METHANOGENS: WHO & WHERE

  • Anaerobic bacteria that act on cellulosic material and produce CH4 (also CO2 and H2).
  • Example: Methanobacterium.
  • Found in rumen of cattle: help breakdown of cellulose and support cattle nutrition.
  • Present in anaerobic sludge digesters during sewage treatment; biogas collected also contains H2S due to other anaerobes.

⚙️BIOGAS PLANT: WORKING (CORE STEPS)

  • Feedstock: bio-wastes + dung slurry (gobar is methanogen-rich).
  • In a deep concrete tank, anaerobic microbes decompose the slurry; gas forms continuously.
  • Biogas collects under a floating cover and is sent out through a gas outlet pipe.
  • Spent slurry is removed through another outlet and used as fertiliser.
  • Common in rural areas due to steady availability of cattle dung.

TOPIC11
Using microbes to control pests and plant pathogens, reducing chemical pesticides

MICROBIAL BIOCONTROL AGENTS

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Microbial biocontrol agents are microbes used as biological methods to control plant diseases and pests.
  • They help reduce use of toxic insecticides/pesticides/weedicides and lower environmental pollution.
  • Biocontrol in organic farming maintains pests at manageable levels (not complete eradication).
  • Targets are controlled through natural interactions like antagonism, infection, or toxin action.
  • Key examples: Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), Trichoderma spp., and baculoviruses (NPV).

🖼️Diagram

Major microbial agents and their typical targets in biocontrol.

🎯Key Concepts

  • 🌿Organic farming: supports biodiversity; promotes checks-and-balances in field ecosystem
  • ☠️Chemical control can be toxic, pollute soil/groundwater, and contaminate crops
  • 🧬Bt toxin genes can be introduced into plants (e.g., Bt-cotton) for insect resistance
  • 🐛Bt kills susceptible insect larvae after ingestion; toxin acts in the larval gut
  • 🍄Trichoderma spp. are free-living root ecosystem fungi; act against several plant pathogens
  • 🦋Baculoviruses (genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus) are species-specific; considered safer for many non-target organisms

🔎WHY BIOCONTROL (ORGANIC FARMING VIEW)

  • Avoids toxic chemical pesticides/insecticides/weedicides and reduces residue in crops.
  • Prevents pollution of soil and ground water.
  • Conventional chemicals can kill useful and harmful organisms indiscriminately.
  • Biocontrol aims for pest management, not pest eradication (supports natural predators/parasites).

⚙️KEY MICROBIAL BIOCONTROL AGENTS (NCERT EXAMPLES)

  • Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt): supplied as dried spores; sprayed on plants; larvae ingest; toxin is released/acts in the larval gut; caterpillars die.
  • Bt is relatively specific for certain insect larvae (not a broad-spectrum chemical insecticide).
  • Bt toxin genes can be introduced into plants to confer insect resistance (e.g., Bt-cotton).
  • Trichoderma spp.: free-living fungi in root ecosystems; effective against several plant pathogens.
  • Baculoviruses: pathogens of insects/arthropods; most used belong to genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus.

TOPIC12
Microbes that naturally improve soil nutrients: nitrogen fixers, mycorrhiza and cyanobacteria

BIOFERTILISERS AND SOIL ENRICHMENT

📖Definition & Introduction

  • Biofertilisers are organisms that enrich the nutrient quality of soil and improve plant nutrition.
  • They reduce dependence on chemical fertilisers, whose overuse contributes to environmental pollution.
  • Major groups used: nitrogen-fixing bacteria (symbiotic and free-living/associative), mycorrhizal fungi, and cyanobacteria.
  • They act mainly by increasing nutrient availability (especially N and P) rather than directly supplying chemical nutrients.
  • Often used in organic farming to replenish soil nutrients and support sustainable yield.

🖼️Diagram

Three major microbial biofertiliser groups and their main nutrient benefit (nitrogen fixation or improved phosphorus uptake) leading to higher soil fertility.

🎯Key Concepts

  • MicrRhizobium forms root nodules in legumes and fixes atmospheric N2 into usable nitrogen forms.
  • MicrFree-living/associative N-fixers: Azospirillum and Azotobacter raise soil nitrogen without nodule formation.
  • FungMycorrhiza (fungus-root symbiosis), commonly by Glomus, improves phosphorus uptake.
  • CyanCyanobacteria (e.g., Anabaena, Nostoc) act as key biofertilisers in paddy fields.
  • ProtMycorrhiza also increases tolerance to drought/salinity and resistance to root pathogens.

🔎NITROGEN-FIXING BIOFERTILISERS (BACTERIA)

  • Symbiotic: Rhizobium associates with leguminous plants and forms root nodules.
  • In nodules, Rhizobium fixes atmospheric N2 into organic nitrogen forms used by the plant.
  • Free-living/associative: Azospirillum and Azotobacter fix N2 while living in soil or in association with roots.
  • Net effect: increased soil nitrogen availability and reduced need for nitrogen fertilisers.

⚙️MYCORRHIZA & CYANOBACTERIA (P + N SUPPORT)

  • Mycorrhiza = symbiosis between fungus and plant roots; common genus: Glomus.
  • Fungal hyphae absorb phosphorus efficiently and transfer it to the plant → ↑ P uptake.
  • Mycorrhiza benefits: root pathogen resistance, salinity tolerance, drought tolerance, better plant growth.
  • Cyanobacteria are autotrophic; many fix N2 (e.g., Anabaena, Nostoc).
  • In paddy fields, cyanobacteria act as major biofertilisers and add organic matter to soil.
