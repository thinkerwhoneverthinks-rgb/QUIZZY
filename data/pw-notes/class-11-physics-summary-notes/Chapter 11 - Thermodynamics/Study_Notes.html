Chapter 11 : Thermodynamics

Introduction (Section 11.1)

Thermodynamics = branch of physics about:
  • heat + temperature, and
  • inter-conversion of heat with other energy forms (esp. work).
Core chapter idea: laws of thermal energy + processes where:
  • work → heat (e.g. rubbing palms in winter → warmth)
  • heat → work (e.g. steam engine: heat of steam does mechanical work on pistons/wheels)
Why precise defs matter: everyday words (heat, temperature, work) must be defined carefully to state laws correctly.

Historical development of the concept of heat: from “caloric” to energy

Old (rejected) “caloric” theory: heat treated as an invisible fluid in pores of matter; when hot + cold bodies touch, caloric “flows” until temperatures equalise.
Caloric analogy: like water flowing in a horizontal pipe between two tanks at different water levels → flow continues until levels equalise. Similarly, “caloric levels” (temperatures) were thought to equalise by caloric flow.
Modern view: heat is a form of energy (not a material fluid). Heat and work are two modes of energy transfer, and each can convert into the other.
Count Rumford (Benjamin Thomson), 1798: cannon-boring experiment
  • Boring a brass cannon produced huge heat, enough to boil water.
  • Key observation: heat produced depended on the work done (e.g. horses turning the drill), not on drill sharpness.
  • Why this hurts caloric theory: if heat were fluid in pores, a sharper drill should “scoop out” more caloric → not seen.
  • Natural explanation: work → heat ⇒ supports heat as energy + shows energy conversion.

Thermodynamics as a macroscopic science

Thermodynamics is macroscopic: studies bulk systems using macroscopic variables; avoids molecule-by-molecule detail.
Historical note: thermodynamic ideas/laws were built in the 19th century, before the molecular picture was firmly established.
Thermo description uses a small set of measurable macroscopic variables (common-sense + often directly measured). Example (gas): state given by pressure, volume, temperature, mass, composition.
Microscopic vs thermodynamic description (gas):
  • Microscopic: needs coordinates + velocities of an enormous number of molecules.
  • Kinetic theory: not full detail, but uses molecular velocity distribution.
  • Thermodynamics: skips molecular description; uses only macroscopic state variables (P, V, T, etc.).
Extra macroscopic variables (not directly sensed):
  • Entropy: measure of disorderness of the system.
  • Enthalpy: measure of total heat content of the system.
Also: a thermodynamic state is specified by five state variables: pressure, volume, temperature, internal energy, entropy.

Mechanics vs thermodynamics (what each studies)

Mechanics: motion of particles/bodies under forces + torques (focus: motion of the system as a whole). Thermodynamics: not about overall motion; focuses on the internal macroscopic state of the body.
Bullet example (mechanical vs thermal change):
  • Bullet fired: mechanical state changes (esp. kinetic energy); temperature need not change.
  • Bullet pierces wood + stops: KE converts to heat → temperature rises (bullet + nearby wood layers).
  • Key idea: temperature links to energy of internal (disordered) motion, not bulk motion of the object.
Exam trap: don’t mix up:
  • energy of motion of body as a whole = mechanical kinetic energy
  • energy of internal random motion = thermal/internal energy (linked to temperature)

Thermal equilibrium (Section 11.2)

Meaning of equilibrium: mechanics vs thermodynamics

Mechanical equilibrium: net external force = 0 and net external torque = 0.
Thermodynamic equilibrium: macroscopic variables characterising the system do not change with time.
Example (thermo equilibrium): gas in a closed rigid container, fully insulated, with fixed:
  • pressure
  • volume
  • temperature
  • mass
  • composition
…and all remain constant with time.

Role of surroundings and walls in achieving equilibrium

Equilibrium depends on:
  • the surroundings, and
  • the nature of the wall between system & surroundings (or between two systems).
Adiabatic wall (insulating): heat flow not allowed. Diathermic wall (conducting): heat flow allowed between systems → thermal equilibrium reached in due course.
Two-system setup (A & B, two gases): for a given mass of gas, choose pressure and volume as two independent variables.
  • System A: (PA, VA)
  • System B: (PB, VB)
These variables (plus the wall type between A & B) decide if heat exchange happens and whether thermal equilibrium is reached.
Exam tip: always mention wall type:
  • Adiabatic → no heat transfer → thermal equalisation blocked.
  • Diathermic → heat transfer allowed → temperatures tend to equalise with time.

Adiabatic Wall, Diathermic Wall, and Thermal Equilibrium

Adiabatic wall = insulating wall (can be movable) → no energy transfer as heat between two systems. Diathermic wall = conducting wall → allows energy transfer as heat between two systems.

Equilibrium behaviour with an adiabatic wall

  • Two gas systems A and B separated by an adiabatic wall.
  • Observation: any pair (PA, VA) can be in equilibrium with any pair (PB, VB).
  • Meaning: since there is no heat exchange through an adiabatic wall, equilibrium between A and B is not forced by “equal temperature” via heat flow (because heat can’t flow).

Equilibrium behaviour with a diathermic wall

  • Replace the adiabatic wall with a diathermic wall (conducting).
  • Now heat can flow between A and B.
  • Observation: macroscopic variables of A and B change spontaneously until both reach equilibrium states; then there is no further change.
  • State variables may shift to new equilibrium values:
    • System A: (PA, VA) → (P′A, V′A)
    • System B: (PB, VB) → (P′B, V′B)
  • At final equilibrium: no more energy (heat) flow from one to the other.
  • This is: A is in thermal equilibrium with B.
Thermal equilibrium (two systems in thermal contact) means: no net heat flow between them and (by experience + Zeroth Law) equal temperatures.
Constraint-dependent changes: On thermal contact, both P and V need not change—depends on constraints. If each gas is in a fixed-volume container, then only pressure changes to reach thermal equilibrium.

Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

Thought experiment leading to the law

  1. Take two systems A and B separated by an adiabatic wall (so no direct heat exchange A↔B).
  2. Let each be in contact with a third system C via a conducting (diathermic) wall.
  3. States (macroscopic variables) of A and B change until:
    • A reaches thermal equilibrium with C, and
    • B reaches thermal equilibrium with C.
  4. Now swap the A–B wall: replace the adiabatic wall with a conducting wall, and insulate C from A and B using an adiabatic wall.
  5. Observation: states of A and B do not change further when brought into contact ⇒ A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics: “Two systems in thermal equilibrium with a third system separately are in thermal equilibrium with each other.”
Historical note (short fact): Formulated by R. H. Fowler in 1931, after 1st & 2nd laws → so it was numbered “zeroth”.

Temperature as the quantity equal in thermal equilibrium

  • Zeroth Law implies: if two systems are in thermal equilibrium, there exists a physical quantity with the same value for both.
  • This variable is temperature, written T.
If A and B are separately in equilibrium with C: TA = TC and TB = TC ⇒ TA = TB TA: temperature of system A TB: temperature of system B TC: temperature of system C
Don’t treat “no change of state variables” as a vague idea: here it means after equilibrium, no further spontaneous macroscopic changes occur and no net heat flow continues.

Thermometry (what comes next)

  • After defining temperature via Zeroth Law, thermometry does the next step: assign numerical values to temperature → build a temperature scale.

Heat, Internal Energy and Work

Temperature and direction of heat flow

Temperature measures “hotness” and तय करता है heat flow की direction when bodies are in thermal contact.
  • Heat flows from higher temperature → lower temperature.
  • Flow stops when temperatures become equal ⇒ bodies in thermal equilibrium.

Internal energy (U): meaning and reference frame

Internal energy (bulk system) = sum of molecular kinetic energies + molecular potential energies.
  • Bulk system has huge number of molecules; U tracks energy at the molecular level.
  • Whole-system kinetic energy (centre-of-mass motion) is not used when defining internal energy.
  • So U is defined in the frame where the system’s centre of mass is at rest.
  • U includes only disordered energy due to random molecular motion.
  • Notation: internal energy is written U.
Include in U (gas): molecular KE from translational, rotational, vibrational motions (+ molecular PE). Exclude from U: KE due to the container/box moving as a whole (bulk motion) is not part of U.

Internal energy as a thermodynamic state variable

State variable (state function): depends only on the system’s state, not on the path/history used to reach it.
  • Even though we explain U using molecules, in thermo we treat U as a macroscopic variable.
  • Main property: U depends only on the state, not on how the state was achieved.
  • For a fixed mass of gas, U depends on the state given by:
    • pressure (P),
    • volume (V),
    • temperature (T).
  • U does not depend on the process/path to reach that (P, V, T).
  • Examples (gas): P, V, T, U are state variables.

Heat and work as modes of energy transfer

Heat and work are two different ways energy can be transferred to/from a system, changing its internal energy.
  • Heat: energy transfer because of temperature difference between system and surroundings (thermal contact + unequal T).
  • Work: energy transfer by means that do not involve temperature difference; e.g. piston moved by raising/lowering a weight.
Exam pitfall: Heat is not “energy stored in a body”. Heat = energy in transit due to temperature difference. Internal energy U = energy tied to the system’s microscopic state.

Changing Internal Energy: Heat and Work

Internal energy (U) is a state variable → fixed by the state (P, V, T, etc.), not by the path taken to reach it.
For a gas in a cylinder with a movable piston, there are two ways to change state (so U changes):
  • Heat transfer: put gas in contact with a hotter body → energy flows hot → gas (due to ΔT) → U increases.
  • Work done on the system: push piston down → do work on gas → U increases.
Both can also happen in reverse:
  • If surroundings are colder → heat flows out of gas → U decreases.
  • Gas can expand and push piston up → gas does work on surroundings → affects U.
Heat and work are two different modes of energy transfer that change a system’s internal energy.

Heat vs Internal Energy (Crucial Distinction)

Heat (Q) = energy in transit across the boundary because of a temperature difference. Internal energy (U) = energy stored inside the system as part of its thermodynamic state.
Heat and work are not state variables. They are process quantities (path dependent), not fixed just by initial/final states.
Meaningfulness of statements:
  • Meaningful: “A gas in a given state has a certain internal energy.”
  • Meaningful: “A certain amount of heat is supplied to the system.”
  • Meaningful: “A certain amount of work was done by the system.”
  • Meaningless: “A gas in a given state has a certain amount of heat.”
  • Meaningless: “A gas in a given state has a certain amount of work.”
In exams: never say “heat contained in a body”. A body has internal energy; heat is only energy transferred during a process.

First Law of Thermodynamics

Internal energy changes only via heat transfer and work transfer.
  • ∆Q = heat supplied to the system by surroundings
  • ∆W = work done by the system on surroundings
  • ∆U = change in internal energy of the system
First Law of Thermodynamics: ∆Q = ∆U + ∆W ∆Q: heat supplied to the system (J) ∆U: change in internal energy of the system (J) ∆W: work done by the system on the surroundings (J)
The First Law = conservation of energy for thermodynamic systems, counting energy exchange with surroundings via heat + work.
Alternative form: ∆Q − ∆W = ∆U ∆Q: heat supplied to the system ∆W: work done by the system ∆U: change in internal energy

Path Dependence and State Function

A system can go from initial → final state by different paths (e.g. gas from (P1, V1) to (P2, V2)):
  • Path A: change volume at constant pressure to (P1, V2), then change pressure at constant volume to (P2, V2).
  • Path B: change pressure at constant volume first, then change volume at constant pressure.
U is a state variable ⇒ ∆U depends only on initial + final states, not the path. In general ∆Q and ∆W are path dependent, but ∆Q − ∆W is path independent since it equals ∆U.
If a process has ∆U = 0 (example: isothermal expansion of an ideal gas), then: ∆Q = ∆W (all heat supplied becomes work done by the system).
Don’t write “heat supplied = change in internal energy” unless work done is zero. Always start from: ∆Q = ∆U + ∆W (with this sign convention).

Work Done by a Gas at Constant Pressure

In a cylinder + movable piston, the gas does work by moving the piston. Reasoning:
  • Force on piston = pressure × area
  • Work = force × displacement
  • area × displacement = change in volume
Work done against constant external pressure: ∆W = P∆V P: (constant) pressure against which gas expands (Pa) ∆V: change in volume of gas (m³) ∆W: work done by the gas (J)
First law for constant-pressure expansion/compression: ∆Q = ∆U + P∆V ∆Q: heat supplied to the system ∆U: change in internal energy P∆V: work term when pressure is constant

Worked Example: Liquid Water to Steam (1 g at Atmospheric Pressure)

Given:
  • Mass of water = 1 g
  • Latent heat (measured) = 2256 J/g → for 1 g, ∆Q = 2256 J
  • Atmospheric pressure P = 1.013 × 105 Pa
  • Volume in liquid phase Vl = 1 cm3
  • Volume in vapour phase Vg = 1671 cm3
Work done (by expansion at constant atmospheric pressure):
  • ∆V = Vg − Vl ≈ (1671 − 1) cm3 ≈ 1670 cm3 (text effectively uses 1671 cm3 in calculation)
  • Convert cm3 to m3: 1671 cm3 = 1671 × 10−6 m3
So,
∆W = P (Vg − Vl) = 1.013 × 105 × (1671 × 10−6) = 169.2 J ∆W: work done by the system (J) P: atmospheric pressure (Pa) Vg, Vl: vapour and liquid volumes (m³)
Using the first law (Eq. ∆Q = ∆U + ∆W):
∆U = ∆Q − ∆W = 2256 − 169.2 = 2086.8 J ∆U: change in internal energy during vaporisation (J) ∆Q: heat supplied (latent heat input) (J) ∆W: work done against atmosphere (J)
Conclusion: in liquid → vapour, most heat goes into increasing internal energy; a smaller part becomes expansion work against atmospheric pressure.
Unit trap: 1 cm3 = 10−6 m3. Missing this causes huge numerical errors.

Specific Heat Capacity

If heat ΔQ is given to a substance and its temperature rises from T to T + ΔT, then we define the heat capacity / specific heat ideas below.

Heat Capacity (of a given body)

Heat capacity: S = ΔQ / ΔT S: heat capacity of the body (J K−1) ΔQ: heat supplied (J) ΔT: temperature rise (K)
Key expectations:
  • ΔQ (so S too) ∝ mass of the substance.
  • S can depend on temperature (heat needed for a 1 K rise may change with T).

Specific Heat Capacity (per unit mass)

To remove the “how much substance?” effect, divide by mass m (kg).
Specific heat capacity: s = S/m = (1/m)(ΔQ/ΔT) s: specific heat capacity (J kg−1 K−1) S: heat capacity of the body (J K−1) m: mass (kg) ΔQ: heat supplied (J) ΔT: temperature rise (K)
s depends on: the nature of the substance + its temperature. Unit: J kg−1 K−1

Molar Specific Heat Capacity (per mole)

If we measure amount as moles μ (instead of mass), define heat capacity per mole:
Molar specific heat capacity: C = (1/μ)(ΔQ/ΔT) = S/μ C: molar specific heat capacity (J mol−1 K−1) μ: amount of substance (mol) S: heat capacity of the sample (J K−1) ΔQ: heat supplied (J) ΔT: temperature rise (K)
C (like s) does not depend on the amount of substance, but it does depend on:
  • nature of substance,
  • temperature,
  • how heat is supplied (process/conditions) (esp. for gases; extra conditions may be needed to define C or s).
Why define C? Because simple predictions are often possible for molar heat capacities.

Prediction of Molar Heat Capacity of Solids (Equipartition Result)

Measured specific and molar heat capacities of solids (at atmospheric pressure + room temperature) are listed in Table 11.1 (table continues beyond this portion). Using the law of equipartition of energy (covered later in Ch. 12), we can predict molar heat capacity of solids:
  • Take a solid with N atoms, each vibrating about its mean position.
  • A 1D harmonic oscillator has average energy: 2 × (1/2)kBT = kBT.
  • So in 3D: average energy per atom = 3kBT.
For 1 mole of the solid:
Internal energy of 1 mole (solid, classical equipartition): U = 3kBT × NA = 3RT U: internal energy of 1 mole (J) kB: Boltzmann constant T: absolute temperature (K) NA: Avogadro number R: gas constant, using kBNA = R
At constant pressure, first law: ΔQ = ΔU + PΔV. For a solid, ΔV is tiny, so:
For solids at constant pressure: ΔQ ≈ ΔU since PΔV is negligible.
Predicted molar specific heat of a solid (≈ constant pressure): C = ΔQ/ΔT = ΔU/ΔT = 3R C: molar specific heat capacity (J mol−1 K−1) R: gas constant
The step ΔQ ≈ ΔU for solids depends on negligible volume change. Don’t use it blindly for gases (where PΔV can be big).

Specific heat capacity of water

Calorie (old heat unit): originally = heat needed to raise 1 g of water by 1°C.
Careful measurements show water’s specific heat varies slightly with temperature from 0°C to 100°C, so a precise calorie must state the temperature range.
Precise 1 calorie: heat needed to raise 1 g of water from 14.5°C to 15.5°C.
Heat is energy → SI uses the joule (J) for heat (also for work + all energy).
Specific heat capacity of water (SI): c = 4186 J kg−1 K−1 = 4.186 J g−1 K−1 c: specific heat capacity of water J: joule (energy unit) kg, g: mass units K: kelvin (temperature interval; 1 K = 1°C interval)
Mechanical equivalent of heat: once described as the work needed to produce 1 cal of heat; now it’s just a conversion factor (cal ↔ J).
In SI, heat and work are both in joules, so “mechanical equivalent of heat” is basically unnecessary.
Exam tip: Don’t treat calorie as a “different kind” of quantity—heat and work are both energy; only the unit changes (cal vs J).

Specific heats of a gas: Cp, Cv and their relation for an ideal gas

Specific heat depends on the process/conditions of heating. For gases, two key molar specific heats:
  • Cv: molar specific heat at constant volume
  • Cp: molar specific heat at constant pressure
Mayer’s relation (ideal gas): Cp − Cv = R Cp: molar heat capacity at constant pressure Cv: molar heat capacity at constant volume R: universal gas constant

Derivation of Cp − Cv = R (from first law for 1 mole)

First law (1 mole, finite changes): ΔQ = ΔU + PΔV ΔQ: heat absorbed by the system ΔU: change in internal energy P: pressure (during the process) ΔV: change in volume
Constant volume: ΔV = 0 ⇒ from ΔQ = ΔU + PΔV, we get ΔQ = ΔU. So for 1 mole: Cv = (ΔQ/ΔT)v = (ΔU/ΔT)v = (ΔU/ΔT) We drop subscript v at the end because for an ideal gas, U depends only on T.
Constant pressure: divide first law by ΔT (keeping P constant): Cp = (ΔQ/ΔT)p = (ΔU/ΔT)p + (PΔV/ΔT)p For an ideal gas, U = U(T) ⇒ (ΔU/ΔT)p = (ΔU/ΔT).
Ideal gas equation (1 mole): PV = RT P: pressure V: volume (for 1 mole) T: absolute temperature R: universal gas constant
Differentiate (small changes) PV = RT at constant pressure (subscript p): (PΔV/ΔT)p = R Sub into Cp expression: Cp = (ΔU/ΔT) + R But Cv = (ΔU/ΔT), so: Cp − Cv = R
Common mistake: Using Cp − Cv = R for real gases without saying “ideal gas”. This uses PV = RT and U = U(T), which are ideal-gas properties.

Thermodynamic state variables and equation of state

Thermodynamic state variables (macroscopic): quantities whose values completely specify an equilibrium state of a system. Example (equilibrium gas): state given by P, V, T, and mass (plus composition if it’s a mixture).
State variables only make sense for equilibrium states. If the system isn’t in equilibrium, you can’t describe it with one clean set of P, V, T (they can be non-uniform inside the system).
Non-equilibrium examples (can’t use state variables during the process):
  • Free expansion into vacuum: pressure may be non-uniform during rapid expansion.
  • Explosive reaction in a gas mixture (e.g. petrol vapour + air ignited): temperature + pressure not uniform.
After some time, the system can settle to uniform P and T → mechanical + thermal equilibrium with surroundings.
Equation of state: a relation linking state variables (since they’re not all independent).
Ideal gas equation of state: PV = μRT P = pressure V = volume μ = amount (moles) R = universal gas constant T = absolute temperature
For a fixed amount of gas (μ fixed), only two independent variables (e.g. pick P & V, or T & V).
Isotherm: the P–V curve at a fixed temperature.
Real gases can have more complicated equations of state than PV = μRT.

Extensive and intensive state variables

Extensive variables: measure “how much stuff” → scale with amount of matter. Intensive variables: don’t depend on system size → don’t scale with amount of matter.
Quick test: split an equilibrium system into two equal halves.
  • If the value is unchanged in each half → intensive.
  • If the value is halved in each half → extensive.
Examples:
  • Extensive: internal energy U, volume V, total mass M
  • Intensive: pressure P, temperature T, density ρ
Consistency check: thermo equations should be dimensionally + “extensivity-consistent”. Example (first law form): ΔQ = ΔU + PΔV
  • ΔU is extensive
  • P is intensive, ΔV is extensive ⇒ PΔV is extensive
  • So ΔQ must be extensive (as written)
Exam caution: Q (heat) is not a state variable, but ΔQ for a process ∝ system mass, so it behaves like an extensive quantity in these checks.

Thermodynamic processes

Quasi-static process

Start with a gas in thermal + mechanical equilibrium with surroundings:
  • gas pressure = external pressure
  • gas temperature = surroundings’ temperature
If external pressure is suddenly reduced (e.g. lift weight from a movable piston), piston shoots out and the gas goes through non-equilibrium states.
  • In these intermediate states, P and T aren’t well-defined (can be non-uniform).
Same idea if there’s a finite temperature difference between gas and surroundings: heat transfer is rapid → system passes through non-equilibrium states, then settles to equilibrium (well-defined P and T equal to surroundings).
Why we use quasi-static: non-equilibrium states are messy to analyse. So we idealise a process where the system is in equilibrium at every stage → this is a quasi-static process.

Quasi-static (nearly static) process

Quasi-static process = idealised process done infinitely slowly, so the system moves through a continuous chain of equilibrium states.
  • P, T, V change so slowly that the system stays in thermal and mechanical equilibrium with surroundings at all times.
  • At every stage:
    • Pressure difference (system vs surroundings) is infinitesimal (system P differs from external P by an infinitesimal amount).
    • Temperature difference (system vs reservoir) is infinitesimal.
How to picture it (gas):
  • To move from (P, T) to (P′, T′) quasi-statically:
    • Tweak external pressure by a tiny amount, wait for pressure to equalise, repeat infinitely slowly until reaching P′.
    • For temperature: create an infinitesimal ΔT with a reservoir; use a chain of reservoirs with gradually changing temperatures from T to T′ until the system reaches T′.
Quasi-static processes are hypothetical. Real processes only approximate them if they’re slow enough and avoid things like fast piston motion and large temperature gradients. Unless told otherwise, we usually assume quasi-static so P and T are well-defined at each intermediate step.
If a process isn’t quasi-static, intermediate states may not be equilibrium states → formulas like δW = P dV (using system pressure “at each stage”) may fail.

Special thermodynamic processes (definitions)

  • Isothermal: T fixed throughout (T = constant).
  • Isobaric: P fixed (P = constant).
  • Isochoric: V fixed (V = constant).
  • Adiabatic: insulated → no heat flow (Q = 0).
Example (isothermal): gas in a metallic cylinder sitting in a big heat reservoir at fixed temperature. Heat exchange doesn’t noticeably change reservoir temperature because it has a very large heat capacity.

Isothermal process

Boyle’s law form from ideal gas equation

Isothermal (ideal gas): PV = constant
  • P = gas pressure
  • V = gas volume
Fixed mass + constant T ⇒ P ∝ 1/V → this is Boyle’s law.

Work done in isothermal change of an ideal gas

Ideal gas changes isothermally (temperature T) from (P1, V1) to (P2, V2). At some intermediate stage (pressure P), volume goes V → V + ΔV (ΔV tiny):
Tiny work: ΔW = P ΔV
  • ΔW = small work done by gas
  • P = pressure at that stage (quasi-static)
  • ΔV = small volume change
Let ΔV → 0 and add up over the whole path:
Isothermal work (ideal gas): W = ∫V1V2 P dV = μRT ∫V1V2 (dV/V) = μRT ln(V2/V1)   (Eq. 11.12)
  • W = work done by the gas
  • μ = moles of gas
  • R = universal gas constant
  • T = absolute temperature (constant here)
  • V1, V2 = initial, final volumes
  • ln = natural log
Reasoning step used: from PV = μRT ⇒ P = μRT/V, then pull constant μRT outside the integral.

Heat transfer and internal energy in isothermal process

  • For an ideal gas, U depends only on T.
  • Isothermal ⇒ T constant ⇒ ΔU = 0.
  • First Law ⇒ Q = W (heat in = work out by gas).
Sign convention from Eq. (11.12):
  • If V2 > V1 (expansion) ⇒ ln(V2/V1) > 0 ⇒ W > 0: gas absorbs heat + does work.
  • If V2 < V1 (compression) ⇒ W < 0: work done on gas + heat released.

Adiabatic process

Adiabatic process: system thermally insulated ⇒ no heat exchange → Q = 0.
First Law (ΔQ = ΔU + W, with W = work done by gas): if Q = 0 then work comes from internal energy. For an ideal gas, expansion makes T fall.

Adiabatic equation of state (ideal gas)

Adiabatic relation: PVγ = constant   (Eq. 11.13)
  • P = pressure
  • V = volume
  • γ = ratio of specific heats
Gamma: γ = Cp/Cv
  • Cp = specific heat (ordinary or molar) at constant pressure
  • Cv = specific heat (ordinary or molar) at constant volume
Two adiabatic states: P1V1γ = P2V2γ   (Eq. 11.14)
  • (P1, V1) = initial state
  • (P2, V2) = final state
P–V curve note: same endpoints ⇒ adiabatic curve is steeper than isothermal (as shown for two adiabats connecting two isotherms).

Work done in adiabatic change (ideal gas)

Ideal gas goes adiabatically from (P1, V1, T1) to (P2, V2, T2):
Work integral: W = ∫V1V2 P dV   (Eq. 11.15)
  • W = work done by the gas
  • P = pressure along the quasi-static adiabatic path
  • V1, V2 = initial and final volumes
Use PVγ = constant; take constant as P1V1γ (or P2V2γ). Result:
Adiabatic work (same result, 2 forms): W = (P1V1 − P2V2)/(γ − 1)   (Eq. 11.16 form) W = μR(T1 − T2)/(γ − 1)   (Eq. 11.16 form)
  • W = work done by the gas
  • P1, V1 = initial pressure, volume
  • P2, V2 = final pressure, volume
  • γ = Cp/Cv
  • μ = number of moles
  • R = universal gas constant
  • T1, T2 = initial and final temperatures
  • If W > 0 (adiabatic expansion) ⇒ from Eq. (11.16): T2 < T1 (T drops).
  • If W < 0 (adiabatic compression) ⇒ T2 > T1 (T rises).
Don’t mix these up:
  • Isothermal: ΔT = 0 but usually Q ≠ 0.
  • Adiabatic: Q = 0 but usually ΔT ≠ 0.

Isochoric (constant volume) process

Isochoric: V = constant.
  • dV = 0 ⇒ W = 0 (no work by/on gas).
  • First Law ⇒ heat goes fully into internal energy: Q = ΔU.
  • For a given heat input, ΔT depends on Cv (specific heat at constant volume).

Isobaric (constant pressure) process

Isobaric: P = fixed.
Work (isobaric): W = P(V2 − V1) = μR(T2 − T1)   (Eq. 11.17)
  • W = work done by the gas
  • P = constant pressure
  • V1, V2 = initial and final volumes
  • μ = number of moles
  • R = universal gas constant
  • T1, T2 = initial and final temperatures
  • T changes ⇒ U changes too (ideal gas: U depends on T).
  • Heat absorbed is split: part to raise U + part to do work.
  • For given heat supplied, ΔT depends on Cp (specific heat at constant pressure).

Cyclic process

Cyclic process: system returns to the initial state after one cycle.
U is a state variable ⇒ over a full cycle: ΔU = 0.

Second Law of Thermodynamics

Why we need a Second Law: The First Law (energy conservation) still “allows” many processes that are never seen in nature → we need an extra rule to block these energy-allowed but impossible processes. Example (book on a table): First Law alone would allow the table to cool on its own, turning internal energy into mechanical energy so the book jumps up (gain in GPE = that mechanical energy). This never happens → there’s another restriction. Second Law: The principle that forbids many First-Law-allowed phenomena; also sets hard limits on heat engines + refrigerators.
Device consequences:
  • Heat engine: efficiency can never be 1 (100%).
  • Refrigerator: COP can never be infinite.

Kelvin–Planck statement

Kelvin–Planck (Second Law): No process is possible whose only result is: absorb heat from a reservoir and convert all of it into work.

Clausius statement

Clausius (Second Law): No process is possible whose only result is: heat transfer from a colder body to a hotter body.
Equivalence: Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements are fully equivalent (either one ⇒ the other).

Reversible and Irreversible Processes

General idea of reversibility

System goes from initial state i to final state f, absorbs heat Q from surroundings, and does work W on surroundings. Reversibility test: Can we run it backward so that system + surroundings both return to their original states, with no other effect anywhere?
Key observation: Most natural (spontaneous) processes cannot be exactly reversed → they are irreversible.

Examples of irreversible processes (must know)

  • Heat flow within an object: Vessel base on an oven is hotter; after removal, heat flows hot → cold until uniform. Reverse (cold parts heating the base on their own) doesn’t happen; would violate Second Law.
  • Free expansion of a gas: Irreversible.
  • Combustion / explosive chemical reactions: Petrol–air ignited by a spark can’t be reversed back into reactants just by “undoing steps”.
  • Diffusion: Cooking gas leaks into a room and spreads; it won’t spontaneously gather back into the cylinder.
  • Stirring + dissipation: Stirring a liquid (in thermal contact with a reservoir) turns work into heat, increasing reservoir internal energy. Exact reverse would convert heat entirely into work → forbidden by Second Law.
Exam trap: “Irreversible” ≠ “practically hard”. It means impossible to reverse while restoring both system and surroundings to their initial states with no other change anywhere.

Why irreversibility occurs

Main causes:
  1. Non-equilibrium states: Many processes (e.g. free expansion, explosive reactions) push the system through non-equilibrium states.
  2. Dissipative effects: Friction, viscosity, etc. convert mechanical energy → internal energy (heat), e.g.:
  • Moving body stops due to friction: mechanical energy becomes heat in body + floor.
  • Rotating blade in liquid slows due to viscosity: mechanical energy becomes internal energy of the liquid.
Dissipation is everywhere and can only be reduced, not removed → most real processes are irreversible.
Key conclusion: Irreversibility is the normal case in nature.

Definition and conditions for a reversible process

Reversible process (definition): A process (i → f) is reversible if it can be reversed so system + surroundings both return to their original states, with no other change anywhere in the universe.
Reversible = idealised: A process is reversible only if:
  • Quasi-static: system stays in equilibrium with surroundings at every stage.
  • No dissipative effects: no friction, no viscosity losses, etc.
Example: Quasi-static isothermal expansion of an ideal gas in a cylinder with a frictionless piston.

Why reversibility is central to thermodynamics

Thermodynamics asks (among other things): how efficiently can heat → work be done? Second Law blocks a perfect (100%) engine. To get the maximum possible efficiency between reservoirs at T1 and T2, we imagine an engine built from ideal reversible processes. Result: A reversible engine has maximum efficiency. Any engine with irreversibility has lower efficiency than this limit.

Carnot Engine

Goal and historical note

Given a hot reservoir at T1 and a cold reservoir at T2, we ask:
  • What’s the maximum possible efficiency for an engine running between them?
  • Which cycle actually reaches that maximum?
Sadi Carnot (1824): first correct analysis of this idea, before modern heat/thermo language was fully set up.

Why the Carnot engine must be reversible

Efficiency vs irreversibility: irreversible effects (dissipation, friction, finite ΔT heat flow) waste work → lower efficiency. So the “best possible” engine must be reversible (quasi-static + non-dissipative).

Choice of processes in the Carnot cycle

Quasi-static heat transfer rule (thermal contact): if heat flows across a finite temperature difference (system ≠ reservoir), the process is not quasi-static → not reversible. So, in a reversible engine:
  • Heat must be absorbed from the hot reservoir isothermally at T1 (system at T1 too).
  • Heat must be rejected to the cold reservoir isothermally at T2 (system at T2 too).
That gives two clear steps:
  • Isothermal at T1: absorb heat Q1 from the hot reservoir.
  • Isothermal at T2: release heat Q2 to the cold reservoir.
To close the cycle, the working substance must go from T1 → T2 and then T2 → T1 using processes that stay reversible while still operating with only these two reservoirs.
Why the other two steps must be adiabatic:
  • If we used a non-adiabatic path (e.g. isochoric heating/cooling) to change temperature from T1 to T2, then to keep it reversible we’d need contact with a whole sequence of reservoirs with temperatures spanning T2 to T1 (to avoid finite ΔT at every moment).
  • But the engine is defined to run between only two reservoirs: T1 and T2.
  • Therefore the temperature-change legs must be reversible adiabatic processes (no heat exchange with any reservoir).
Definition: a reversible heat engine operating between two temperatures is a Carnot engine. Carnot cycle (as set up here): two isothermal processes (at T1 and T2) + two reversible adiabatic processes linking them, making one full cycle. Working substance example: ideal gas (as in the usual Carnot-cycle diagram).
Exam tip: For truly reversible heat transfer, T(system) = T(reservoir) (no finite ΔT). That’s exactly why Carnot uses isothermals for heat exchange and adiabatics for changing temperature when only two reservoirs are allowed.

Carnot Cycle (Ideal Gas Working Substance): Steps, Work, and Efficiency

Carnot cycle = ideal (reversible) heat-engine cycle: 2 isothermal + 2 adiabatic processes. Working substance here: ideal gas.

Step 1 → 2: Isothermal Expansion at Hot Reservoir (Temperature T1)

State: (P1, V1, T1) → (P2, V2, T1). Heat absorbed from hot reservoir: Q1. Isothermal ideal gas ⇒ ΔU = 0 ⇒ Q1 = W1→2 (work done by gas).
(11.18) Isothermal work / heat absorbed (ideal gas) W1→2 = Q1 = μ R T1 ln(V2/V1) W1→2: work done by gas in 1→2 Q1: heat absorbed from hot reservoir μ: moles of gas R: universal gas constant T1: hot reservoir temperature (constant in 1→2) V1, V2: start/end volumes ln: natural log

Step 2 → 3: Adiabatic Expansion (Temperature Drops from T1 to T2)

State: (P2, V2, T1) → (P3, V3, T2). Adiabatic ⇒ Q = 0. Gas does work using its internal energy ⇒ T decreases.
(11.19) Work done by ideal gas in adiabatic expansion 2→3 W2→3 = μ R (T1 − T2) / (γ − 1) W2→3: work done by gas in 2→3 μ: moles R: universal gas constant T1, T2: initial/final temperatures γ: specific heat ratio, γ = Cp/Cv

Step 3 → 4: Isothermal Compression at Cold Reservoir (Temperature T2)

State: (P3, V3, T2) → (P4, V4, T2). Heat released to cold reservoir: Q2. Isothermal ideal gas ⇒ ΔU = 0 ⇒ |heat rejected| = |work on gas|. Sign-language used here: W3→4 is work done on the gas, and equals Q2 (heat released).
(11.20) Isothermal compression work / heat rejected (ideal gas) W3→4 = Q2 = μ R T2 ln(V3/V4) W3→4: work done on gas in 3→4 Q2: heat rejected to cold reservoir μ: moles R: universal gas constant T2: cold reservoir temperature (constant in 3→4) V3, V4: start/end volumes (step 3→4)

Step 4 → 1: Adiabatic Compression (Temperature Rises from T2 to T1)

State: (P4, V4, T2) → (P1, V1, T1). Adiabatic compression ⇒ Q = 0. Work done on gas increases internal energy ⇒ T rises back to T1.
(11.21) Work done on ideal gas in adiabatic compression 4→1 W4→1 = μ R (T1 − T2) / (γ − 1) W4→1: work done on gas in 4→1 μ: moles R: universal gas constant T1, T2: final/initial temperatures γ: specific heat ratio, γ = Cp/Cv
Exam sign-convention trap: Here, W1→2, W2→3 = work done by gas, but W3→4, W4→1 = work done on gas. So net work output must subtract the compression-step works.

Total Work Done per Cycle

Net work output = (expansion works) − (compression works), as written in the text:
(11.22) Net work done in one Carnot cycle W = W1→2 + W2→3 − W3→4 − W4→1 = μ R T1 ln(V2/V1) − μ R T2 ln(V3/V4) W: net work done by gas per cycle (area enclosed on P–V diagram) T1, T2: hot/cold reservoir temperatures V1, V2, V3, V4: volumes at states 1–4

Efficiency of Carnot Engine and Its Simplification

Efficiency = useful work out / heat absorbed from hot reservoir:
(11.23) Efficiency in terms of heat and volumes η = W/Q1 = 1 − Q2/Q1 = 1 − (T2 ln(V3/V4)) / (T1 ln(V2/V1)) η: efficiency W: net work per cycle Q1: heat absorbed at T1 Q2: heat rejected at T2
To simplify: use adiabatic relations for steps 2→3 and 4→1.
(11.24) Adiabatic relation for step 2→3 T1 V2γ−1 = T2 V3γ−1 ⇒ V2/V3 = (T2/T1)1/(γ−1) T1, T2: temperatures at ends of adiabatic expansion V2, V3: volumes at states 2 and 3 γ: Cp/Cv
(11.25) Adiabatic relation for step 4→1 T2 V4γ−1 = T1 V1γ−1 ⇒ V1/V4 = (T2/T1)1/(γ−1) T1, T2: temperatures at ends of adiabatic compression V4, V1: volumes at states 4 and 1 γ: Cp/Cv
Compare RHS of (11.24) and (11.25):
(11.26) Volume-ratio equality (used in Carnot η derivation) V3/V4 = V2/V1 V2/V1: volume ratio in isothermal expansion at T1 V3/V4: volume ratio in isothermal compression at T2
Use (11.26) in (11.23) ⇒ logs cancel ⇒ standard Carnot efficiency:
(11.27) Carnot engine efficiency η = 1 − T2/T1 η: max possible efficiency for any engine between T1 and T2 T1: absolute hot-reservoir temperature T2: absolute cold-reservoir temperature
Carnot η depends ONLY on reservoir temperatures (T1, T2) — not on the working substance. Bigger T1 and/or smaller T2 ⇒ higher η, but η < 1 unless T2 = 0 K (not achievable).

Reversible Nature: Carnot Engine and Reversible Refrigerator

Reversible engine: cycle can be run backward step-by-step with no net change to system + surroundings (except heat/work directions reverse).
  • The Carnot engine is reversible (each step can be reversed).
  • Reverse the cycle ⇒ reversible refrigerator:
    • Heat Q2 is taken from cold reservoir at T2,
    • Work W is done on the system,
    • Heat Q1 is delivered to hot reservoir at T1.

Carnot’s Theorem: Maximum Efficiency and Independence from Working Substance

Carnot’s theorem (big result):
  • (a) For two fixed temperatures T1 (source) and T2 (sink), no heat engine can have efficiency greater than a Carnot engine working between the same T’s.
  • (b) Carnot efficiency is independent of the working substance.

Proof Idea for (a): Coupling an Irreversible Engine with a Reversible Refrigerator

Take two devices between the same hot reservoir (source) and cold reservoir (sink):
  • I: an irreversible engine (heat engine).
  • R: a reversible (Carnot) engine run backwards (refrigerator).
Couple them so that:
  • I absorbs Q1 from the source, gives work W′, rejects Q1 − W′ to the sink.
  • R sends the same heat Q1 back to the source, pulls Q2 from the sink, and needs input work W = Q1 − Q2.
Assume (to get a contradiction) that ηR < ηI. For the same Q1 ⇒ W < W′.
  • Q2 = Q1 − W. Since W < W′ ⇒ Q2 > Q1 − W′.
  • Net heat taken from the sink by (I + R): (heat taken from sink by R) − (heat delivered to sink by I) = (Q1 − W) − (Q1 − W′) = W′ − W .
  • Net work output of the coupled setup = W′ − W (I produces W′, R consumes W).
This would mean: take heat (W′ − W) from the cold reservoir and convert it completely into work, with no other effect. That breaks the Kelvin–Planck statement of the 2nd law. So ηI > ηR is impossible ⇒ no engine beats a Carnot engine.

Consequence for (b): Independence from Working Substance

Same “coupling” logic: one reversible engine (using substance A) can’t be more efficient than another reversible engine (using substance B) between the same T1, T2. Hence the maximum efficiency depends only on T1 and T2, not on the material/system.
Exam phrasing: “Carnot engine is the only reversible engine possible between two reservoirs” = for given T1, T2, every reversible engine must have Carnot efficiency (same max η), i.e. it matches Carnot performance.

Universal Relation in a Carnot Cycle and Thermodynamic Temperature Scale

Since η is universal (only temperature-dependent), the reversible heat-exchange ratio in a Carnot cycle is also universal.
(11.28) Universal heat–temperature relation (reversible/Carnot cycle) Q1/Q2 = T1/T2 Q1: heat absorbed isothermally from hot reservoir at T1 Q2: heat rejected isothermally to cold reservoir at T2 T1, T2: reservoir temps on a universal thermodynamic scale
Thermodynamic (universal) temperature scale: Because Q1/Q2 for a reversible Carnot cycle doesn’t depend on the working substance, Eq. (11.28) can define a temperature scale not based on any particular material’s properties.
If the working substance is an ideal gas, this universal temperature matches the ideal-gas temperature scale defined earlier.

Chapter Summary (Given in Textbook Portion)

Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

Zeroth law: If system A is in thermal equilibrium with system C, and system B is also in thermal equilibrium with system C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This is what gives meaning to the idea of temperature.

Internal Energy, Heat, and Work

Internal energy (U): Total microscopic energy = (molecular) kinetic + potential energies inside the system. It does not include the kinetic energy of the system moving as a whole. Heat and work are the two ways energy is transferred into/out of a system.
  • Heat: energy transfer only because of a temperature difference (system ↔ surroundings).
  • Work: energy transfer by any other mechanism (e.g. piston motion; raising/lowering a weight via piston).

First Law of Thermodynamics

First law (energy conservation for thermodynamic systems): once heat + work transfers are counted, the energy balance is:
First law of thermodynamics ΔQ = ΔU + ΔW ΔQ: heat supplied to the system ΔU: change in internal energy of the system ΔW: work done by the system

Specific Heat Capacity, Molar Specific Heat Capacity, Equipartition Result for Solids, and Calorie

Specific heat capacity (s) s = (1/m) (ΔQ/ΔT) s: specific heat capacity of the substance m: mass of the substance ΔQ: heat required ΔT: temperature change produced
Molar specific heat capacity (C) C = (1/μ) (ΔQ/ΔT) C: molar specific heat capacity μ: number of moles ΔQ: heat required ΔT: temperature change
Equipartition result for a solid (ordinary temperatures): C = 3R (matches experiment fairly well at ordinary temperatures).
Calorie (old heat unit): heat needed to raise 1 g of water from 14.5 °C to 15.5 °C.
Unit conversion 1 cal = 4.186 J cal: calorie J: joule

Ideal Gas Specific Heats Relation

Relation between molar specific heats of an ideal gas Cp − Cv = R Cp: molar specific heat at constant pressure Cv: molar specific heat at constant volume R: universal gas constant

State Variables, Path Dependence, and Equation of State

State variables: describe an equilibrium state; depend only on the state, not the path taken to reach it. Examples: P, V, T, m. Heat and work are not state variables → they depend on the process/path. Equation of state: an equation linking state variables (e.g. ideal gas law).
Ideal gas equation of state PV = μRT P: pressure V: volume μ: number of moles R: universal gas constant T: absolute temperature

Quasi-static Process

Quasi-static process: done infinitely slowly so the system stays in thermal + mechanical equilibrium with surroundings throughout. So, pressure and temperature differences between system and environment are only infinitesimal.

Isothermal Expansion of an Ideal Gas: Heat Absorbed Equals Work Done

Isothermal expansion (ideal gas) from V1 to V2 at temperature T Q = W = μRT ln(V2/V1) Q: heat absorbed W: work done by the gas μ: moles R: universal gas constant T: constant absolute temperature V1, V2: initial and final volumes

Adiabatic Process of an Ideal Gas: PVγ Constant and Work Expression

Adiabatic condition (ideal gas) PVγ = constant P: pressure V: volume γ: Cp/Cv
Work done in adiabatic change (ideal gas) from (P1,V1,T1) to (P2,V2,T2) W = μR (T1 − T2) / (γ − 1) W: work done by the gas in the adiabatic process μ: number of moles R: universal gas constant T1, T2: initial and final temperatures γ: ratio of specific heats, Cp/Cv
Common exam error: writing Carnot efficiency as 1 − T1/T2. Correct: η = 1 − T2/T1, where T1 is the hot reservoir temperature.

Second Law of Thermodynamics

Second Law of Thermodynamics: adds extra restrictions beyond the First Law; it eliminates some “energy-conserving” processes that still cannot happen in reality.

Kelvin–Planck Statement

Kelvin–Planck statement: No process can have the sole result of absorbing heat from a reservoir and converting all of it into work.
This rules out a cyclic heat engine with 100% efficiency that takes heat from only one reservoir.

Clausius Statement

Clausius statement: No process can have the sole result of moving heat from a colder body to a hotter body.
This rules out a refrigerator/heat pump that moves heat cold → hot with no external work input.

Immediate Consequences (Exam Statements)

  • No heat engine can have efficiency η = 1.
  • No refrigerator can have coefficient of performance α = ∞.
In Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements, “sole result” is the key phrase: it means “nothing else changes”. If something else happens (e.g. work input), it’s not forbidden by that wording.

Reversible and Irreversible Processes

Reversible process: can be reversed so that system + surroundings both return to their original states, with no other change anywhere in the universe.
  • Natural spontaneous processes are irreversible.
  • An ideal reversible process is quasi-static and has no dissipative effects (no friction, viscosity, etc.).
Don’t mix up “slow (quasi-static)” with “reversible”. Quasi-static is necessary but not sufficient. Dissipation (friction/viscosity/turbulence/finite temperature gradients) → makes the process irreversible.

Carnot Engine and Carnot Cycle

Carnot engine: ideal reversible heat engine working between two temperatures — T1 (source) and T2 (sink). Carnot cycle: made of 2 isothermal processes joined by 2 adiabatic processes.

Efficiency of a Carnot Engine

η = 1 − (T2/T1) (Carnot engine) η = efficiency (no unit) T1 = source absolute temperature (K) T2 = sink absolute temperature (K)
Carnot theorem (exam line): No engine operating between two given temperatures can have efficiency greater than a Carnot engine operating between the same two temperatures.
Use Kelvin (absolute T) in η = 1 − T2/T1. Plugging in °C directly = wrong.

Sign Conventions for Heat and Work

  • Q > 0: heat added to the system.
  • Q < 0: heat removed from the system.
  • W > 0: work done by the system.
  • W < 0: work done on the system.
Biggest mistakes = mixing sign conventions. Here, W > 0 means system does work on surroundings (e.g., expansion).

Key Thermodynamic Quantities: Symbols, Units, Remarks

Coefficient of Volume Expansion

Coefficient of volume expansion: symbol αv, unit K−1, dimensions [K−1].
Given relation: αv = 3 αl.

Heat Supplied to a System

Heat supplied: symbol ΔQ, unit J, dimensions [M L2 T−2].
Q is not a state variable → depends on the path (process), not just initial & final states.

Specific Heat Capacity

Specific heat capacity: symbol s, unit J kg−1 K−1, dimensions [L2 T−2 K−1].
“Heat capacity” is not always one fixed number; it can change with the process (constant P vs constant V).

Thermal Conductivity (Conduction Law Form Used Here)

Thermal conductivity: symbol K, unit (as given) J s−1 K−1, dimensions [M L T−3 K−1].
H = − K A (dT/dx) H = heat flow rate (heat current) K = thermal conductivity A = area normal to heat flow dT/dx = temperature gradient along x − sign: heat flows hot → cold (opposite to +dT/dx).
Don’t drop the “−” sign: it tells the direction (heat goes down the temperature gradient).

Points to Ponder (Conceptual Clarifications)

Temperature vs Motion of the Centre of Mass

Temperature relates to average internal energy, not KE of the body’s centre of mass. So a fast bullet is not hotter just because it’s moving fast.

Meaning of “Equilibrium” in Thermodynamics vs Mechanics

  • Thermodynamic equilibrium: state variables (P, V, T, etc.) do not change with time.
  • Mechanical equilibrium: net external force and net torque are zero.

Microscopic Constituents in Thermodynamic Equilibrium

Even at thermodynamic equilibrium, microscopic particles are not in mechanical equilibrium — they still move randomly and collide.

Heat Capacity Depends on the Process

Heat capacity depends on the process: heating at constant volume vs constant pressure gives different heat capacities.

Isothermal Quasi-static Process: Heat Exchange with “Same Temperature”

In an isothermal quasi-static process, the system can absorb/release heat even though the gas temperature matches the reservoir temperature at every stage. This works because there is an infinitesimal temperature difference between system and reservoir, allowing heat flow while keeping the process effectively isothermal.
Heat transfer needs ΔT. In quasi-static isothermal change, ΔT is infinitesimally small (not exactly zero).
