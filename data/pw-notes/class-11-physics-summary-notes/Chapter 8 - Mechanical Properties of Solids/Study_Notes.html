Chapter 8 : Mechanical Properties of Solids

8.1 Introduction

Rigid body (idealised): A hard solid object having a definite shape and size, assumed not to deform under applied forces. Reality: No solid is perfectly rigid. With sufficiently large external force, even apparently rigid objects (e.g., a steel bar) can be stretched, compressed, or bent → i.e., deformed.
DeformationChange in shape and/or size due to an applied force
ElasticityTendency to regain original size & shape after force is removed
Elastic deformationDeformation disappears after unloading (returns to original configuration)
PlasticityPermanent deformation; no gross tendency to regain original shape
Plastic substancesClose to ideal plastics: putty, mud (permanent deformation)
Engineering relevance: Elastic behaviour is crucial in design (buildings, bridges, automobiles, ropeways, etc.). Many “why/how” design questions begin with understanding deformation under simple loads, e.g.:
  • Designing structures that are light yet sufficiently strong (airplanes, artificial limbs).
  • Why certain shapes are chosen (e.g., railway track cross-section like I).
  • Why some materials are brittle (glass) while others (brass) are not.
This chapter therefore studies elastic behaviour and mechanical properties of solids to address such questions.
Exam trap: “Rigid body” is an approximation used for simpler analysis; in real materials, deformation occurs when forces are large enough. Also: “Elastic” does not mean “easily stretchable”; it means “returns to original state after unloading.”

8.2 Stress and Strain

When deforming forces act on a body while it remains in static equilibrium, the body deforms by an amount depending on:
  • Nature of material
  • Magnitude of deforming force
Even if deformation is not visible, it can still be present.
Deforming force applied
→
Internal restoring force develops
→
Static equilibrium (if balanced)
Restoring force: When a body is subjected to a deforming force, an internal restoring force develops in the body. Direction and magnitude: Restoring force is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the applied force (for equilibrium under static loading).
Stress: Restoring force per unit area developed inside a body due to deformation.
Stress (normal stress): Stress = F/A (Eq. 8.1)
  • F = force applied normal (perpendicular) to the cross-sectional area
  • A = cross-sectional area over which the force acts
  • Unit (SI): N m−2 = pascal (Pa)
  • Dimensions: [ M L−1 T−2 ]
Key idea: Stress depends on how force is distributed: the same force gives larger stress if area is smaller.
Ways a solid may change dimensions under external force:
Longitudinal deformationChange in length due to tensile/compressive loading
Shear deformationChange in shape at nearly constant volume due to tangential forces
Hydraulic compressionUniform compression from all sides → volume change without shape change

8.2.1 Longitudinal (Tensile/Compressive) Stress and Longitudinal Strain

Tensile stressRestoring force/area when a body (e.g., cylinder) is stretched by equal forces applied normal to cross-sectional area (elongation)
Compressive stressRestoring force/area when a body is compressed by applied forces (shortening)
Longitudinal stressTensile or compressive stress (acts along length → change in length)
Longitudinal strain: Ratio of change in length to original length.
Longitudinal strain: (ΔL)/L (Eq. 8.2)
  • ΔL = change in length (elongation or compression)
  • L = original length of the body
Exam trap: Strain is a ratio (change/original), so it is dimensionless.

8.2.2 Tangential (Shearing) Stress and Shearing Strain

Shearing (tangential) stress: Restoring force per unit area developed when two equal and opposite forces act parallel to the cross-sectional area, producing a tendency for one face to slide relative to the opposite face. Physical effect: Relative displacement occurs between opposite faces; shape changes.
Shearing strain: Ratio of relative displacement between faces to the length (height) of the body.
Shearing strain: (Δx)/L = tan θ (Eq. 8.3)
  • Δx = relative lateral displacement between opposite faces
  • L = length (height) of the cylinder/body between the faces
  • θ = angular displacement from the original (vertical) position
Small-angle approximation: Usually θ is very small, so tan θ ≈ θ (θ in radians for numerical equality). Given example: at θ = 10°, θ and tan θ differ by only about 1%.
For small θ: Shearing strain = tan θ ≈ θ (Eq. 8.4)
  • θ = small angular displacement (use radians when substituting numerically)
Everyday visualisation of shear: Pressing a book and pushing it horizontally produces shearing deformation (layers tend to slide).
Exam trap: Do not confuse direction of forces:
  • Normal stress: force ⟂ area (tensile/compressive)
  • Shear stress: force ∥ area (tangential)
Also: tan θ ≈ θ is valid only for small θ; for larger angles, use tan θ explicitly.

8.2.3 Hydraulic Stress (Uniform Pressure) and Volumetric Strain

Hydraulic compression / hydraulic stress: When a solid body (e.g., a sphere) is placed in a fluid under high pressure, it is compressed uniformly on all sides. Nature of forces: The fluid exerts force perpendicular to the surface at every point. Result: There is a decrease in volume (compression), but no change in shape.
Volumetric strain: (ΔV)/V (8.5)
  • ΔV = change in volume (negative for compression)
  • V = original volume
Key distinction: Under hydraulic stress, deformation is purely volumetric: volume changes but shape does not.
Magnitude relation: Hydraulic stress (internal restoring force per unit area) is equal in magnitude to the hydraulic pressure (applied force per unit area).

Hooke’s Law

For small deformations, stress and strain are proportional. This proportionality is called Hooke’s law.
stress ∝ strain stress = k × strain (Eq. 8.6)
  • k = proportionality constant, called the modulus of elasticity (for the relevant type of stress/strain)
Nature of Hooke’s law: It is empirical (based on experiments). It is valid for most materials for small deformations, but some materials do not show a linear stress–strain relation.

Interpretation of a typical metal stress–strain curve (key points O, A, B, D, E)

Region O → A (linear region):
  • Graph is linear ⇒ Hooke’s law holds (stress ∝ strain).
  • If load is removed, body regains original dimensions.
  • Material behaves as an elastic body.
Region A → B (non-linear but still elastic):
  • Stress and strain are not proportional.
  • Yet, on removing load, body still returns to original dimensions.
  • Point B = yield point (also called elastic limit).
  • Corresponding stress at B = yield strength (σy).
Beyond yield (B → D and unloading from within this region):
  • If stress exceeds σy, strain increases rapidly even for small increase in stress (curve portion between B and D shows this).
  • If load is removed at some point C between B and D, body does not regain original dimensions.
  • Even when stress becomes zero, strain remains non-zero ⇒ permanent set.
  • This is plastic deformation.
Ultimate strength and fracture:
  • Point D = ultimate tensile strength (σu).
  • Beyond D: additional strain occurs even if applied force is reduced.
  • Fracture occurs at point E.
Brittlepoints D and E are close
Ductilepoints D and E are far apart

Non-metallic/biological examples: elastomers and non-Hookean elasticity

  • Stress–strain behaviour differs strongly by material.
Rubbercan be stretched several times its original length and still returns to original shape (large elastic strain possible)
Elastic tissue of aortaelastic region is very large, but it does not obey Hooke’s law over most of the region (non-linear elasticity); no well-defined plastic region
Elastomers: Substances (e.g., rubber, aorta tissue) that can be stretched to cause large strains and still show elastic recovery over a large range.

Elastic Moduli

Modulus of elasticity: In the proportional region within the elastic limit (region OA), the ratio stress/strain is (approximately) constant and is a characteristic property of the material.
The proportional region within the elastic limit is crucial in structural and manufacturing engineering designs, because predictable (linear) response is required.

Young’s Modulus

Observation: For a given material, the magnitude of strain produced is the same whether the stress is tensile or compressive (within elastic limits). Young’s modulus (Y): Ratio of longitudinal stress (tensile/compressive) to longitudinal strain.
Young’s modulus: Y = σ / ε (Eq. 8.7)
  • Y = Young’s modulus (Pa)
  • σ = longitudinal stress (N m−2)
  • ε = longitudinal strain (dimensionless)
Using σ = F/A and ε = ΔL/L: Y = (F/A) / (ΔL/L) = (F × L) / (A × ΔL) (Eq. 8.8)
  • F = applied force (N)
  • A = cross-sectional area (m2)
  • L = original length (m)
  • ΔL = change in length / elongation (m)
  • Units: Since strain is dimensionless, Young’s modulus has the same units as stress: N m−2 = Pascal (Pa).
  • Material comparison: Metals generally have large Young’s moduli → large force needed for small strain.

Interpreting “more elastic” using Young’s modulus

  • To produce the same strain in wires of equal cross-sectional area, the required force is proportional to Y.
Given example comparison for 0.1 cm2 wire area and strain 0.1%:
Steel2000 N
Aluminium690 N
Brass900 N
Copper1100 N
  • Conclusion: Steel is more elastic than copper, brass, aluminium because it has higher Young’s modulus (smaller strain for the same stress).
  • Engineering implication: steel preferred for heavy-duty machines and structural designs.
  • Materials like wood, bone, concrete, glass have comparatively small Young’s moduli.
Exam tip: “More elastic” in physics typically means larger Young’s modulus (greater stiffness), not “stretches more.” Rubber stretches more but does not necessarily have a large Y.
Now find load using copper relation:
W = (A × Yc × ΔLc) / Lc
  • W = load (N)
  • A = πr2 = cross-sectional area (m2)
  • Yc = Young’s modulus of copper
  • ΔLc = elongation of copper wire
  • Lc = original length of copper wire
Common mistake: For wires in series, students often assume same strain in both. Correct is: same force (and stress if areas equal), but extensions differ due to different Y and lengths.
How to approach (method the example is designed to test):
  • Compression is a longitudinal deformation, so use Young’s modulus relation: ΔL = (F L)/(A Y) or equivalently ΔL = (stress × L)/Y.
  • “Extra load” indicates only the load due to the balanced group (280 kg) is additionally supported by the femurs (the performer’s own weight is separate).
  • Since there are two femurs, the extra force is shared: force on each femur = (extra weight)/2.
  • Area of each femur A = πr2 using effective radius.

Shear Modulus (Modulus of Rigidity)

Definition and Meaning

Shear modulus G is the ratio of shearing stress to the corresponding shearing strain. It is also called the modulus of rigidity.
Think: pushing the top of a book sideways while the bottom stays stuck → it “slants” without (ideally) changing volume.

Equations for Shear Modulus

Shear modulus (from displacement form) G = (shearing stress)/(shearing strain) = (F/A)/(Δx/L) = (F L)/(A Δx)    (Eq. 8.10)
  • G = shear modulus (Pa)
  • F = tangential (shearing) force (N)
  • A = area of the face on which force acts (m2)
  • Δx = lateral displacement of the top surface relative to bottom (m)
  • L = perpendicular separation between the two sheared faces (thickness/height) (m)
Shear modulus (from shear angle) G = (F/A)/θ = F/(A θ)    (Eq. 8.11)
  • θ = shear strain expressed as shear angle (radians) (for small deformations, θ ≈ tanθ = Δx/L)
Shearing stress relation σs = G θ    (Eq. 8.12)
  • σs = shearing stress (Pa)
  • G = shear modulus (Pa)
  • θ = shear strain (radians)
  • SI unit of G is N m−2 (Pa).
  • In general, G is less than Young’s modulus Y.
  • For most materials: G ≈ Y/3 (use only when approximate relation is allowed).

Bulk Modulus

Definition

When a body is under hydraulic stress (uniform pressure), its volume changes, producing volume strain (ΔV/V). The ratio of hydraulic stress to volume strain is the bulk modulus, denoted by B.
Like squeezing a sealed syringe: pressure rises and volume drops (shape mostly stays the same).
Bulk modulus B = − p/(ΔV/V)    (Eq. 8.12)
  • B = bulk modulus (Pa)
  • p = applied pressure (hydraulic stress) (Pa)
  • ΔV = change in volume (m3)
  • V = original volume (m3)
  • Negative sign: if pressure increases (p positive), volume decreases (ΔV negative). Hence B is positive for an equilibrium system.
  • SI unit of bulk modulus is the same as pressure: N m−2 (Pa).

Compressibility

Compressibility k is the reciprocal of bulk modulus. It equals the fractional change in volume per unit increase in pressure.
Compressibility k = 1/B = − (1/Δp) × (ΔV/V)    (Eq. 8.13)
  • k = compressibility (Pa−1)
  • B = bulk modulus (Pa)
  • Δp = increase in pressure (Pa)
  • ΔV/V = volume strain (dimensionless)

Relative Magnitudes: Solids, Liquids, Gases

  • From typical data: B(solids) ≫ B(liquids) ≫ B(gases).
  • Equivalently: k(gases) ≫ k(liquids) ≫ k(solids).
Interpretation of compressibility differences:
Solidsleast compressible (tight coupling between neighbouring atoms)
Liquidsmore compressible than solids (less tightly bound)
Gasesmost compressible (poorly coupled; varies strongly with pressure and temperature)
Extra factGases are about a million times more compressible than solids

Poisson’s Ratio

Lateral Strain and Longitudinal Strain

  • Lateral strain: strain perpendicular to the applied force.
  • Longitudinal strain: strain along the direction of applied force.
  • Within elastic limit, Simon Poisson stated: lateral strain ∝ longitudinal strain.

Definition of Poisson’s Ratio

Poisson’s ratio = (lateral strain)/(longitudinal strain) for a stretched wire.
For a wire:
DiameterOriginal diameter = d; contraction in diameter = Δd → lateral strain = Δd/d
LengthOriginal length = L; elongation = ΔL → longitudinal strain = ΔL/L
Poisson’s ratio Poisson’s ratio = (Δd/d)/(ΔL/L) = (Δd/ΔL) × (L/d)
  • Δd/d = lateral strain (dimensionless)
  • ΔL/L = longitudinal strain (dimensionless)
  • Poisson’s ratio is dimensionless (no units)
  • Poisson’s ratio depends only on the nature of material.
  • Typical values: steel ≈ 0.28 to 0.30; aluminium alloys ≈ 0.33.

Elastic Energy Density

This work W is stored as elastic potential energy U. Hence elastic potential energy per unit volume (energy density) u is:
Elastic potential energy per unit volume u = (1/2) σ ε    (Eq. 8.14)
  • u = elastic energy density (J m−3)
  • σ = stress (Pa)
  • ε = strain (dimensionless)
Exam tip: Do not confuse total energy U with energy density u. Use volume = A L to move between them.

Engineering Applications of Elasticity: Design Safety, Bending of Beams, Buckling

Steel rope for a crane: choosing cross-sectional area using yield strength

A load-lifting rope must be designed so that it does not deform permanently. Hence the stress in the rope must not exceed the elastic limit; in practice we use the material’s yield strength (stress at which plastic flow begins) as the limiting stress.
Minimum area condition (no yielding): A ≥ W/σy = Mg/σy   (Eq. 8.15)
  • A = required cross-sectional area of rope (m²)
  • W = load (weight) = Mg (N)
  • M = mass being lifted (kg)
  • g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2)
  • σy = yield strength of material (N m−2)
Given data used in the example:
  • lifting capacity = 10 tonnes = 10 metric tons
  • 1 metric ton = 1000 kg ⇒ M = 104 kg
  • Mild steel yield strength σy ≈ 300 × 106 N m−2
Worked example (designing the crane rope): A ≥ Mg/σy = (104 kg × 9.8 m s−2)/(300 × 106 N m−2) = 3.3 × 10−4 m² For a circular rope, A = πr² ⇒ r ≈ √(A/π) ≈ √(3.3 × 10−4/π) ≈ 1 cm (approx.)
Margin of safety: In real designs, a large safety factor (about a factor of 10 in load) is used. Therefore a thicker rope is recommended: radius ≈ 3 cm.
Why ropes are braided (many thin wires): A single solid wire of radius ~3 cm would be almost rigid like a rod. Braiding many thin wires gives flexibility, ease of manufacture, and strength.
Exam tip: When asked “how thick should it be?”, use yield strength/elastic limit (not ultimate tensile strength) and then apply the safety factor if stated/expected.

Bending of beams under load: central load on a simply supported beam

Bridges and buildings commonly use beams. A key design requirement is controlling bending (sagging/deflection) so that the beam neither bends excessively nor breaks.
Deflection (sag) of a beam supported near ends and loaded at centre: δ = W l3 / (4 b d3 Y)   (Eq. 8.16)
  • δ = sag/deflection at the centre (m)
  • W = central load (N)
  • l = length/span of the beam between supports (m)
  • b = breadth (width) of rectangular cross-section (m)
  • d = depth (thickness/height) of beam cross-section (m)
  • Y = Young’s modulus of the beam material (N m−2)
Design implications from Eq. (8.16):
  • To reduce bending for a given load, choose material with large Young’s modulus Y.
  • For a given material, increasing depth d is far more effective than increasing breadth b, because δ ∝ d−3 but δ ∝ b−1.
  • Keep span l as small as possible because δ ∝ l3.

Buckling and choice of beam cross-section

Buckling: If depth is increased significantly, and the load is not exactly applied at the intended line/point (hard to ensure, especially with moving traffic on bridges), a deep slender bar can bend sideways as in the “buckled” shape. This instability is called buckling.
Practical compromise in beam design: A commonly used cross-section (like an I/optimized section) provides:
  • large load-bearing surface,
  • sufficient depth to reduce bending,
  • reduced weight without sacrificing strength → lower cost.

Pillars/columns: effect of end shape on load supported

Columns are widely used in buildings and bridges. A pillar with rounded ends supports less load than a pillar with distributed (spread-out) ends. End design affects how forces are transmitted and the tendency to buckle/fail.
Real structural design must consider working conditions, cost, and long-term reliability of materials.

Elastic Properties and Earth Science Application: Maximum Mountain Height

Why mountains on Earth are not arbitrarily tall (~10 km estimate)

Rock at the base of a mountain is not under uniform compression (not pure bulk compression). Because the sides are free, there is a significant shear component of stress; rocks can flow when the shear stress exceeds a critical value (elastic limit / critical shearing stress).
Think “stack of books”: if the sides aren’t supported, the pile doesn’t just get squashed — it tends to slide sideways (shear) when it gets too tall.
Stress due to weight of mountain at base (order estimate): σ ≈ hρg
  • σ = stress (here, approximate shear component) at the base (N m−2)
  • h = height of mountain (m)
  • ρ = density of mountain rock (kg m−3)
  • g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2)
Worked estimate: Take typical rock elastic limit (critical shear stress) ≈ 30 × 107 N m−2. Set hρg equal to this limiting stress.
hρg
→
30 × 107 N m−2
With ρ = 3 × 103 kg m−3, g ≈ 10 m s−2:
h = (30 × 107)/(3 × 103 × 10) m = 104 m = 10 km
This is slightly more than the height of Mt. Everest, consistent with observation.
Common mistake: Treating base stress as “pressure” (uniform on all sides). Here the sides are free, so the state is not hydrostatic; shear matters.

Hooke’s law and elastic moduli

  • Hooke’s law: For small deformations, stress is directly proportional to strain (linear relation) for many materials.
  • Modulus of elasticity: proportionality constant between stress and strain.
  • Elastic moduli: Young’s modulus (Y), shear modulus (G), bulk modulus (B) describe elastic response under different kinds of deformation.
Elastomers: A class of solids that does not obey Hooke’s law (non-linear stress–strain behaviour).

Young’s modulus form of Hooke’s law (tension/compression)

Hooke’s law (longitudinal): F/A = Y (ΔL/L)
  • F = applied force causing extension/compression (N)
  • A = cross-sectional area normal to F (m²)
  • Y = Young’s modulus (N m−2)
  • ΔL/L = tensile or compressive strain (dimensionless)
  • Stress = F/A

Shear deformation and shear modulus

Shear deformation: Two equal and opposite forces applied tangentially to opposite faces cause one face to slide sideways relative to the other. If height is L and sideways displacement is ΔL, then shear strain is ΔL/L. Shearing stress exists only in solids (fluids cannot sustain shear in static equilibrium).
Hooke’s law (shear): F/A = G (ΔL/L)
  • F = tangential force producing shear (N)
  • A = area of face on which shear acts (m²)
  • G = shear modulus (N m−2)
  • ΔL = lateral displacement of one face/end (m)
  • L = separation/height between faces (m)

Hydraulic compression and bulk modulus

Hydraulic stress: uniform pressure exerted by a surrounding fluid compressing an object equally from all sides, producing a volume change without shape change (idealized).
Hooke’s law (bulk): p = B (ΔV/V)
  • p = pressure (hydraulic stress) (N m−2)
  • B = bulk modulus (N m−2)
  • ΔV/V = volume strain = absolute fractional change in volume (dimensionless)

Points to Ponder (Highly Testable Clarifications)

Tension and tensile stress in a hanging wire

A wire hanging from a ceiling with a weight F attached has an upward force from the ceiling equal and opposite to F. But the tension at any cross-section of the wire is F, not 2F. Therefore tensile stress = F/A.

Validity range of Hooke’s law

Hooke’s law is valid only in the linear part of the stress–strain curve.

Which moduli apply to which states of matter

Young’s modulus (Y)relevant only for solids
Shear modulus (G)relevant only for solids
Bulk modulus (B)relevant for solids, liquids, and gases
  • Y and G apply to solids (solids have definite length and shape).
  • B applies to solids, liquids, and gases (volume change under uniform stress with shape unchanged).

Comparing materials: metals, alloys, elastomers

Metals typically have larger Young’s modulus than alloys and elastomers. Large Y means a large force is required to produce a small change in length.

Correct meaning of “more elastic” (common misconception)

Daily-life usage is misleading: “stretches more” is often called “more elastic,” but in physics a material that stretches less under the same load (i.e., has larger Y) is considered more elastic.

Poisson effect and need for more than one elastic constant

A deforming force in one direction can produce strains in other directions. Thus stress–strain proportionality may require more than one elastic constant. Example: under longitudinal strain in a wire, the lateral dimension (radius) changes. This is described by another constant called the Poisson ratio.

Why stress is not a vector

Stress is not a vector quantity because, unlike force, it cannot be assigned a single specific direction. The force on a specified side of a section has a definite direction, but stress is defined as force per unit area and depends on the chosen plane/section.
