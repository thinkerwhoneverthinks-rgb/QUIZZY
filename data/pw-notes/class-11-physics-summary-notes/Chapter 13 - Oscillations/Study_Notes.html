Chapter 13 : Oscillations

13.1 Introduction

Non-repetitive motions: don’t repeat with time (e.g. rectilinear motion, projectile motion). Periodic motion: repeats itself after a fixed time interval (motion “recurs” identically). Oscillatory motion: a type of periodic motion where the body moves to-and-fro about a mean position (usually the equilibrium position).
Everyday examples of oscillatory (to-and-fro) motion:
  • Rocking in a cradle; swinging on a swing
  • Pendulum of a wall clock
  • Boat tossing up and down in a river
  • Piston in a steam engine moving back and forth
Why oscillations matter in physics (applications):
  • Musical instruments: vibrating strings (sitar, guitar, violin) → sound
  • Drums: vibrating membranes
  • Telephones/speakers: vibrating diaphragms
  • Sound propagation: oscillations of air molecules
  • Solids: atoms vibrate about equilibrium; average vibrational energy ∝ temperature
  • AC power supply: voltage alternates + and − about mean value (zero)
To describe periodic/oscillatory motion we use: period, frequency, displacement, amplitude, phase. (Developed आगे in the chapter.)

13.2 Periodic and Oscillatory Motions

Examples of periodic motion (height–time graphs)

Think of motions that repeat identically with time:
  • Insect on a ramp: climbs up, falls down, returns to start, repeats → periodic (height–time repeats).
  • Child on a step: climbs up, comes down, repeats same way → periodic.
  • Bouncing ball: bounces between palm and ground → periodic (height–time repeats with curved segments).
Newton’s equation for vertical displacement (for bouncing ball’s curved/parabolic parts): Downward: h = ut + (1/2)gt2 Upward: h = ut − (1/2)gt2 h = height above ground (vertical displacement) u = initial vertical velocity for that segment (can change each segment) g = acceleration due to gravity t = time from start of that segment
Periodic motion: motion that repeats at regular time intervals.

Equilibrium position and origin of oscillations

Equilibrium position: position where net external force = 0. If placed there at rest, it stays there. Restoring tendency: small displacement from equilibrium → a force appears that pulls it back → leads to oscillations/vibrations.
Illustrative example: ball in a bowl: equilibrium at the bottom. Displace slightly → it oscillates due to restoring tendency toward the bottom.
Periodic vs oscillatory:
  • Every oscillatory motion is periodic.
  • Not every periodic motion is oscillatory. Example: uniform circular motion is periodic but not “to-and-fro” about a mean position.

Oscillations vs vibrations (terminology)

No major physical difference between oscillations and vibrations. Usually we name them by frequency:
  • Lower frequency: called oscillation (e.g. tree branch)
  • Higher frequency: called vibration (e.g. musical string)

Simple harmonic motion (SHM): the simplest oscillation

Simple harmonic motion (SHM): simplest oscillation, when:
  • Force ∝ displacement from mean position (mean position = equilibrium position).
  • Force always acts towards mean (equilibrium) position ⇒ restoring force.
Exam tip: For SHM, write both conditions: (1) force proportional to displacement, AND (2) force directed toward mean position. Proportionality alone isn’t enough if direction is wrong.

Damping and forced oscillations (practical note)

Damping: real oscillations slowly die out; body finally rests at equilibrium due to friction + other dissipative effects. Forced oscillations: can be sustained by an external periodic agency (periodic driving). These are discussed later in the chapter.

Coupled oscillators and waves (link to next chapter)

Any material medium ≈ huge collection of coupled oscillators. Their collective oscillations appear as waves.
Examples of waves: water waves, seismic waves, electromagnetic waves. (Wave phenomena → next chapter.)

13.2.1 Period and frequency

Period (T): for a periodic motion, the smallest time after which the motion repeats.
Symbol: T SI unit of period: second (s)

Oscillations: Period and Frequency

Period (T): time for one complete repetition (one cycle/one oscillation). Frequency (ν): number of repetitions (oscillations) per unit time; reciprocal of period.
Frequency–Period relation (Eq. 13.1): ν = 1/T ν = frequency (oscillations per second) T = time period (time for one oscillation)
If seconds aren’t convenient, use suitable time units:
  • Quartz crystal vibrations: periods often in microseconds, µs = 10−6 s
  • Mercury orbital period: 88 earth days
  • Halley’s comet recurrence: about 76 years
Unit of frequency: s−1. Special name: hertz (Hz), after Heinrich Rudolf Hertz.
Definition of hertz (Eq. 13.2): 1 hertz = 1 Hz = 1 oscillation per second = 1 s−1 Hz = unit of frequency
Exam tip: Frequency ν doesn’t have to be an integer; it can be fractional (e.g. 1.25 Hz).

Example 13.1: Heartbeat frequency and period

Given: average heartbeats = 75 per minute. Frequency: ν = 75/(1 min) = 75/(60 s) = 1.25 s−1 = 1.25 Hz Period: T = 1/ν = 1/(1.25 s−1) = 0.8 s
Minutes → seconds before writing Hz (since Hz = s−1).

Displacement in Oscillations (General Meaning)

In oscillations, displacement is used in a general way: it just means a physical quantity that changes with time (not only position).
Examples of “displacement” variables:
  • Rectilinear motion of a steel ball: displacement = distance from a chosen starting point (origin picked for convenience).
  • Spring–block system: measure displacement from equilibrium position; call it x.
  • Simple pendulum: use angular displacement θ from the vertical.
  • AC circuit: voltage across a capacitor varies with time.
  • Sound wave: pressure variation with time.
  • Light wave: time-varying electric and magnetic fields.
A displacement variable can be + or −. In experiments, record it at different times and write it as a function of time.

Periodic functions used to represent displacement

For periodic motion, displacement is shown by a function that repeats after a fixed time.
Simple periodic function (Eq. 13.3a): f(t) = A cos(ωt) f(t): displacement-like quantity vs time A: amplitude (max value of f) ω: angular frequency (rad s−1) t: time
Since cos(θ) repeats when θ increases by 2π (or any integer multiple), cos(ωt) is periodic.
Period of cos(ωt) (Eq. 13.3b): T = 2π/ω T: time period (s) ω: angular frequency (rad s−1)
Periodicity condition: f(t) = f(t + T) Same period result also holds for: f(t) = A sin(ωt)
Linear combination (Eq. 13.3c): f(t) = A sin(ωt) + B cos(ωt) A, B: constants (set how much sine/cosine contribute)
Any combo of sin & cos with the same ω is still periodic with the same period T = 2π/ω.
Single-sine form (Eq. 13.3d): if A = D cosφ and B = D sinφ, then f(t) = D sin(ωt + φ) D: resultant amplitude φ: phase angle (constant)
Relations for D and φ: D = √(A2 + B2) tanφ = B/A D: overall amplitude of the combined wave A, B: coefficients in A sin(ωt) + B cos(ωt) φ: phase angle set by A and B
Fourier’s result (Fourier theorem/idea) (Jean Baptiste Joseph Fourier): Any periodic function can be written as a sum (superposition) of sines and cosines with different time periods (frequencies), with suitable coefficients.

Example 13.2: Identifying periodic vs non-periodic functions

Task: Decide if each is (a) periodic or (b) non-periodic; if periodic, find the period. (ω > 0) (i) sin(ωt) + cos(ωt)
  • Periodic.
  • Rewrite: √2 sin(ωt + π/4).
  • Repeat check: √2 sin(ωt + π/4) = √2 sin(ωt + π/4 + 2π) = √2 sin[ω(t + 2π/ω) + π/4].
  • Period: T = 2π/ω.
(ii) sin(ωt) + cos(2ωt) + sin(4ωt)
  • Periodic (sum of periodic terms).
  • Individual periods:
    • sin(ωt): T0 = 2π/ω
    • cos(2ωt): period = 2π/(2ω) = π/ω = T0/2
    • sin(4ωt): period = 2π/(4ω) = (2π/ω)/4 = T0/4
  • Overall period = least time after which the whole sum repeats. Since T0 is a multiple of the other two, smallest common repeating interval is T0.
  • Period: T = 2π/ω.
(iii) e−ωt
  • Non-periodic: decreases monotonically, tends to 0 as t → ∞, never repeats.
(iv) log(ωt)
  • Non-periodic: increases monotonically, never repeats.
  • As t → ∞, log(ωt) → ∞ (diverges), so it can’t represent a physical displacement (here).
How to test periodicity: periodic only if some finite T exists with f(t+T)=f(t) for all t in the domain. Strictly monotonic (always ↑ or always ↓) functions can’t be periodic.

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)

A particle oscillating between +A and −A about an origin executes SHM if its displacement is sinusoidal in time:
Displacement in SHM (Eq. 13.4): x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ) x(t): displacement from origin at time t A: amplitude (max |displacement|) ω: angular frequency φ: phase constant (phase angle at t = 0) (ωt + φ): phase (varies with time)
SHM ≠ any periodic motion. SHM is periodic motion where displacement is specifically a sine/cosine function of time.

Standard parameters describing SHM: A, ω, phase and phase constant

Amplitude (A): maximum displacement magnitude from equilibrium/origin.
  • cos ranges +1 to −1 ⇒ x ranges +A to −A.
  • Take A > 0 (no loss of generality).
  • Two SHMs can share same ω and φ but have different A (different max displacement).
Phase: the time-dependent angle (ωt + φ); it tells the “state” of motion (position + velocity) at time t. Phase constant (φ): phase at t = 0; also called phase angle.
  • If A is known, find φ from initial displacement x(0).
  • Two SHMs can have same A and ω but different φ (they’re out of phase).
From SHM diagrams/description: motion repeats after time T no matter which position you pick as t = 0. Speed is max at x = 0 and zero at the extremes (x = ±A).

Angular frequency and its connection to period (setup)

Angular frequency ω is linked to period T. To see this: set φ = 0 in x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), then match one full phase repeat (phase increase of 2π) to one time period T.

Angular frequency, time period and phase in SHM

Motion is SHM if displacement is a single sinusoid in time. Standard form: x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ)
  • A = amplitude (max displacement from equilibrium)
  • ω = angular frequency
  • φ = phase constant (initial phase)
x(t) = A cos ωt (special case: φ = 0) (Eq. 13.5)
  • x(t): displacement at time t
  • A: amplitude
  • ω: angular frequency (rad s−1)
  • t: time

Derivation of ω = 2π/T using periodicity

If the motion has period T, displacement repeats after one period: x(t) = x(t + T) Using Eq. (13.5): A cos ωt = A cos ω(t + T) (Eq. 13.6) Cos repeats when its angle increases by 2π: So for the first repeat, ω(t + T) = ωt + 2π Cancel ωt: ωT = 2π ⟹ ω = 2π/T (Eq. 13.7)
ω = 2π/T (Eq. 13.7)
  • ω: angular frequency (rad s−1)
  • T: time period (s)
  • π: constant (≈ 3.1416)
  • ω is 2π times ordinary frequency: since f = 1/T, we get ω = 2πf.
  • Two SHMs can have same A and same φ, but different ω (so different period/frequency).
  • From the SHM form: T = 2π/ω.

Period of related periodic functions (as illustrated)

  • x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ) is SHM with T = 2π/ω and phase angle φ (example phase: −π/4 or equivalently 7π/4).
  • sin2(ωt) using identity: sin2(ωt) = 1/2 − (1/2)cos(2ωt) So it’s periodic with T = π/ω.
  • sin2(ωt) has a harmonic cosine part but oscillates about shifted equilibrium 1/2 (not about 0).
Exam tip: “Periodic” ≠ automatically “SHM”. SHM must be a single sine/cosine in time: A cos(ωt + φ) (or A sin(ωt + φ)) about an equilibrium position (could be shifted if you redefine the origin).

Example 13.3: Identifying SHM vs periodic-but-not-SHM

(1) sin ωt − cos ωt

Check if it can be written as one sinusoid. Write cos as sine: cos ωt = sin(π/2 − ωt) So, sin ωt − cos ωt = sin ωt − sin(π/2 − ωt) Use: sin α − sin β = 2 cos((α+β)/2) sin((α−β)/2)
  • α = ωt
  • β = π/2 − ωt
Then: sin ωt − sin(π/2 − ωt) = 2 cos(π/4) sin(ωt − π/4) Since cos(π/4) = 1/√2: sin ωt − cos ωt = √2 sin(ωt − π/4)
  • It’s a single sine ⇒ it is SHM.
  • Amplitude = √2 (same units as the original scale).
  • Angular frequency = ω ⇒ T = 2π/ω.
  • Phase constant (sine form) = −π/4.

(2) sin2 ωt

Use identity: sin2(ωt) = 1/2 − (1/2)cos(2ωt)
  • Periodic: cosine term has angular frequency 2ω, so T = 2π/(2ω) = π/ω
  • Not SHM about zero because of the constant term 1/2 ⇒ equilibrium (mean) is shifted to 1/2.
Common exam slip: Don’t say “sin²(ωt) is SHM” just because it looks sinusoidal. Must fit A sin(Ωt + φ) or A cos(Ωt + φ) with no added constant. Here the constant shifts the mean value.

Simple harmonic motion and uniform circular motion

Key link: The projection of uniform circular motion on any diameter gives SHM along that diameter.

Physical visualisation (ball tied to a string)

  • Ball moves in a horizontal circle about a fixed point with constant angular speed ⇒ uniform circular motion.
  • Viewed edge-on (or as its shadow on a wall ⟂ to the plane), it appears to move back-and-forth in a line.
  • That straight-line motion is the projection on a diameter normal to the viewing direction.

Reference circle description and SHM equation

Let particle P move uniformly on a circle of radius A with angular speed ω (anticlockwise). At t = 0, OP makes angle φ with the +x-axis. After time t, the angle is ωt + φ. Projection onto x-axis (point P′) gives displacement:
x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ) (SHM form; referenced as Eq. 13.4 in text)
  • x(t): displacement of x-projection
  • A: radius of reference circle = SHM amplitude
  • ω: circular angular speed = SHM angular frequency
  • φ: initial phase angle at t = 0
P is the reference particle, and the circle is the reference circle.

Projection on y-axis and phase difference

Projection of OP on y-axis:
y(t) = A sin(ωt + φ)
  • y(t): displacement of y-projection
  • A, ω, φ: same meanings as above
  • x- and y-projections have the same amplitude A and same ω.
  • Phase difference is π/2 (sine = cosine shifted by π/2).
Very important: SHM can be described as a projection of circular motion, but the linear SHM force is not the centripetal force. The link is kinematic (description), not “same forces”.

Example 13.4: Finding SHM from circular motion (x-projection)

General method

  • At t = 0, note initial angle from +x-axis: φ
  • Angular displacement in time t: (2π/T)t (sign depends on rotation)
    • anticlockwise: + (2π/T)t
    • clockwise: − (2π/T)t
  • Angle at time t: φ ± (2π/T)t
  • x-projection: x(t) = (radius) cos(angle)

(a) Anticlockwise motion, radius A, T = 4 s, initial angle 45°

  • At t = 0: φ = 45° = π/4
  • In time t, angle covered = (2π/T)t anticlockwise
  • Angle at time t = π/4 + (2π/T)t
So the x-projection is: x(t) = A cos( (2π/T)t + π/4 ) For T = 4 s: x(t) = A cos( (π/2)t + π/4 )
  • This is SHM with:
    • Amplitude = A
    • Period = 4 s
    • Initial phase = π/4

Angle units note (radian vs degree)

  • Natural angle unit = radian = (arc length)/(radius) ⇒ angle is dimensionless.
  • If trig argument is written without units, assume radians.
  • If using degrees, write the ° explicitly.
  • sin(15) means sine of 15 radians.
  • sin(15°) means sine of 15 degrees.
  • In this chapter, “rad” is often omitted → assume radians unless degrees are shown.

(b) Clockwise motion, radius B, T = 30 s, initial angle 90°

  • At t = 0: φ = 90° = π/2
  • Clockwise ⇒ angle decreases by (2π/T)t
  • Angle at time t = π/2 − (2π/T)t
x-projection: x(t) = B cos( π/2 − (2π/T)t ) Use identity cos(π/2 − θ) = sin θ: x(t) = B sin( (2π/T)t ) For T = 30 s: x(t) = B sin( (π/15)t ) Write in cosine form to match x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ): x(t) = B cos( (π/15)t − π/2 )
  • This represents SHM with:
    • Amplitude = B
    • Period = 30 s
    • Initial phase (cos form) = −π/2

Velocity and acceleration in simple harmonic motion

Speed in uniform circular motion

v = ωA (Eq. 13.8)
  • v: speed in uniform circular motion
  • ω: angular speed
  • A: radius of circle
Velocity is always tangential to the circle.

Instantaneous velocity in SHM

For x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), projection idea (or differentiate) gives:
v(t) = −ωA sin(ωt + φ) (Eq. 13.9)
  • v(t): instantaneous SHM velocity
  • − sign: direction opposite to +x (for this setup)
  • ω, A, φ: as defined earlier
Also (definition of instantaneous velocity):
v(t) = dx(t)/dt (Eq. 13.10)
  • dx/dt: time derivative of displacement
Sign matters: velocity in SHM flips sign with direction. Only give magnitude if they ask for “speed”.

Instantaneous acceleration in SHM

In uniform circular motion: centripetal acceleration magnitude v2/A = ω2A, toward the centre. Project onto x-axis (SHM projection) ⇒
a(t) = −ω2A cos(ωt + φ) = −ω2x(t) (Eq. 13.11)
  • a(t): instantaneous SHM acceleration
  • − sign: acceleration points toward equilibrium (opposite to displacement)
  • x(t): instantaneous displacement
Also (definition):
a(t) = dv(t)/dt (Eq. 13.12)
  • dv/dt: time derivative of velocity
Key SHM property: a ∝ x but opposite direction:
  • If x(t) > 0, then a(t) < 0
  • If x(t) < 0, then a(t) > 0
So acceleration always “pulls back” to the equilibrium position.

Oscillations: Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration in SHM (Phase relations)

In SHM, x(t) stays between -A and +A. For any x in this range, acceleration a(t) always points towards the centre (mean position).
Often we set φ = 0 to simplify. SHM doesn’t change — it just fixes the time origin.
x(t) = A cos(ωt) v(t) = -ωA sin(ωt) a(t) = -ω2A cos(ωt) x(t): displacement from mean position (m) v(t): velocity (m s-1) a(t): acceleration (m s-2) A: amplitude (max displacement) (m) ω: angular frequency (rad s-1) t: time (s)
x, v, a are all sinusoidal with the same period T, but different maxima + phase.
  • x(t) ranges: -A to +A
  • v(t) ranges: -ωA to +ωA
  • a(t) ranges: -ω2A to +ω2A
  • Compared to displacement: v is phase-shifted by π/2, and a by π.
Same T ≠ same phase: they repeat after T, but peaks/zeros happen at different times (phase differences).

Worked Example 13.5: Displacement, speed, acceleration at a given time

Given (SI): x = 5 cos(2πt + π/4). At t = 1.5 s, find: (a) displacement, (b) speed, (c) acceleration.
From x = A cos(ωt + φ), read off:
  • A = 5 m
  • ω = 2π s-1
  • T = 2π/ω = 1 s
(a) Displacement at t = 1.5 s: x = (5.0 m) cos[(2π)(1.5) + π/4] = 5 cos(3π + π/4) cos(3π + π/4) = -cos(π/4) = -0.707 x = -5(0.707) = -3.535 m (b) Speed (velocity) at t = 1.5 s using v = -ωA sin(ωt+φ): v = -(5.0)(2π) sin[(2π)(1.5) + π/4] = -(10π) sin(3π + π/4) sin(3π + π/4) = -sin(π/4) = -0.707 v = -(10π)(-0.707) = 10π(0.707) m s-1 ≈ 22 m s-1 (c) Acceleration using a = -ω2x: a = -(2π)2 × (displacement) = -(2π)2 × (-3.535) a ≈ 140 m s-2
Part (b): “speed” = magnitude of velocity. Work out v with sign first, then take magnitude only if they explicitly ask. Here the simplified numerical answer is positive.

13.6 Force Law for Simple Harmonic Motion

Use Newton’s 2nd law + SHM acceleration: in SHM, net force ∝ displacement and points opposite to it (back to the mean position).
F(t) = ma = -mω2x(t) F(t) = -kx(t) (13.13) k = mω2 (13.14a) ω = √(k/m) (13.14b) F: restoring force (N) m: mass (kg) ω: angular frequency (rad s-1) x: displacement (m) k: force constant (spring constant in spring SHM) (N m-1)
  • Because F always points to the mean position, it’s called the restoring force.
  • SHM can be defined either by:
    • displacement form: x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), or
    • force law: F = -kx.
  • From displacement → force law: differentiate twice (since a = d2x/dt2).
  • From force law → displacement: integrate twice (to recover x(t)).

Linear vs Non-linear Oscillators

If restoring force is linear in displacement (F ∝ x, specifically F = -kx), it’s a linear harmonic oscillator.
Real systems may have extra small terms like x2, x3, … ⇒ these are non-linear oscillators.

Worked Example 13.6: Block connected to two identical springs

Two identical springs (each spring constant k) attached to a block of mass m between fixed supports. Displace either way: show motion is SHM and find the period.
Displace the block a small distance x to the right from equilibrium.
  • Left spring stretched by x ⇒ force back to mean: F1 = -kx.
  • Right spring compressed by x ⇒ force back to mean: F2 = -kx.
Net restoring force: F = F1 + F2 = -kx -kx = -2kx
Force ∝ displacement and points to mean ⇒ SHM, with effective force constant: keff = 2k
T = 2π √(m/keff) = 2π √(m/2k) T: time period (s) m: mass (kg) keff: effective spring constant (N m-1)
Common error: using k instead of 2k. Two identical springs act together, so restoring forces add.

13.7 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion

In SHM, kinetic energy (K) and potential energy (U) swap back and forth (0 ↔ max). Total mechanical energy E stays constant if forces are conservative.

Kinetic Energy in SHM

  • v = 0 at extremes (x = ±A), and v is max at mean (x = 0).
  • So K is 0 at extremes, max at mean position.
  • K ∝ v2 ⇒ sign of v doesn’t matter.
  • Kinetic energy has period T/2 (repeats twice per oscillation).
K = ½mv2 v = -ωA sin(ωt+φ) K = ½mω2A2 sin2(ωt+φ) K = ½kA2 sin2(ωt+φ) (13.15) K: kinetic energy (J) m: mass (kg) v: speed/velocity magnitude for energy (m s-1) ω: angular frequency (rad s-1) A: amplitude (m) φ: phase constant (rad) k: force constant (N m-1), using k = mω2

Potential Energy in SHM (spring force as conservative force)

Potential energy is only defined for conservative forces. Spring restoring force F = -kx is conservative.
U = ½kx2 (13.16) x = A cos(ωt+φ) U(x) = ½kA2 cos2(ωt+φ) (13.17) U: potential energy (J) k: spring constant/force constant (N m-1) x: displacement (m) A: amplitude (m) ω: angular frequency (rad s-1) φ: phase constant (rad)
  • U is 0 at mean position (x=0), max at extremes (x=±A).
  • U has period T/2.

Total Mechanical Energy in SHM (constant)

E = U + K E = ½kA2cos2(ωt+φ) + ½kA2sin2(ωt+φ) E = ½kA2[cos2(ωt+φ) + sin2(ωt+φ)] E = ½kA2 (13.18) E: total mechanical energy (J) k: force constant (N m-1) A: amplitude (m)
  • Using sin2θ + cos2θ = 1, total energy is constant (time-independent) for SHM with a conservative force.
  • Energy sharing:
    • At x=0: all energy is kinetic.
    • At x=±A: all energy is potential.
    • In between: K increases as U decreases, and vice-versa.
  • K and U are always positive:
    • K ≥ 0 since it depends on v2.
    • U taken positive by choosing the arbitrary additive constant in PE.
  • Both K and U peak twice in each period T ⇒ repeat after T/2.
Exam trap: total energy repeats with T (actually it’s constant), but K and U repeat with T/2, not T.

Worked Example 13.7: Energies of a spring–block oscillator at a given displacement

m = 1 kg, k = 50 N m-1. Pulled to x = 10 cm on a frictionless surface, released from rest at t=0 ⇒ amplitude A=0.10 m. Find K, U, and E when it’s 5 cm from mean (x = 0.05 m).
Angular frequency: ω = √(k/m) = √(50/1) = 7.07 rad s-1 Displacement (released from rest at max displacement ⇒ cosine): x(t) = 0.1 cos(7.07t) At x = 0.05 m: 0.05 = 0.1 cos(7.07t) This equation just picks the time(s) when the block is at 5 cm; energies at that displacement are found straight from the energy formulas in x and A.
U = ½kx2 E = ½kA2 K = E - U U: potential energy at displacement x (J) K: kinetic energy at displacement x (J) E: total energy (J) k: spring constant (N m-1) x: instantaneous displacement (m) A: amplitude (m)
Frictionless surface + conservative spring force ⇒ E stays constant and depends only on A (not on position along the path).

Energy in SHM (spring–block): K.E., P.E. and conservation

Mass–spring SHM (no friction):
  • Kinetic energy depends on speed:K = ½ m v2
  • Spring potential energy depends on displacement:U = ½ k x2
  • Total mechanical energy stays fixed:E = K + U = constant
At max displacement (x = A): v = 0 ⇒ K = 0, so all energy is spring P.E.: E = ½ k A2

Worked energy check at x = 5 cm (given m = 1 kg, k = 50 N m–1, A = 0.10 m, ω = 7.07 s–1)

At x = 5 cm = 0.05 m, from SHM displacement relation: cos(7.07 t) = 0.5 ⇒ sin(7.07 t) = √(1 − 0.52) = √(3/4) = 0.866 Speed at this instant (using |v| = ωA sin(ωt + φ)): v = 0.1 × 7.07 × 0.866 m s–1 = 0.61 m s–1 Kinetic energy: K = ½ m v2 = ½[1 × (0.6123)2] J = 0.19 J Spring potential energy: U = ½ k x2 = ½(50 × 0.05 × 0.05) J = 0.0625 J Total energy at x = 5 cm: E = K + U = 0.19 + 0.0625 = 0.25 J Energy at max displacement x = A = 0.10 m: E = ½ k A2 = ½(50 × 0.1 × 0.1) = 0.25 J
Same total energy (0.25 J) at x = 5 cm and at x = A ⇒ mechanical energy is conserved in SHM (no friction), even though K and U each change with time.
Common exam slip: taking x = 5 cm as 5 (instead of 0.05 m). Always convert cm → m before using energy formulas.

13.8 The Simple Pendulum

Simple pendulum: small bob (mass m) on an inextensible, massless string (length L), hung from a fixed rigid support. Bob swings in a vertical plane about the vertical through the support (mean position at θ = 0).

Forces on the bob and their components

When the string makes angle θ with the vertical, forces on the bob:
  • Tension T along the string (towards support).
  • Weight mg vertically downward.
Resolve mg relative to the string:
  • Along string: mg cosθ
  • Perpendicular (tangential): mg sinθ
Bob moves on a circular arc (radius L, centre at support), so:
  • Radial (centripetal) acceleration magnitude ω2L, from net radial force (T − mg cosθ).
  • Tangential acceleration due to mg sinθ (speed along arc changes).
For SHM derivation, using torque about the support is easier, since radial forces give zero torque about the support.

Restoring torque and equation of motion

Restoring torque about the support: τ = −L (mg sinθ)    (13.19) τ = torque about the support (N m) L = length (m) m = bob mass (kg) g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2) θ = angular displacement from vertical (rad) Minus sign: torque reduces θ (restoring).
Newton’s rotational law: τ = I α    (13.20) I = moment of inertia about support (kg m2) α = angular acceleration (rad s−2)
Combine (13.19) + (13.20): I α = −m g L sinθ    (13.21) So, α = −(m g L / I) sinθ    (13.22)

Small-angle approximation and SHM condition

Series for sinθ (θ in radians): sinθ = θ − θ3/3! + θ5/5! ± …    (13.23) θ = angular displacement (rad)
If θ is small: sinθ ≈ θ, so from (13.22):
Small-angle pendulum equation: α = −(m g L / I) θ    (13.24) α = angular acceleration (rad s−2) θ = angular displacement (rad) m, g, L, I as above
(13.24) matches linear SHM form: acceleration ∝ displacement and opposite in direction. So for small θ, a simple pendulum executes SHM. Angular SHM form: α = −ω2 θ
Text note: comparing sinθ with θ (in radians) shows that even up to ~20°, sinθ ≈ θ (rad) closely enough for many exam questions.

Angular frequency and time period of a simple pendulum

Compare α = −(m g L / I) θ with α = −ω2 θ:
Simple pendulum (small angles): ω and T: ω = √(m g L / I) T = 2π √(I / (m g L))    (13.25) ω = angular frequency (rad s−1) T = time period (s) I = moment of inertia about support (kg m2) m = bob mass (kg) g = gravitational acceleration (m s−2) L = length (m)
For a massless string, the bob ≈ point mass at distance L: I = mL2 Sub into (13.25):
Time period (massless string, small angles): T = 2π √(L/g)    (13.26) T = time period (s) L = length (m) g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2)
For T = 2π√(L/g), exams assume: (i) small angle (sinθ ≈ θ, θ in radians), (ii) string massless + inextensible, bob small (point mass). If not, formula isn’t exact.

Example 13.8: Length of a seconds pendulum

Seconds pendulum: “ticks seconds” ⇒ time period T = 2 s.
Using (13.26): T = 2π √(L/g) Rearrange for L: L = gT2 / (4π2) Given g = 9.8 m s−2, T = 2 s: L = 9.8 × (2)2 / (4π2) = 1 m
So a seconds pendulum has length ≈ 1 m (for g ≈ 9.8 m s−2).
