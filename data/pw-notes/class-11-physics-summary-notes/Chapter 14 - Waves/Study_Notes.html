Ch14: Waves

14.1 Introduction (Waves)

Wave: a moving disturbance pattern travelling through a medium (or space) without overall transfer/flow of matter. Key idea: medium particles mainly oscillate about equilibrium; the disturbance (so energy + information) moves point to point.

From isolated oscillations to waves in a system (role of a medium)

  • Earlier: studied oscillations of single objects in isolation.
  • In a system (many objects), esp. in a material medium, parts are linked by elastic forces.
  • So motion of one part affects neighbours → disturbance can propagate.

Water-wave example: disturbance travels, matter does not

  • Drop a pebble in still water → surface disturbance.
  • Disturbance spreads outward as circles moving away from the source.
  • Easy wrong idea: water itself flows outward.
  • Test with cork pieces on the surface:
    • corks move up and down
    • but do not drift away from the centre
  • Conclusion: no outward flow of water mass; it’s a moving disturbance that travels.
Exam takeaway: in wave motion, medium particles don’t travel long distances with the wave; they oscillate locally. What moves is the disturbance → hence energy + information.

Sound-wave example: propagation without net airflow

  • When we speak, sound spreads outward.
  • This happens without bulk airflow from speaker to listener (no net transport of air as a whole).
  • Air disturbances aren’t easily seen; detected by ears or a microphone.

Waves as carriers of energy and information; communication

  • Waves transfer energy.
  • The travelling disturbance also carries signals/information.
  • Most communication = send + detect signals using waves:
    • Speech = making sound waves in air
    • Hearing = detecting those sound waves
  • Often the signal changes form across stages:
    • sound waves → electric current signal → generates an electromagnetic (EM) wave
    • EM wave may travel via optical cable or satellite
    • at detection: steps are usually reversed to recover the original signal

Types of waves (classification by need for medium)

  • Mechanical waves: need a material medium; cannot travel in vacuum; involve oscillations of particles; depend on the medium’s elastic properties.
  • Electromagnetic waves: don’t need a medium; can travel in vacuum. Examples: light, radio, X-rays.
  • Matter waves: linked with particles (electrons, protons, neutrons, atoms, molecules); from quantum mechanics; abstract but useful (e.g. electron microscopes use electron matter waves).
This chapter focus: only mechanical waves (need a material medium).

Electromagnetic waves in vacuum: universal speed

Speed of EM waves in vacuum: c = 299,792,458 m s−1 (Eq. 14.1) c = speed of light (and all EM waves) in vacuum

Historical note and link between oscillations and waves

  • Waves show up in art/literature early, but scientific wave analysis began in the 17th century.
  • Key names: Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695), Robert Hooke, Isaac Newton.
  • Wave physics grew out of studying:
    • oscillations of masses on springs
    • the simple pendulum
  • Core connection: waves in elastic media are closely tied to harmonic oscillations.
  • Elastic media examples: stretched strings, coiled springs, air, etc.

Model 1: coupled springs — how a disturbance travels

  • Imagine many springs joined in series.
  • Pull the spring at one end suddenly and release → disturbance goes to the far end.
  • Mechanism:
    • First spring displaced from equilibrium length.
    • Because it’s connected, it stretches/compresses the next spring.
    • This influence passes along spring-to-spring.
  • Key outcome:
    • Disturbance moves down the chain.
    • Each spring does only small oscillations about equilibrium (local motion).

Practical analogy: train bogies with spring coupling

  • A stationary train: bogies linked by spring couplings.
  • Engine attaches at one end and pushes the nearest bogie:
    • push is passed from bogie to bogie
    • without the whole train shifting bodily all at once
  • Shows how a disturbance transmits through coupled parts.

Model 2: sound waves in air — compressions and rarefactions

  • As sound passes, it locally compresses or expands small air regions.
  • This creates a density change δρ.
  • Density change causes a pressure change δp.
  • Since pressure = force/area, pressure differences give a restoring force proportional to the disturbance (spring-like).
  • Spring analogy:
    • spring variable: extension/compression
    • sound variable: density change (compression/rarefaction)
  • How propagation happens:
    • Compressed region: molecules crowded → push outward to neighbours → makes a compression next door while the first region turns rarefied.
    • Rarefied region: nearby air rushes in → rarefaction shifts to the next region.
    • So compressions + rarefactions travel region to region → disturbance propagates.
Common exam mistakes to avoid:
  • Don’t write “water/sound wave carries matter from source to observer.” Correct: it carries energy + information; particles mainly oscillate about equilibrium.
  • Mechanical waves cannot travel in vacuum; EM waves can.
  • For sound, what travels is the pressure/density variation pattern (compressions/rarefactions), not bulk air motion.

Waves in Solids: Lattice as Springs

Crystalline solid model: atoms (or atom-groups) sit in a periodic lattice. Each is in equilibrium due to forces from neighbours. Restoring-force idea: displace one atom while neighbours stay fixed → forces appear that pull it back: restoring forces (spring-like). Spring–lattice analogy: treat atoms as end points and interatomic forces as springs between pairs → basis of mechanical wave travel in solids.

14.2 Transverse and Longitudinal Waves

Definitions (based on particle oscillations vs propagation direction)

Mechanical waves: wave involves oscillations of the medium’s particles/elements. Transverse wave: particles oscillate perpendicular to wave travel direction. Longitudinal wave: particles oscillate along/parallel to wave travel direction.

Transverse waves on a stretched string: pulse and sinusoidal wave

Single pulse (jerk): one quick up–down jerk at an end → a pulse moves along the string (propagation along string, say x), while elements move up–down (y). Since oscillations ⟂ propagation ⇒ transverse.
Ignoring reflection condition: if string is very long compared to pulse size, pulse damps out before the far end → reflection can be ignored.
Sinusoidal (harmonic) wave: drive one end with continuous periodic sinusoidal up–down motion → a sinusoidal travelling wave goes along the string. Elements oscillate about equilibrium perpendicular to travel direction ⇒ transverse.

Two complementary ways to “look at” a wave

  • Snapshot in space: fix a time, plot displacement vs position → wave shape in space at that instant.
  • Time history at a point: fix a position, watch displacement vs time → that element’s oscillation in time.

Longitudinal waves in air (sound) in a pipe with a piston

Pulse production: sudden push forward + pull back of piston → a pulse made of:
  • Condensations: higher density (and pressure)
  • Rarefactions: lower density (and pressure)
Sinusoidal sound wave: piston moves continuously + periodically (sinusoidal) → sinusoidal longitudinal wave along the pipe.
Air volume elements oscillate parallel to propagation ⇒ longitudinal.

Progressive (travelling) waves vs bulk flow of a medium

The transverse/longitudinal waves above are travelling (progressive) waves: disturbance moves through the medium. Key distinction: the medium as a whole does not travel with the wave; only the disturbance does.
  • Stream: whole water moves (bulk flow) → not a wave.
  • Water wave: disturbance moves; water does not move along with it as a whole.
  • Wind: bulk motion of air → not a wave.
  • Sound wave in air: disturbance (pressure/density variations) propagates with no net motion of air as a whole.

Which media can support transverse vs longitudinal waves?

Transverse waves: particle motion is normal to propagation → each element suffers shearing strain as the wave passes.
Consequence: transverse waves only in media that can sustain shearing stress (e.g. solids), and not in fluids (fluids can’t sustain shear stress in static equilibrium).
Longitudinal waves: involve compressive strain → both solids and fluids can sustain this.
Consequence: longitudinal waves can travel in all elastic media (solids + fluids).
  • Steel: supports transverse + longitudinal.
  • Air: supports only longitudinal.

Surface waves on water: capillary and gravity waves; particle motion

Two kinds of surface waves on water:
  • Capillary waves: short wavelength ripples (≤ a few cm); restoring force = surface tension.
  • Gravity waves: wavelength ~ several m to several hundred m; restoring force = gravity (keeps surface near lowest level).
Depth involvement (gravity waves): motion not just at surface; oscillations extend downward with diminishing amplitude to the bottom.
Particle motion in water waves is complex: particles move up–down and back–forth → ocean waves = mix of longitudinal + transverse components.

Speed difference of transverse and longitudinal waves

In the same medium, transverse and longitudinal waves generally travel at different speeds.

Example 14.1: Classifying wave motion

Task: decide if wave motion is transverse, longitudinal, or both.
  1. (a) Motion of a kink in a longitudinal spring produced by displacing one end sideways Answer: Transverse and longitudinal
  2. (b) Waves produced in a cylinder containing a liquid by moving its piston back and forth Answer: Longitudinal
  3. (c) Waves produced by a motorboat sailing in water Answer: Transverse and longitudinal
  4. (d) Ultrasonic waves in air produced by a vibrating quartz crystal Answer: Longitudinal
Exam tip: compare particle motion direction vs propagation direction. In a fluid (air / bulk water), expect no transverse in the bulk; surface water waves are special → can have both components.

14.3 Displacement Relation in a Progressive Wave

Need for a function of position and time

To describe a travelling (progressive) wave properly, displacement must depend on position and time: y(x,t).
  • At a fixed instant: plot y vs x → gives the wave’s shape in space.
  • At a fixed location: plot y vs t → gives the oscillation of that medium element.
For a sinusoidal travelling wave, the function must itself be sinusoidal.

Sinusoidal transverse travelling wave: standard form

Take a transverse wave (easy to picture). Let x be along the direction of travel; y is the transverse displacement from equilibrium.
Sinusoidal progressive wave: y(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt + φ)   (Eq. 14.2) a = amplitude (max displacement) k = wave number (spatial angular frequency) ω = angular frequency (temporal angular frequency) φ = phase constant (initial phase) x = position coordinate t = time

Role of phase constant and equivalent sine–cosine combination

The φ inside the sine just means a phase shift. Same idea as writing the wave as a suitable mix of sine + cosine (with the same k and ω).
Equivalent form: y(x,t) = A sin(kx − ωt) + B cos(kx − ωt)   (Eq. 14.3) A = sine coefficient B = cosine coefficient k, ω = same meanings as above
Relations between (a, φ) and (A, B): a2 = A2 + B2 tan φ = B/A   (so φ = tan−1(B/A), choose the correct quadrant) a = resultant amplitude φ = phase constant set by A and B

Why y(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt + φ) represents a travelling wave (start of reasoning)

To read Eq. (14.2), freeze time at t = t0. Then the sine argument becomes a function of x only, so y varies sinusoidally with x → that’s the wave’s spatial profile at that instant.
Common mistake: treating y as only y(x) or only y(t). A progressive wave needs both x and t dependence to show propagation.

Travelling Harmonic (Sinusoidal) Wave: Meaning of the Standard Form

A harmonic (sinusoidal) travelling wave along the x-axis is written as a displacement depending on position + time: y(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt + φ) (standard form for travel along +x)

How the expression shows it is a travelling sine wave

  • Wave shape at fixed time: Fix t → (kx − ωt + φ) becomes “kx + constant” → y is sinusoidal in x. So the profile vs x is a sine curve.
  • Time variation at fixed position: Fix x = x0 → (kx − ωt + φ) becomes “constant − ωt” → y is sinusoidal in time. So each particle does SHM.
  • Direction of propagation: Hold phase constant: (kx − ωt + φ = constant). As t increases, x must increase to keep phase constant → motion is along +x.
Travelling wave criterion (phase argument): A wave travels because constant-phase points move through space as time passes.

Wave travelling in the negative x-direction

Flip the sign before ωt → travel reverses direction: y(x,t) = a sin(kx + ωt + φ) is a wave travelling along −x.

Crest, trough, and what “motion” means in a travelling wave

  • Crest: point of maximum +ve displacement.
  • Trough: point of maximum −ve displacement.
  • To picture propagation: follow one crest → it shifts along x as time increases (pattern moves).
  • To picture particle motion: fix a point (e.g. x = 0) → that particle moves up/down (or back/forth) periodically about its mean position as the wave passes.
  • In one full oscillation of a particle at a fixed position, a crest advances a definite distance (later: one wavelength in one period).

Meaning of symbols in the standard wave equation

  • y(x,t): displacement of the medium element at position x and time t (transverse case).
  • a: amplitude.
  • ω: angular frequency.
  • k: angular wave number (propagation constant).
  • (kx − ωt + φ): phase.
  • φ: phase at x = 0, t = 0 → initial phase angle.
Exam pitfall: y can be + or −, but amplitude a is defined positive.

14.3.1 Amplitude and Phase

Amplitude

In y(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt + φ), since sin ranges from −1 to +1, y ranges from −a to +a. Amplitude a: maximum displacement from equilibrium. Convention: a > 0.

Phase, initial phase angle, and choosing φ = 0

Phase: the sine argument (kx − ωt + φ). For a fixed a, phase sets the displacement at any x, t. Initial phase angle φ: phase at x = 0 and t = 0.
By choosing the x-origin and the start time suitably, you can set φ = 0 (no loss of generality; simplifies working).

14.3.2 Wavelength and Angular Wave Number

Definition of wavelength (λ)

Wavelength λ: the minimum distance between two points with the same phase. Usually measured between successive crests (or successive troughs) → that separation is λ.

Derivation of relation between λ and k

Set φ = 0 in the standard wave equation. At t = 0:
y(x,0) = a sin(kx) (Eq. 14.5)
  • y(x,0): displacement at position x when t = 0
  • a: amplitude
  • k: angular wave number
  • x: position coordinate
Sine repeats when its argument increases by 2π, so displacement at x and at x + (2πn/k) is the same for n = 1, 2, 3, … The smallest such separation is n = 1, so:
λ = 2π/k   and equivalently   k = 2π/λ (Eq. 14.6)
  • λ: wavelength (m)
  • k: angular wave number / propagation constant
Unit of k: rad m−1 (often just m−1 since rad is dimensionless). Meaning: k = 2π × (number of waves per unit length) = phase change per unit length.
Exam pitfall: λ is the minimum same-phase separation. Points 2λ, 3λ, … apart are also in phase, but that distance is not the wavelength.

14.3.3 Period, Angular Frequency and Frequency

Period (T) from time variation at a fixed point

To track one medium element: fix x = 0 and take φ = 0. From y(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt), at x = 0: y(0,t) = a sin(−ωt) = −a sin(ωt)
Period (T): time taken by a medium element to complete one full oscillation.
After one period the motion repeats: y(0,t) = y(0,t + T). Since sine repeats when its argument changes by 2π:
T = 2π/ω  or  ω = 2π/T (Eq. 14.7)
  • T: time period (s)
  • ω: angular frequency (rad s−1)

Angular frequency (ω) and frequency (ν)

Angular frequency (ω): rate of change of phase with time at a fixed position; unit rad s−1. Frequency (ν): number of oscillations per second; unit hertz (Hz).
ν = 1/T = ω/(2π) (Eq. 14.8)
  • ν: frequency (Hz = s−1)
  • T: time period (s)
  • ω: angular frequency (rad s−1)
Exam pitfall: Don’t mix up ω and ν. They differ by 2π: ω = 2πν.

Longitudinal waves: displacement along the direction of propagation

In a longitudinal wave, the medium’s displacement is parallel to the direction of propagation.
s(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt + φ) (Eq. 14.9)
  • s(x,t): displacement of the medium element along the direction of propagation at position x and time t
  • a: displacement amplitude
  • k: angular wave number
  • ω: angular frequency
  • φ: initial phase angle
Same maths form as transverse waves; only the displacement meaning changes: use y(x,t) for transverse displacement, and s(x,t) for longitudinal displacement.

Example 14.2: Reading wave parameters from a travelling-wave equation

A harmonic travelling wave (moving in +x direction): y(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt)
  • a = amplitude (maximum displacement) (m)
  • k = angular wave number (rad m−1)
  • ω = angular frequency (rad s−1)

Given wave and identification of parameters

Given: y(x,t) = 0.005 sin(80.0 x − 3.0 t) (SI units). Compare with y(x,t)=a sin(kx−ωt):
  • a = 0.005 m
  • k = 80.0 rad m−1
  • ω = 3.0 rad s−1

(a) Amplitude

Amplitude a = 0.005 m = 5 mm.

(b) Wavelength from k

λ = 2π/k
  • λ = wavelength (m)
  • k = angular wave number (rad m−1)
Substitute k = 80.0: λ = 2π/80.0 m = 0.0785 m = 7.85 cm.

(c) Period and frequency from ω

T = 2π/ω
  • T = time period (s)
  • ω = angular frequency (rad s−1)
Substitute ω = 3.0: T = 2π/3.0 s = 2.09 s.
ν = 1/T
  • ν = frequency (Hz)
  • T = time period (s)
ν = 1/2.09 = 0.48 Hz.

Displacement at x = 30.0 cm, t = 20 s

Use the given wave equation: y = (0.005) sin(80.0×0.30 − 3.0×20) = (0.005) sin(24 − 60) = (0.005) sin(−36) = (0.005) sin(−36 + 12π) = (0.005) sin(1.699) = 5 mm So, y = (0.005 m) sin(1.699) (numerical value obtained by evaluating sin(1.699)).
When using sin, the angle must be in radians. You can add/subtract 2πn since sine is periodic: sin(θ) = sin(θ + 2πn).

14.4 The Speed of a Travelling Wave

To get wave speed, follow a point of fixed phase (like a crest). In a small time Δt, the pattern shifts by Δx ⇒ speed = Δx/Δt.

Fixed-phase condition

kx − ωt = constant (fixed phase point)
  • x = position of the chosen phase point (m)
  • t = time (s)
  • k = angular wave number (rad m−1)
  • ω = angular frequency (rad s−1)
As time changes, x must change so the phase stays constant: kx − ωt = k(x+Δx) − ω(t+Δt) ⇒ kΔx − ωΔt = 0

Wave speed in terms of ω and k

v = ω/k
  • v = wave speed (m s−1)
  • ω = angular frequency (rad s−1)
  • k = angular wave number (rad m−1)

General relation between speed, wavelength and frequency

v = λ/T = λν
  • v = wave speed (m s−1)
  • λ = wavelength (m)
  • T = time period (s)
  • ν = frequency (Hz)
Meaning of v = λ/T: in one time period T (one full oscillation of a particle), the wave pattern moves forward by exactly one wavelength λ.

What determines wave speed in mechanical waves?

  • Speed of a mechanical wave depends on inertial properties (e.g. linear mass density for a string; mass density in general) and elastic properties (e.g. Young’s modulus for linear media; shear modulus; bulk modulus).
  • In the same medium, transverse and longitudinal waves can both travel, but usually with different speeds.
  • The medium sets the speed; then v = λν links wavelength and frequency for that speed.

14.4.1 Speed of a Transverse Wave on a Stretched String

Waves on a stretched string:
  • Restoring influence → tension T
  • Inertial property → linear mass density μ = m/L

Dimensional analysis leading to the form of the speed

Dimensions:
  • Linear mass density: [μ] = [M L−1]
  • Tension (force): [T] = [M L T−2]
  • Speed: [v] = [L T−1]
Quick check: T/μ has dimensions [T/μ] = [M L T−2]/[M L−1] = [L2 T−2] So √(T/μ) has [L T−1] → i.e. a speed.
v = C √(T/μ) (from dimensional analysis)
  • v = transverse wave speed (m s−1)
  • T = tension (N)
  • μ = linear mass density (kg m−1)
  • C = dimensionless constant (can’t get this from dimensional analysis)
Dimensional analysis can’t give the exact number out front → it leaves an unknown dimensionless factor C.

Exact result for a stretched string

v = √(T/μ)
  • v = wave speed on string (m s−1)
  • T = tension (N)
  • μ = linear mass density (kg m−1)
So, in the exact formula: C = 1.
  • v depends only on medium properties (here: T and μ). It does not depend on the wave’s λ or ν.
  • More advanced: some waves are dispersive (speed depends on frequency), but a stretched string here shows independence from ν.

How frequency and wavelength are set

  • The source of disturbance sets the frequency ν.
  • Once the medium fixes v, wavelength follows from:
λ = v/ν (equivalently v = λν)
  • λ = wavelength (m)
  • v = wave speed (m s−1)
  • ν = frequency (Hz)

Example 14.3: Speed of transverse waves on a steel wire

Given:
  • Length L = 0.72 m
  • Mass m = 5.0 × 10−3 kg
  • Tension T = 60 N
Step 1: Linear mass density
μ = m/L
  • μ = linear mass density (kg m−1)
  • m = mass (kg)
  • L = length (m)
μ = (5.0 × 10−3)/0.72 = 6.9 × 10−3 kg m−1. Step 2: Wave speed on the wire v = √(T/μ) = √(60 / (6.9 × 10−3)) v ≈ √(8.70 × 103) ≈ 93 m s−1.
⚠ Common exam slip: using mass density ρ instead of linear mass density μ for strings. For a string/wire use μ = m/L (kg m−1), not kg m−3.

14.4.2 Speed of a Longitudinal Wave (Speed of Sound)

Longitudinal wave: particles oscillate parallel to wave travel. Sound in air moves as alternating compressions + rarefactions of tiny volume elements.

Elastic property: Bulk modulus

B = −ΔP / (ΔV/V)
  • B = bulk modulus (Pa)
  • ΔP = change in pressure (Pa)
  • ΔV/V = volumetric strain (dimensionless)
  • − sign: pressure ↑ when volume ↓
B has same dimensions as pressure; SI unit = pascal (Pa).

Inertial property: Mass density

For longitudinal waves, inertia is set by mass density ρ, with dimensions [M L−3].

Dimensional hint for speed dependence

By inspection, B/ρ has the right dimensions to build a speed (used later for speed of sound).

Speed of Longitudinal Waves in a Medium

For longitudinal waves (e.g. sound) in a medium, speed depends on:
  • Elastic property (resistance to compression)
  • Inertial property (mass density)

General result using Bulk Modulus

If the only relevant quantities are:
  • Bulk modulus B
  • Mass density ρ
Dimensional analysis →
v = C √(B/ρ) (Eq. 14.18)
  • v = speed of longitudinal wave in the medium
  • B = bulk modulus
  • ρ = mass density
  • C = dimensionless constant (not fixed by dimensional analysis)
Exact derivation (not just dimensional) gives C = 1 → so:
v = √(B/ρ) (Eq. 14.19)
  • v = speed of longitudinal wave
  • B = bulk modulus
  • ρ = mass density

Longitudinal waves in a solid bar (linear medium) and Young’s modulus

For a linear medium like a solid bar:
  • Lateral expansion ≈ negligible
  • So it’s basically longitudinal strain only
  • Relevant elastic constant: Young’s modulus Y (same dimensions as bulk modulus)
Same story: dimensional form like Eq. (14.18), and exact derivation again gives constant 1.
v = √(Y/ρ) (Eq. 14.20)
  • v = speed of longitudinal wave in a solid bar
  • Y = Young’s modulus of the bar material
  • ρ = density of the bar material

Why sound travels faster in solids/liquids than in gases

  • Solids + liquids are much harder to compress than gases ⇒ B is much bigger
  • They also have bigger ρ than gases (which by itself would lower speed)
  • But the jump in B is way larger than the jump in ρ
From v = √(B/ρ): big B dominates ⇒ v is higher in solids/liquids than in gases.

Speed of Sound in an Ideal Gas: Newton’s Formula and Laplace Correction

Ideal gas equation used

PV = NkBT (Eq. 14.21)
  • P = gas pressure
  • V = gas volume
  • N = number of molecules in volume V
  • kB = Boltzmann constant
  • T = absolute temperature (K)

Isothermal assumption (Newton)

For an isothermal change (T constant): from PV = constant
  • Small change gives: VΔP + PΔV = 0
  • So: ΔP / (ΔV/V) = −P
Bulk modulus definition (from earlier):
  • B = − ΔP / (ΔV/V)
So for isothermal changes in an ideal gas:
  • B = P
v = √(P/ρ) (Eq. 14.22, Newton’s formula)
  • v = sound speed (longitudinal) in an ideal gas
  • P = gas pressure (assuming isothermal variations)
  • ρ = gas density
Eq. (14.22) is Newton’s formula for sound speed in a gas.

Worked Example 14.4: Speed of sound in air at STP using Newton’s formula

Given:
  • At STP: 1 mole occupies 22.4 L = 22.4 × 10−3 m3
  • Mass of 1 mole of air = 29.0 × 10−3 kg
Step 1: Density of air at STP
ρ0 = (mass of 1 mole)/(volume of 1 mole at STP)
  • ρ0 = density of air at STP
Substitute: ρ0 = (29.0 × 10−3 kg)/(22.4 × 10−3 m3) = 1.29 kg m−3 Step 2: Speed using Newton’s formula Using Eq. (14.22), predicted speed of sound in air at STP:
v = 280 m s−1 (Eq. 14.23)
  • v = predicted sound speed in air at STP (Newton: isothermal)
Prediction 280 m s−1 is about 15% smaller than experiment: 331 m s−1.

Why Newton’s formula is wrong: sound propagation is adiabatic, not isothermal

  • Newton assumed pressure variations in sound are isothermal.
  • Laplace: variations are too fast for heat flow to keep T constant.
  • So the changes are adiabatic, not isothermal.

Adiabatic relation and adiabatic bulk modulus

For an ideal gas in an adiabatic process:
PVγ = constant
  • P = pressure
  • V = volume
  • γ = specific heat ratio = Cp/Cv
Differentiating: D(PVγ) = 0 gives:
  • Pγ Vγ−1 dV + Vγ dP = 0
Using bulk modulus, the adiabatic bulk modulus is:
Bad = γP
  • Bad = adiabatic bulk modulus
  • γ = Cp/Cv
  • P = equilibrium pressure
So the correct sound speed in an ideal gas:
v = √(γP/ρ) (Eq. 14.24)
  • v = sound speed in an ideal gas (adiabatic)
  • γ = ratio of specific heats
  • P = pressure
  • ρ = density
Replacing Newton’s isothermal result with the adiabatic one is the Laplace correction.
For air: γ = 7/5. Using Eq. (14.24) at STP:
  • v = 331.3 m s−1 (matches the measured value)
Exam pitfall: Don’t use v = √(P/ρ) for air unless the question clearly says isothermal. For sound in gases, assume adiabatic → use v = √(γP/ρ).

14.5 Principle of Superposition of Waves

Principle of superposition: If 2 (or more) waves overlap in a medium, the resultant displacement at a point = the algebraic sum of the individual displacements.

Physical picture: two pulses crossing

  • Two pulses travelling opposite ways cross and then keep their identities afterwards.
  • While overlapping: net displacement of the medium = sum of the two displacements.
  • Since displacement can be +/−, they can reinforce or cancel.
  • For equal + opposite pulses, overlap can give complete cancellation (zero displacement everywhere during overlap).

Mathematical statement

y(x,t) = y1(x,t) + y2(x,t) (Eq. 14.25)
  • y(x,t) = net displacement
  • y1(x,t), y2(x,t) = displacements from each wave alone
For n waves moving in the medium with wave functions:
  • y1 = f1(x − vt), y2 = f2(x − vt), … , yn = fn(x − vt)
y = f1(x − vt) + f2(x − vt) + … + fn(x − vt) = ∑i=1n fi(x − vt) (Eq. 14.26)
  • fi(x − vt) = shape of the i-th pulse travelling at speed v
  • v = wave speed
Superposition is the foundation of interference.

Interference of Two Harmonic Travelling Waves (Same ω and k)

Assumptions for the interference derivation

  • Two harmonic travelling waves on a stretched string.
  • Same angular frequency ω and wave number k ⇒ same wavelength λ + same wave speed.
  • Equal amplitudes a.
  • Both travel in the +x direction.
  • They differ only by initial phase (phase difference ϕ).

Individual waves

y1(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt) (Eq. 14.27)
  • a = amplitude
  • k = wave number
  • ω = angular frequency
y2(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt + ϕ) (Eq. 14.28)
  • ϕ = phase difference (initial phase of wave 2 relative to wave 1)

Resultant wave by superposition

y(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt) + a sin(kx − ωt + ϕ) (Eq. 14.29)
  • y(x,t) = net displacement
Use trig identity for sin(A) + sin(B):
  • sin A + sin B = 2 sin((A+B)/2) cos((A−B)/2)
So we get:
y(x,t) = 2a cos(ϕ/2) sin(kx − ωt + ϕ/2) (Eq. 14.31)
  • 2a cos(ϕ/2) = amplitude of resultant wave
  • ϕ/2 = initial phase shift of resultant wave (relative to y1)
Resultant is still a harmonic travelling wave in +x with the same ω, k, λ — only the amplitude changes with phase difference ϕ.

Amplitude as a function of phase difference

A(ϕ) = 2a cos(ϕ/2) (Eq. 14.32)
  • A(ϕ) = resultant amplitude
  • a = amplitude of each wave
  • ϕ = phase difference

Special cases: constructive and destructive interference

1) In phase (ϕ = 0):
  • cos(0/2) = 1 ⇒ A = 2a (maximum)
y(x,t) = 2a sin(kx − ωt) (Eq. 14.33)
  • Resultant amplitude = 2a (largest possible)
Eq. (14.33) = constructive interference (amplitudes add).
2) Completely out of phase (ϕ = π):
  • cos(π/2) = 0 ⇒ A = 0
y(x,t) = 0 (Eq. 14.34)
  • Net displacement is zero everywhere, all the time
Eq. (14.34) = destructive interference (amplitudes cancel).
Common mistake: don’t say “waves disappear.” In destructive interference the net displacement becomes zero due to cancellation; the individual waves still exist and pass through each other.

14.6 Reflection of Waves (Introduction)

Up to now: waves in an unbounded medium. When a pulse/wave meets a boundary:
  • If the boundary is rigid, the pulse/wave is reflected.

Reflection of Waves at Boundaries: Phase Change

Reflection at a boundary depends on whether the end point is constrained (rigid) or free to move (open/free). This sets the phase change of the reflected wave.

Rigid boundary (fixed end)

At a rigid boundary, displacement at the boundary is zero at all times (boundary condition) ⇒ reflected wave must cancel the incident wave at the end. Result: reflection causes a phase change of π (180°) = inversion.
Physical reasoning (must know):
  • Boundary-condition argument: end can’t move ⇒ incident + reflected must always sum to zero at the boundary ⇒ must be out of phase by π.
  • Newton’s Third Law argument: pulse pushes on wall; wall pushes back equally/oppositely on string ⇒ inverted reflected pulse (phase shift π).
  • Example: echo = reflection by a rigid boundary.
Incident travelling wave: y(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt) a = amplitude k = wave number (= 2π/λ) ω = angular frequency (= 2πν) x = position coordinate t = time
Reflected wave at a rigid boundary: yr(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt + π) = −a sin(kx − ωt) (14.35) yr = reflected wave displacement +π indicates phase reversal (inversion)
At the rigid boundary, superposition gives zero displacement at all times: y = yi + yr = 0 at the boundary (fixed-end condition).

Open boundary (free end)

At a completely free (non-rigid) boundary, reflected pulse has the same phase as the incident pulse, and the same amplitude (if no energy loss). Result: no phase change on reflection.
  • Net displacement at the boundary: incident + reflected add with same sign ⇒ boundary displacement can reach 2× the amplitude of each pulse (equal amplitudes, no dissipation).
  • Example: open end of an organ pipe acts like a non-rigid (open) boundary.
Reflected wave at an open boundary: yr(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt + 0) = a sin(kx − ωt) (14.36) 0 phase shift = no inversion
Exam trap: “Open end” ≠ “wave disappears.” It reflects, just without phase reversal (fixed end reflects with π shift).

Partial reflection, transmission, and refraction (qualitative)

If the boundary is not perfectly rigid or is an interface between two different elastic media, the incident wave usually splits:
  • Part reflected
  • Part transmitted into the second medium
If the wave hits the interface obliquely, the transmitted wave is the refracted wave. Laws: incident + refracted obey Snell’s law; incident + reflected obey the usual laws of reflection.

Standing Waves and Normal Modes (14.6.1)

Standing (stationary) waves = steady patterns formed by repeated reflections at boundaries (e.g. string fixed at both ends; air column in open/closed pipes). They come from superposition of two waves with the same amplitude and same wavelength, travelling in opposite directions.

Formation of a standing wave by superposition

Take two harmonic travelling waves with equal amplitude a and same k, ω, moving opposite ways (phase constant φ = 0):
y1(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt) y2(x,t) = a sin(kx + ωt) y1 travels in +x direction; y2 travels in −x direction
By superposition: y(x,t) = y1(x,t) + y2(x,t) Use trig identity:
Identity: sin(A + B) + sin(A − B) = 2 sin A cos B A, B = angles (choose to match kx and ωt terms)
Standing wave equation: y(x,t) = 2a sin(kx) cos(ωt)   (14.37) 2a sin(kx) = amplitude depends on position cos(ωt) = time oscillation (same ω everywhere)
Travelling vs standing: travelling wave has (kx − ωt) or (kx + ωt) together. In (14.37) for standing waves, kx and ωt appear separately.

Properties of standing waves: amplitude, phase, nodes and antinodes

  • Amplitude depends on position: A(x) = 2a sin(kx) → different points have different amplitudes.
  • Same angular frequency everywhere: every element oscillates with angular frequency ω (so same time period).
  • No phase difference between elements: the string vibrates in phase (except points with zero amplitude).
  • No net propagation: pattern doesn’t move left/right → “standing/stationary”.
Node: point where amplitude = 0 (no motion). Antinode: point where amplitude is maximum.
Common mistake: nodes aren’t “times”. They are fixed positions with zero displacement for all time.

Positions of nodes

From (14.37), amplitude ∝ sin(kx). Nodes are where amplitude = 0:
Node condition: sin(kx) = 0 ⇒ kx = nπ,   n = 0, 1, 2, 3, … n = node index (integer)
Using k = 2π/λ:
Node positions: x = n(λ/2),   n = 0, 1, 2, 3, …   (14.38) λ = wavelength of the component travelling waves
Node spacing: distance between successive nodes = λ/2.

Positions of antinodes

Antinodes happen where |sin(kx)| is max, i.e. |sin(kx)| = 1:
Antinode condition: |sin(kx)| = 1 ⇒ kx = (n + 1/2)π,   n = 0, 1, 2, 3, …
Using k = 2π/λ:
Antinode positions: x = (n + 1/2)(λ/2),   n = 0, 1, 2, 3, …   (14.39)
Antinode spacing: distance between successive antinodes = λ/2.

Normal Modes for a String Fixed at Both Ends

Normal modes (natural modes): only certain discrete standing-wave patterns are allowed because boundary conditions restrict possible wavelengths + frequencies. Unlike a travelling wave, which (in principle) can have any frequency.

Boundary conditions and allowed wavelengths

String length = L, fixed at both ends: x = 0 and x = L.
  • Fixed ends must be nodes.
  • x = 0 is automatically a node (from x = nλ/2 with n = 0).
  • x = L must also satisfy the node condition.
Fixed-end condition: L = n(λ/2),   n = 1, 2, 3, …   (14.40) Start n at 1 since n = 0 → L = 0 (not a real mode)
Allowed wavelengths (string fixed at both ends): λ = 2L/n,   n = 1, 2, 3, …   (14.41) n = mode number (harmonic number)

Allowed frequencies (harmonics)

Use ν = v/λ, with λ = 2L/n:
Allowed frequencies: ν = n v/(2L),   n = 1, 2, 3, …   (14.42) ν = frequency of the nth mode v = wave speed on the string (set by medium properties) L = string length
Fundamental (1st harmonic): n = 1 → lowest natural frequency ν1 = v/(2L) 2nd harmonic: n = 2; 3rd harmonic: n = 3; etc. Write harmonics as νn, n = 1, 2, …

Superposition of modes and musical relevance

  • A real string usually vibrates as a superposition of several normal modes, not just one.
  • Some modes get excited more strongly than others.
  • Musical instruments (e.g. sitar, violin): where you pluck/bow affects which harmonics stand out.

Standing Waves in Air Columns (One End Closed, One End Open)

Closed end vs open end (air column)
  • Closed end (touching water) → displacement node (displacement min/≈0) + pressure antinode (pressure change max).
  • Open end → displacement antinode (displacement max) + pressure node (pressure change min).

Boundary conditions and allowed wavelengths

Take closed end as x = 0. Node at x = 0 is automatically satisfied. Other end at x = L must be an antinode, so the standing-wave condition is:
Length–wavelength condition (one end closed, one end open) L = (n + 1/2) (λ/2), for n = 0, 1, 2, 3, … Symbols: L = length of the air column λ = wavelength of the standing-wave mode n = mode index (integer starting from 0 for this case)
Rearrange → wavelengths are limited to:
Allowed wavelengths (one end closed, one end open) λ = 2L/(n + 1/2), for n = 0, 1, 2, 3, … (Eq. 14.43) Symbols: λ = wavelength of a permitted normal mode L = pipe length n = mode index (0, 1, 2, …)

Normal modes (natural frequencies) and harmonics

Corresponding normal-mode (natural) frequencies:
Allowed frequencies (one end closed, one end open) ν = (n + 1/2) (v/(2L)), for n = 0, 1, 2, 3, … (Eq. 14.44) Symbols: ν = frequency of the mode v = speed of sound in the medium (air) L = pipe length n = mode index (0, 1, 2, …)
  • Fundamental is n = 0 ⇒ v/(4L).
  • Only odd harmonics show up: 3v/(4L), 5v/(4L), … (odd multiples of the fundamental).
  • Figures (idea): closed end = node, open end = antinode; so odd harmonics only.

Open pipe (both ends open): harmonic content

Pipe open at both ends: each end is a displacement antinode ⇒ gives all harmonics (1st, 2nd, 3rd, …).

Forced oscillations and resonance (connection)

Strings + air columns can have forced oscillations. If the driving frequency ≈ one of the natural frequencies, you get resonance (very large amplitude).

Normal modes of a circular membrane (tabla) — boundary condition

  • Circular membrane clamped at the rim ⇒ boundary condition: displacement = 0 all along the circumference (rim doesn’t move).
  • Mode frequencies are harder because waves are 2D, but the core idea stays: boundary conditions → standing waves/normal modes.

Example 14.5: Resonance of a 30.0 cm pipe with a 1.1 kHz source

Given: L = 30.0 cm = 0.30 m, source frequency = 1.1 kHz = 1100 Hz, speed of sound v = 330 m s−1. (A) Pipe open at both ends
Open pipe: fundamental frequency ν1 = v/(2L) Symbols: ν1 = first harmonic (fundamental) frequency v = speed of sound L = length of pipe
Open pipe: nth harmonic νn = n v/(2L), for n = 1, 2, 3, … Symbols: νn = frequency of nth harmonic n = harmonic number (1, 2, 3, …) v = speed of sound L = length of pipe
Compute: 2L = 0.60 m, so νn = n × 330 / 0.60 = 550 n Hz. Match 1100 Hz: 1100 = 550 n ⇒ n = 2.
So the 1.1 kHz source resonates with the 2nd harmonic of the open pipe.
(B) If one end of the same pipe is closed
Pipe closed at one end: fundamental frequency ν1 = v/(4L) Symbols: ν1 = fundamental frequency for closed pipe v = speed of sound L = length of pipe
For L = 0.30 m: ν1 = 330/(4×0.30) = 330/1.2 = 275 Hz. Source multiple: 1100/275 = 4 ⇒ 4th multiple of the fundamental.
  • Closed-at-one-end pipe supports odd harmonics only: 1st, 3rd, 5th, … (i.e., 1×, 3×, 5× of v/(4L)).
  • “4th multiple” is not allowed ⇒ no resonance when one end is closed.
Exam tip: For closed–open pipes, formulas often use mode index n = 0 in (n + 1/2), but harmonic talk is usually odd only (1st, 3rd, 5th...). Don’t mix the counting styles.

Beats

Beats happen due to interference (superposition) of two harmonic sound waves with close (but not equal) frequencies.
  • What you hear: pitch ≈ average of the two frequencies.
  • What changes: intensity (loudness) waxes & wanes at a rate = difference of the frequencies.
  • Use in tuning: musicians adjust until no beats (frequencies match).

Mathematical description (superposition of close frequencies)

Take a fixed point (say x = 0). Two harmonic longitudinal displacement waves have equal amplitude a and angular frequencies ω1, ω2 (with ω1 slightly larger). Choose phase suitably, then:
Two close-frequency waves (Eq. 14.45) s1 = a cos(ω1 t),    s2 = a cos(ω2 t) Symbols: s1, s2 = longitudinal displacements due to each wave a = (equal) amplitude of each wave ω1, ω2 = angular frequencies (rad s−1) t = time
By superposition: s = s1 + s2. Using cosA + cosB = 2cos[(A−B)/2] cos[(A+B)/2], we get:
Resultant displacement showing beats (Eq. 14.46–14.47) s = 2a cos[(ω1−ω2)t/2] cos[(ω1+ω2)t/2] Equivalently: s = [2a cos(ωb t)] cos(ωa t) Symbols: ωb = (ω1 − ω2)/2 (beat angular frequency parameter) ωa = (ω1 + ω2)/2 (average angular frequency)
  • If |ω1 − ω2| ≪ ω1, ω2, then ωa ≫ ωb.
  • Fast oscillation: angular frequency ≈ ωa (average). Slow amplitude envelope: controlled by cos(ωb t).
  • Max amplitude when cos(ωb t) = ±1 → envelope magnitude = 2a.
  • Intensity waxes & wanes at angular frequency 2ωb = ω1 − ω2.

Beat frequency

Since ω = 2πν, beat frequency becomes:
Beat frequency (Eq. 14.48) νbeat = ν1 − ν2 (magnitude taken in practice) Symbols: νbeat = number of beats per second (Hz) ν1, ν2 = frequencies of the two sources (Hz)
Example shown: 11 Hz and 9 Hz → beats at |11 − 9| = 2 Hz.
Common mistake: don’t use average frequency as beat frequency. Average = perceived pitch, difference = beat rate.

Example 14.6: Beats between two sitar strings

Given: String A: νA = 427 Hz. Initially beats = 5 Hz. Increase tension of B → beat frequency drops to 3 Hz. Find original νB. Key reasoning: Higher tension → higher frequency. If originally νB > νA, increasing νB increases |νB − νA|, so beats would increase. But beats decrease ⇒ originally νB < νA. Initial beats: νA − νB = 5 Hz ⇒ νB = 427 − 5 = 422 Hz.

Musical Pillars (Application of Vibrations)

Some temple pillars give musical notes when gently tapped because of mechanical vibrations. The vibration depends on:
  • Elasticity of the stone
  • Density of the stone
  • Shape (geometry) of the pillar

Classification of musical pillars

  • Shruti Pillar: basic notes (“swaras”) — Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni, Sa.
  • Gana Thoongal: generates basic tunes that form “ragas”.
  • Laya Thoongal: produces “taal” (beats) when tapped.
  • Nellaiappar temple pillars are a mix of Shruti and Laya types.

Nellaiappar temple note (context)

Archaeologists date Nellaiappar temple to the 7th century, built under successive rulers of the Pandyan dynasty. Similar musical pillars exist at Hampi, Kanyakumari, and Thiruvananthapuram, and are described as unique with no claimed parallel elsewhere.

Waves: Summary of Core Results

Mechanical waves and Newton’s laws

Mechanical waves only exist in a material medium. They follow Newton’s laws: particles oscillate because of elastic restoring forces + inertia.

Transverse and longitudinal waves

Transverse wave: medium particles oscillate perpendicular to wave direction. Longitudinal wave: medium particles oscillate along (parallel to) wave direction.

Progressive (travelling) wave

Progressive wave: wave that moves from one point of the medium to another. Key idea: it transports energy through the medium.

Sinusoidal progressive wave: displacement equation and phase

Sinusoidal wave travelling in +x direction: y(x,t) = a sin (kx − ωt + φ) a = amplitude (max displacement) k = angular wave number ω = angular frequency (kx − ωt + φ) = phase φ = phase constant / phase angle
Sign check: (kx − ωt) means wave travels in +x. If you see kx + ωt, that’s propagation in −x.

Wavelength (λ)

Wavelength λ (progressive wave): distance between two consecutive points with the same phase (at a fixed time). Standing wave: λ = 2 × (distance between consecutive nodes) or 2 × (distance between consecutive antinodes).

Time period (T) and angular frequency (ω)

Period T: time for any medium element to complete one full oscillation.
T = 2π/ω T = time period (s) ω = angular frequency (rad s⁻¹)

Frequency (ν) and its relation to ω

Frequency ν: oscillations per second; ν = 1/T.
ν = ω/(2π) ν = frequency (Hz) ω = angular frequency (rad s⁻¹)

Wave speed relations

v = λ/T = λν = ω/k v = wave speed λ = wavelength T = time period ν = frequency ω = angular frequency k = angular wave number
Common exam slip: don’t mix up ν (frequency) with v (speed). Also: ω is in rad s⁻¹, not Hz.

Speed of transverse waves on a stretched string

v = √(T/μ) v = transverse wave speed on string T = tension in string (N) μ = linear mass density (kg m⁻¹)
For a given string: speed depends on tension + mass per unit length. Not on how hard/fast you shake it.

Sound waves and speeds in fluids, solids, and gases

Sound waves are longitudinal mechanical waves. They travel through solids, liquids, and gases.
Speed of sound in a fluid: v = √(B/ρ) v = speed of sound B = bulk modulus of the fluid ρ = density of the fluid
Speed of longitudinal waves in a metallic bar: v = √(Y/ρ) v = longitudinal wave speed in bar Y = Young’s modulus of the material ρ = density
Speed of sound in a gas (using B = γP): v = √(γP/ρ) v = speed of sound in gas γ = ratio of specific heats (Cp/Cv) P = pressure of gas ρ = density of gas

Superposition, Interference, Reflection, Standing Waves, Resonance, Beats

Principle of superposition

Superposition principle: if 2+ waves travel together in the same medium, the displacement at any point = algebraic sum of displacements from each wave.
y = Σ (from i = 1 to n) yi = Σ (from i = 1 to n) fi(x − vt) y = resultant displacement yi = displacement due to i-th wave fi(x − vt) = travelling-wave form for i-th wave n = number of waves v = wave speed in the medium

Interference of two sinusoidal waves (same direction)

Interference: superposition effect → can give reinforcement or cancellation depending on relative phase.
Here: two sinusoidal waves on the same string, travelling in the same direction, same amplitude a, same frequency, but with phase difference (phase constant) φ.
y(x,t) = 2a cos(φ/2) sin(kx − ωt + φ/2) y(x,t) = resultant displacement a = amplitude of each wave φ = phase difference (phase constant between the waves) k = angular wave number ω = angular frequency
Constructive: φ = 0 or any integer multiple of 2π → in phase → amplitude = 2a. Destructive: φ = π → out of phase → complete cancellation (resultant amplitude 0) if amplitudes are equal.

Reflection at boundaries: rigid vs open end

A travelling wave reflects at a boundary. Reflection can be with/without phase reversal depending on the boundary condition.
Incident wave: yi(x,t) = a sin(kx − ωt) yi = incident displacement a = amplitude k = angular wave number ω = angular frequency
Reflected at rigid boundary / closed end (phase reversal): yr(x,t) = −a sin(kx + ωt) yr = reflected displacement Minus sign ⇒ phase change of π (inversion)
Reflected at open boundary (no phase change): yr(x,t) = a sin(kx + ωt) yr = reflected displacement
Exam cue: rigid/closed end → phase reversal; open end → no phase reversal.

Standing waves from two identical opposite-travelling waves

Standing waves: interference of two identical waves travelling in opposite directions → fixed nodes + antinodes.
Standing wave on a string with fixed ends: y(x,t) = [2a sin(kx)] cos(ωt) y(x,t) = displacement a = amplitude of each travelling wave k = angular wave number ω = angular frequency
Node: fixed point with zero displacement. Antinode: fixed point with maximum displacement.
Distance between consecutive nodes = λ/2; between consecutive antinodes = λ/2.

Normal modes (string fixed at both ends)

Normal modes: allowed standing-wave shapes (and frequencies) that satisfy the boundary conditions.
String length L, fixed at both ends: νn = n(v/2L), n = 1, 2, 3, … νn = nth mode frequency v = wave speed on string L = string length n = mode number (harmonic number)
Fundamental / 1st harmonic: n = 1 (lowest frequency). 2nd harmonic: n = 2; higher harmonics: n = 3, 4, …

Normal modes (pipe closed at one end, open at the other)

Pipe (air column) length L, one end closed + other open: νn = (n + 1/2)(v/2L), n = 0, 1, 2, 3, … νn = nth allowed frequency v = speed of sound in the pipe L = length of air column n = mode index starting from 0
Lowest frequency: ν = v/(4L) = fundamental mode / first harmonic.

Resonant frequencies and resonance idea

A string (length L) fixed at both ends, an air column closed at one end + open at the other, or a pipe open at both ends vibrates only at certain frequencies called normal modes. Each normal-mode frequency is a resonant frequency of the system.

Beats

Beats: superpose two waves with slightly different frequencies ν1, ν2 (similar amplitudes) → amplitude rises/falls periodically.
νbeat = V1 − V2 (use magnitude: |V1 − V2|) νbeat = beat frequency V1, V2 = frequencies of the two waves
Beat frequency = difference in frequencies; always give it as a positive value.

Points to Ponder (Conceptual Essentials for Exams)

Wave vs bulk motion of matter

A wave is not the whole-medium moving along. Example: wind transports air, but a sound wave is moving compressions + rarefactions with no large-scale net air transport.

Energy transfer, not matter transfer

Waves transfer energy point-to-point; they don’t transfer the medium’s matter overall.

Why mechanical waves transfer energy

Mechanical waves transfer energy because neighbouring parts of the medium are coupled by elastic restoring forces.

Which media support transverse vs longitudinal waves

Transverse waves need shear modulus (can resist shear) → only in such media (not in fluids in bulk). Longitudinal waves need bulk modulus → possible in solids, liquids, gases.

Progressive vs stationary waves: phase and amplitude patterns

Harmonic progressive wave (fixed frequency): same amplitude for all particles, but phase changes with position (so different phases at one instant). Stationary (standing) wave: particles between two nodes have the same phase at an instant, but amplitude depends on position (nodes: zero amplitude).

Wave speed depends on medium, not source speed

For an observer at rest in the medium: mechanical wave speed v depends only on medium properties (mainly elasticity + mass density), and not on the source’s speed.
