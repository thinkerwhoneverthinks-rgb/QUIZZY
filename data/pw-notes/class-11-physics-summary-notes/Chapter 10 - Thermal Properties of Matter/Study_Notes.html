Ch10: Thermal Properties of Matter

Introduction

Thermal properties of matter = heat + temperature, how we measure them, what heating does (ΔT, expansion, change of state), and how heat flows.
  • Everyday “hot” vs “cold” feels obvious, but physics needs precise definitions.
  • Heat flow explains real-life stuff: heating an iron ring so it fits; wind direction changes near a beach after sunset (both due to temperature differences causing effects).
  • During boiling/freezing of water, temperature may stay constant even while lots of heat enters/leaves (covered later under change of state).

Temperature and Heat

Temperature: meaning and limitations of touch

Temperature = a relative measure/indication of how hot or cold a body is.
  • If body A has higher temperature than body B, A is hotter.
  • “Hot” and “cold” are relative terms (like “tall”/“short”).
  • Touch can sense temperature, but it’s unreliable + has a limited range → not good enough for scientific measurement.

Heat: energy transfer due to temperature difference

Heat = energy transferred between systems (or system ↔ surroundings) because of a temperature difference.
  • If a body and surroundings are at different temperatures, heat transfer happens until both reach the same temperature (thermal equilibrium).
  • Direction of heat flow:
    • Always from higher temperature → lower temperature.
    • Example: ice-cold water warms as heat flows environment → water; hot tea cools as heat flows tea → environment.
Units
  • SI unit of heat (energy transferred): joule (J)
  • SI unit of temperature: kelvin (K)
  • Common practical unit: degree Celsius (°C)

Effects of heating

  • When you heat an object, one (or more) can happen:
    • Temperature rises
    • Expansion (dimensions/volume change)
    • Change of state (e.g. melting/boiling)
Exam tip: Don’t define heat as “something stored in a body.” Here, heat means energy in transit due to a temperature difference.

Measurement of Temperature

Thermometers and thermometric properties

A thermometer measures temperature using a material property that changes clearly with temperature = a thermometric property.
  • Common thermometric property: liquid volume changes with temperature.
  • In liquid-in-glass thermometers (mercury/alcohol), liquid volume changes approx linearly with temperature over a wide range.

Calibration: fixed reference points

  • To give temperatures numbers, thermometers must be calibrated using a temperature scale.
  • A standard temperature scale needs two fixed reference points.
  • No absolute “no-expansion” reference exists because all substances expand/contract with temperature.
  • So we choose fixed points from reproducible physical phenomena that always occur at the same temperature.
Ice point (freezing) and steam point (boiling) of water are convenient fixed points: temperatures where pure water freezes/boils at standard pressure.

Fahrenheit and Celsius scales

  • Fixed-point values:
    • Fahrenheit: ice point = 32 °F, steam point = 212 °F
    • Celsius: ice point = 0 °C, steam point = 100 °C
  • Equal intervals between ice and steam points:
    • Fahrenheit: 180 intervals (212 − 32)
    • Celsius: 100 intervals (100 − 0)
Fahrenheit ↔ Celsius conversion (Eq. 10.1) (tF − 32) / 180 = tC / 100 tF = temperature on Fahrenheit scale (°F) tC = temperature on Celsius scale (°C)
Common mistake: Don’t swap the 180 and 100 in Eq. (10.1) — they’re the number of divisions between freezing & boiling on each scale.

Ideal-Gas Equation and Absolute Temperature

Why gas thermometers are special

  • Liquid-in-glass thermometers can disagree away from fixed points → different liquids expand differently.
  • A gas thermometer gives the same readings no matter which gas is used (as long as the gas is low density).
  • Experiments: all low-density gases show the same expansion behaviour.

State variables and Celsius–Kelvin connection

  • A fixed mass of gas is described by pressure (P), volume (V), temperature (T).
  • Kelvin–Celsius link:
    • T = t + 273.15 (t in °C)

Boyle’s law (constant temperature)

Boyle’s law: fixed quantity of gas, constant temperature → PV = constant.
  • Due to Robert Boyle (1627–1691).

Charles’ law (constant pressure)

Charles’ law: fixed quantity of gas, constant pressure → V/T = constant.
  • Due to Jacques Charles (1747–1823).
  • Low-density gases obey Boyle’s + Charles’ laws well.

Ideal gas law and ideal-gas equation

  • Combine: PV = constant (at constant T) + V/T = constant (at constant P) ⇒ for fixed gas amount:
    • PV/T = constant
  • This combined relation is the ideal gas law.
Ideal-gas equation (Eq. 10.2): PV = μRT P = pressure (Pa) V = volume (m³) μ = number of moles (mol) R = universal gas constant T = absolute temperature (K)
Universal gas constant: R = 8.31 J mol−1 K−1 R = same proportionality constant for all low-density (ideal) gases
From PV = μRT, if gas amount is fixed (μ constant):
  • PV ∝ T (pressure × volume ∝ absolute temperature)

Constant-volume gas thermometer (P ∝ T)

  • Hold volume constant; from PV ∝ T:
    • P ∝ T
  • So a constant-volume gas thermometer measures temperature via pressure.
  • Pressure–temperature graph is a straight line (low-density gases, wide range).

Real gas deviation and absolute zero

  • Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at low temperatures.
  • But over a big range, the P–T relation stays ~linear → suggests pressure would hit zero at some low T (if gas stayed gaseous).
  • Extrapolate the straight-line P–T graph to the T-axis (where P = 0) ⇒
    • −273.15 °C
  • This temperature is absolute zero.
Absolute zero (−273.15 °C) underpins the Kelvin (absolute) scale: −273.15 °C ↔ 0 K.

Kelvin scale relation to Celsius

  • Unit size is the same: change of 1 K = change of 1 °C.
Kelvin–Celsius relation (Eq. 10.3): T = tC + 273.15 T = absolute temperature (K) tC = Celsius temperature (°C)
Exam tip: In PV = μRT, always use T in kelvin. Never plug in °C directly.

Thermal Expansion

Everyday observations illustrating thermal expansion

  • Metal lids on sealed bottles can stick; dip lid in hot water → lid expands → loosens → easier to open.
  • Liquids: mercury in a thermometer rises in warm water → liquid expands when heated.

Thermal Expansion: Basic Idea and Types

Thermal expansion = increase in a body’s dimensions when its temperature rises. Most substances expand on heating and contract on cooling.
  • Linear expansion: change in length.
  • Area (superficial) expansion: change in area.
  • Volume (cubical) expansion: change in volume.
Everyday effects (thermometers, balloons in hot/cold water, etc.) come from expansion/contraction.

Coefficient of Linear Expansion (Linear Expansivity)

For a long rod and small temperature change ΔT: fractional change in length ∝ ΔT.
Linear expansion (Eq. 10.4): Δl/l = αl ΔT Δl = change in length l = original length ΔT = change in temperature αl = coefficient of linear expansion (K−1), material property
  • αl depends on material; usually quoted over a range (here 0 °C → 100 °C).
  • Metals usually have higher αl than non-metals like glass → expand more for same ΔT.
  • From values given: copper expands ~5× more than pyrex glass for the same temperature rise.
  • Typical αl (×10−5 K−1): Aluminium 2.5, Brass 1.8, Iron 1.2, Copper 1.7, Silver 1.9, Gold 1.4, Glass (pyrex) 0.32, Lead 0.29.

Coefficient of Volume Expansion (Volume Expansivity)

For temperature change ΔT: fractional change in volume defines αv.
Volume expansion (Eq. 10.5): ΔV/V = αv ΔT ΔV = change in volume V = original volume ΔT = change in temperature αv = coefficient of volume expansion (K−1)
  • αv is a substance property but not strictly constant; generally varies with temperature.
  • For some substances (e.g. copper), αv is ~constant only at high temperature.
  • Typical αv (0–100 °C): Aluminium 7×10−5, Brass 6×10−5, Iron 3.55×10−5, Paraffin 58.8×10−5, Glass (ordinary) 2.5×10−5, Glass (pyrex) 1×10−5, Hard rubber 2.4×10−4, Invar 2×10−6, Mercury 18.2×10−5, Water 20.7×10−5, Alcohol (ethanol) 110×10−5 (all in K−1).
From values: ethanol has larger αv than mercury ⇒ ethanol expands more for the same ΔT. Some materials (e.g. pyrex glass, invar) have very low thermal expansion.

Anomalous Expansion of Water (0 °C to 4 °C)

Anomalous behaviour of water: from 0 °C to 4 °C, water contracts on heating (opposite of usual expansion).
  • Cool water from room temp down to 4 °C → volume decreases (so density increases).
  • Below 4 °C → volume increases (so density decreases).
Water has maximum density at 4 °C. Environmental significance: lakes/ponds freeze from the top first.
  • Cooling toward 4 °C: surface water loses energy → denser → sinks; warmer water rises (mixing continues).
  • Once surface water cools below 4 °C: it becomes less dense → stays on top → freezes there.
  • If water didn’t do this, water bodies would freeze bottom-up, threatening aquatic plant/animal life.

Thermal Expansion of Gases and Ideal Gas Result

  • At ordinary temperatures, gases expand much more than solids + liquids.
  • For liquids: αv is fairly independent of temperature.
  • For gases: αv is temperature-dependent.
For an ideal gas, αv at constant pressure comes straight from the ideal gas equation.
Ideal gas equation: PV = μRT P = pressure V = volume μ = number of moles R = gas constant T = absolute temperature (K)
At constant pressure (ideal gas): αv = (1/V)(ΔV/ΔT) = 1/T   (Eq. 10.6) αv = coefficient of volume expansion (constant pressure) T = absolute temperature (K)
  • At 0 °C (273 K): αv ≈ 3.7 × 10−3 K−1 (huge vs solids/liquids).
  • Since αv = 1/T, αv decreases when temperature increases.
  • At room temperature (constant pressure): αv ≈ 3300 × 10−6 K−1 (orders of magnitude bigger than typical liquids).
For gases, use absolute temperature (kelvin) in αv = 1/T. Using °C directly is a classic exam slip.

Relation Between Volume Expansivity and Linear Expansivity (Isotropic Solids)

For an isotropic solid (same expansion in all directions): volume expansivity links to linear expansivity.
Δl = αl l ΔT Δl = change in length of cube edge l = original edge length αl = linear expansivity ΔT = temperature change
For a cube: ΔV = (l + Δl)3 − l3 ≃ 3l2Δl   (Eq. 10.7) ΔV = change in volume l = original edge length Δl = small increase in edge length Approximation: ignore (Δl)2 and (Δl)3 since Δl ≪ l
ΔV/V = 3(Δl/l) = 3αlΔT   (Eq. 10.8) V = l3 (original volume)
Key relation: αv = 3αl   (Eq. 10.9) αv = volume expansivity αl = linear expansivity
αv = 3αl works only for uniform (isotropic) expansion + small ΔT (so higher-order terms stay negligible).

Thermal Stress (Expansion Prevented)

If a rod’s ends are rigidly fixed (can’t expand), it develops compressive strain. The resulting stress is thermal stress.
  • If free: strain would be Δl/l = αlΔT.
  • If prevented: that “would-be” strain is stored as elastic strain → produces stress.
Thermal strain (if free): ε = Δl/l = αlΔT ε = strain αl = linear expansivity ΔT = temperature change
Thermal stress (expansion prevented): σ = Y ε = Y αlΔT σ = stress (N m−2) Y = Young’s modulus (N m−2) ε = strain
Force due to thermal stress: ΔF = Aσ = AYαlΔT ΔF = compressive force developed A = cross-sectional area
Worked numerical (steel rail):
  • Given: l = 5 m, A = 40 cm2 = 40×10−4 m2, ΔT = 10 °C, αl(steel)=1.2×10−5 K−1, Y(steel)=2×1011 N m−2.
  • Strain: Δl/l = αlΔT = 1.2×10−5×10 = 1.2×10−4.
  • Stress: σ = Y(Δl/l) = 2×1011×1.2×10−4 = 2.4×107 N m−2.
  • Force: ΔF = Aσ = 2.4×107×(40×10−4) ≈ 1×105 N (order as given).
  • Interpretation: forces this big can bend rails when rails touch and expansion is constrained.

Example 10.1: Coefficient of Area Expansion

Derivation for a Rectangular Sheet: αA = 2αl

Coefficient of area expansion = (ΔA/A)/ΔT (for small ΔT).
Take a rectangle: length a, breadth b.
  • Initial area: A = ab
  • Heat by ΔT:
    • Δa = αlaΔT
    • Δb = αlbΔT
Area increase = 3 contributions:
  • ΔA1 = a(Δb)
  • ΔA2 = b(Δa)
  • ΔA3 = (Δa)(Δb) (tiny “corner” term)
So,
  • ΔA = aΔb + bΔa + (Δa)(Δb)
  • = a(αlbΔT) + b(αlaΔT) + (αlaΔT)(αlbΔT)
  • = αlabΔT(2 + αlΔT)
  • = αlAΔT(2 + αlΔT)
αl ~ 10−5 K−1, so αlΔT ≪ 2 ⇒ drop αlΔT in (2 + αlΔT).
ΔA ≈ 2αlAΔT ⇒ (ΔA/A)/ΔT = 2αl ΔA = change in area A = original area αl = linear expansivity ΔT = temperature change
Don’t delete (Δa)(Δb) immediately—first note it’s second order in the small quantity αlΔT, then neglect it.

Example 10.2: Heating an Iron Ring to Fit a Wooden Wheel

Using Linear Expansion on Diameter

Situation: heat an iron ring so its diameter increases enough to fit the wooden rim.
  • Rim diameter at 27 °C: 5.243 m
  • Iron ring diameter at 27 °C: 5.231 m
Linear expansion works for any linear size — including diameter.
LT2 = LT1[1 + αl(T2 − T1)] LT1 = length (here: diameter) at T1 LT2 = length (diameter) at T2 αl = coefficient of linear expansion (iron) = 1.20×10−5 K−1
Given: T1 = 27 °C, LT1 = 5.231 m, LT2 = 5.243 m. Substitute:
  • 5.243 = 5.231[1 + 1.20×10−5(T2 − 27)]
  • On solving: T2 = 218 °C.
Temperature change can be in °C or K (same number), but keep α in K−1 and stay consistent.

10.6 Specific Heat Capacity

Factors affecting heat required to raise temperature

Heating experiments (same heater; time taken ∝ heat supplied):
  • Same mass: double the temperature rise → about double the time ⇒ heat required ∝ ∆T.
  • Same temperature rise: double the mass → about double the time ⇒ heat required ∝ m.
  • Same mass + same ∆T: different substances take different times ⇒ heat required depends on nature of substance.
So heat needed to warm a substance depends on mass (m), temperature change (∆T), and the material.

Heat capacity (S)

Heat capacity tells how much the temperature of a given body changes when it absorbs (or gives out) heat.
(10.10) Heat capacity: S = ∆Q/∆T ∆Q: heat supplied to the substance ∆T: temperature change produced (from T to T + ∆T) S: heat capacity of that body (depends on amount + material)
Give the same ∆Q to equal masses of different substances → you get different ∆T values ⇒ each substance responds differently to heating.

Specific heat capacity (s)

Specific heat capacity = heat needed per unit mass to raise temperature by 1 unit (with no phase change). It’s a property of the substance: it controls how much T changes for a given heat absorbed / released.
  • Depends on nature of substance and its temperature.
  • Defined only when there is no phase change happening.
(10.11) Specific heat capacity: s = ∆Q/(m∆T) ∆Q: heat absorbed or given off m: mass of substance ∆T: temperature change produced s: specific heat capacity
SI unit of specific heat capacity: J kg–1 K–1.

Molar specific heat capacity (C)

If we measure amount of substance in moles (µ) instead of mass (kg), we use molar specific heat capacity.
(10.12) Molar specific heat capacity: C = ∆Q/(µ∆T) ∆Q: heat absorbed or given off µ: amount of substance (moles) ∆T: temperature change C: molar specific heat capacity
Like S, molar specific heat capacity C depends on the nature of the substance and its temperature. SI unit of molar specific heat capacity: J mol–1 K–1.

Molar specific heats of gases: Cp and Cv

For gases, you must state the heating condition because heat transfer can be at:
  • Constant pressure → molar specific heat at constant pressure, Cp
  • Constant volume → molar specific heat at constant volume, Cv
When using molar specific heats for gases, always say whether heating is at constant pressure or constant volume (more details later in thermodynamics).

Measured values: specific heat capacities (Table 10.3) and molar specific heats of gases (Table 10.4)

Specific heat capacity at room temperature + atmospheric pressure (J kg–1 K–1):
  • Aluminium: 900.0
  • Carbon: 506.5
  • Copper: 386.4
  • Iron: 450
  • Lead: 127.7
  • Silver: 236.1
  • Tungesten: 134.4
  • Mercury: 140
  • Ice: 2060
  • Glass: 840
  • Kerosene: 2118
  • Edible oil: 1965
  • Water: 4186.0
Molar specific heat capacities of some gases (J mol–1 K–1):
  • He: Cp=20.8, Cv=12.5
  • H2: Cp=28.8, Cv=20.4
  • N2: Cp=29.1, Cv=20.8
  • O2: Cp=29.4, Cv=21.1
  • CO2: Cp=37.0, Cv=28.5

Consequences of water having a high specific heat capacity

Water has the highest specific heat capacity in the list (4186 J kg–1 K–1), so for the same heat input its temperature rises less than most materials.
  • Coolant in car radiators: takes in lots of heat with only a small rise in temperature.
  • Hot water bags: stores lots of heat energy, releases it slowly.
  • Coastal climate: sea warms slower than land in summer → sea breeze feels cool.
  • Desert climate: land (lower s than water) heats fast by day, cools fast at night.
Exam tip: always link to the chain: high s → more heat needed for same ∆T → temperature changes slowly.

10.7 Calorimetry

Isolated system and heat exchange principle

Isolated system = system where no heat is exchanged with the surroundings. If parts of an isolated system are at different temperatures, heat flows from hotter → colder until thermal equilibrium.
Heat balance (isolated system): heat lost by hot part = heat gained by cold part.

Meaning of calorimetry

Calorimetry = measurement of heat. When a hot body touches a cold body:
  • Heat lost by hot body = heat gained by cold body
  • Provided no heat escapes to the surroundings (i.e. setup is effectively isolated).

Calorimeter: construction and function

Calorimeter = device used for heat measurements.
  • Made of a metal vessel + stirrer (same material; usually copper or aluminium).
  • Vessel kept inside a wooden jacket with insulating material (e.g. glass wool).
  • Outer jacket acts as a heat shield → reduces heat loss from inner vessel.
  • Has an opening to insert a mercury thermometer.
In calorimetry numericals, add heat absorbed by the calorimeter vessel (and stirrer if relevant) along with water, unless the question says to neglect it.

Example 10.3: Determination of specific heat capacity of aluminium using calorimetry

Principle: at steady state (final common temperature), Heat given by hot body = Heat absorbed by water + calorimeter (no heat loss to surroundings).
Given:
  • Mass of aluminium sphere, m1 = 0.047 kg
  • Initial temperature of Al sphere = 100 °C (kept long enough in boiling water)
  • Mass of copper calorimeter = 0.14 kg
  • Mass of water in calorimeter = 0.25 kg
  • Initial temperature of water + calorimeter = 20 °C
  • Final steady temperature = 23 °C
Temperature changes:
  • Aluminium cooling: ∆TAl = 100 − 23 = 77 °C
  • Water + calorimeter heating: ∆T = 23 − 20 = 3 °C
Let specific heat capacity of aluminium = sAl.
Heat exchanged: Heat lost by aluminium: QAl = m1sAl(100−23) Heat gained by water: Qw = mwsw(23−20) Heat gained by copper calorimeter: QCu = mCusCu(23−20) m1 = mass of aluminium sphere sAl = specific heat capacity of aluminium (unknown) mw = mass of water sw = specific heat capacity of water (= 4186 J kg–1 K–1) mCu = mass of copper calorimeter sCu = specific heat capacity of copper (= 386.4 J kg–1 K–1)
Heat balance equation: m1sAl(100−23) = mwsw(23−20) + mCusCu(23−20) Substitute:
  • Left: 0.047·sAl·77
  • Right: 0.25·4186·3 + 0.14·386.4·3
Right side:
  • Water: 0.25×4186×3 = 3139.5 J
  • Calorimeter: 0.14×386.4×3 = 162.288 J
  • Total gained = 3301.788 J
So: 0.047×77×sAl = 3301.788 0.047×77 = 3.619 sAl = 3301.788 / 3.619 ≈ 9.12×102 J kg–1 K–1 (≈ 912 J kg–1 K–1, close to tabulated 900).
  • Use consistent temperature differences: hot object → (Thot,initial − Tfinal); cold bodies → (Tfinal − Tcold,initial).
  • Include calorimeter heat gain (here copper) unless stated negligible.
  • Steady state here = final common temperature reached (no further temperature change).

Calorimetry Worked Example: Specific Heat Capacity of Aluminium (Method of Mixtures)

Principle (steady state / no heat loss): at thermal equilibrium, Heat lost by hot body = Heat gained by colder bodies (water + calorimeter).
Heat due to temperature change (no phase change): Q = m s ΔT Q = heat transferred (J) m = mass (kg) s = specific heat capacity (J kg−1 K−1) ΔT = temperature change (K or °C; same numerical value for differences)
Given (from the example):
  • Mass of aluminium sphere, mAl = 0.047 kg
  • Temperature fall of aluminium sphere, ΔT1 = 77 °C
  • Mass of water, m2 = 0.25 kg
  • Mass of calorimeter (copper), m3 = 0.14 kg
  • Initial temperature of water and calorimeter = 20 °C
  • Final temperature of mixture = 23 °C
  • Rise in temperature of water and calorimeter: ΔT2 = 23 − 20 = 3 °C
  • Specific heat capacity of water: sw = 4.18 × 103 J kg−1 K−1
  • Specific heat capacity of copper (calorimeter): scu = 0.386 × 103 J kg−1 K−1
Heat gained by water + calorimeter: Qgain = m2 sw ΔT2 + m3 scu ΔT2 = (m2sw + m3scu) ΔT2 = (0.25 × 4.18×103 + 0.14 × 0.386×103) × (3) Heat lost by aluminium sphere: Qloss = mAl sAl ΔT1 = 0.047 × sAl × 77
Equilibrium condition: Heat lost by aluminium sphere = Heat gained by water + Heat gained by calorimeter.
So, 0.047 × sAl × 77 = (0.25 × 4.18×103 + 0.14 × 0.386×103) × 3 From this, the example gives: sAl = 0.911 kJ kg−1 K−1
Exam tips / common mistakes:
  • Use temperature differences (ΔT), not absolute temperatures.
  • ΔT in °C and K are numerically identical, but s must match units (J kg−1 K−1).
  • Don’t miss the calorimeter heat gain (common exam error).
  • Steady state = thermal equilibrium reached; external heat loss neglected.

10.8 Change of State

States of matter: solid, liquid, gas. Change of state: shifting from one state to another (solid ↔ liquid, liquid ↔ gas) due to heat exchange with surroundings.

Heating Curve of Ice-Water System (Temperature–Time Graph)

What you notice when heating ice slowly (stir continuously + note temperature each minute):
  • As long as ice is still present, the system temperature shows no rise even though heat is supplied.
  • The heat supplied is used for state change (ice → water), not for increasing temperature.
During any phase change, temperature stays constant even while heat is absorbed/released, because energy goes into rearranging molecules (state), not increasing kinetic energy (temperature).

Melting (Fusion) and Freezing

Melting / fusion: solid → liquid. Freezing: liquid → solid.
Main feature: temperature remains constant until the whole solid melts. During melting, solid + liquid coexist in thermal equilibrium.
Melting point: the temperature at which solid and liquid phases coexist in thermal equilibrium. It is characteristic of the substance and depends on pressure.
Normal melting point: melting point at standard atmospheric pressure.

Regelation (Wire Through Ice Demonstration) and Skating

Experiment idea: put a metallic wire on an ice slab; hang heavy blocks (e.g., 5 kg) at both ends. Observation: wire cuts through the ice, but the slab doesn’t split.
Regelation: refreezing of melted ice when pressure is removed.
Why it happens:
  • Pressure under the wire becomes large → ice just below the wire melts at a lower temperature.
  • Once the wire passes, pressure reduces → the water freezes again.
Skating on snow/ice: high pressure under skates makes a thin water film → acts as a lubricant → smooth motion.

Vaporisation and Boiling Point

Vaporisation: liquid → vapour (gas).
Heating water after all ice has melted:
  • Temperature starts rising once all ice becomes water.
  • It rises up to about 100 °C, then becomes steady again while liquid changes to vapour.
  • During this, liquid + vapour coexist in thermal equilibrium.
Boiling point: the temperature at which liquid and vapour phases coexist in thermal equilibrium.

Boiling Process in a Flask (What You See and Why)

Setup: round-bottom flask > half filled with water; heat; fit thermometer + steam outlet through cork. What you observe (in order):
  • First: small bubbles appear due to dissolved air coming out.
  • Next: steam bubbles form at the bottom but condense in cooler upper water and vanish.
  • When the whole water reaches ~ 100 °C, steam bubbles reach the surface continuously → boiling starts.
  • Steam may be invisible inside; outside it condenses into tiny droplets → foggy look.

Effect of Pressure on Boiling Point (and Applications)

If the steam outlet is closed briefly: pressure inside rises → boiling stops for a while; extra heating is needed to start boiling again.
Boiling point ↑ when pressure ↑. Boiling point ↓ when pressure ↓.
Demo: lower pressure → lower boiling point:
  • Cool water to ~80 °C, remove thermometer + outlet, seal with airtight cork.
  • Invert the flask; pour ice-cold water on it.
  • Vapour condenses → pressure above water drops → water boils again at lower temperature.
Consequences:
  • Cooking on hills is difficult: atmospheric pressure low → boiling point low → cooking temperature low.
  • Pressure cooker: pressure high → boiling point high → faster cooking.
Normal boiling point: boiling point at standard atmospheric pressure.

Sublimation

Sublimation: solid → vapour directly (no liquid stage), and vice versa.
Examples: dry ice (solid CO2), iodine. During sublimation, solid + vapour coexist in thermal equilibrium.

Triple Point and Phase Diagram (P–T Diagram)

Phase change feature: temperature stays constant during change of state.
Phase diagram / P–T diagram: graph of temperature (T) vs pressure (P) for a substance.
What the phase diagram tells you:
  • It splits the P–T plane into 3 regions: solid, liquid, vapour.
  • Regions are separated by curves:
    • Sublimation curve (BO): solid + vapour coexist (equilibrium solid–vapour).
    • Fusion curve (AO): solid + liquid coexist (equilibrium solid–liquid).
    • Vaporisation curve (CO): liquid + vapour coexist (equilibrium liquid–vapour).
Triple point: the unique T and P where fusion, vaporisation, and sublimation curves meet and all 3 phases (solid, liquid, vapour) coexist in equilibrium.
Triple point of water: T = 273.16 K, P = 6.11 × 10−3 Pa. Phase diagrams are commonly shown for water and CO2.

10.8.1 Latent Heat

Latent heat (for a process): heat transferred per unit mass during a change of state.
Key idea (using ice):
  • Heat ice at −10 °C → temperature rises to 0 °C.
  • At 0 °C (melting point), adding heat does not increase temperature → it causes melting (state change).
  • After all ice melts, more heating raises the water temperature.
Same idea at boiling point:
  • At boiling point, extra heat causes vaporisation with no temperature rise.
Exam tip: latent heat applies only to the phase-change (constant-temperature) part and is defined per unit mass. Heating within a single phase uses specific heat capacity and gives a temperature change.

Latent Heat and Change of State

Latent heat (L) = heat needed per unit mass to change a substance’s state at constant temperature (during the phase change), at a stated pressure (usually 1 atm). Depends on: nature of the substance + pressure (values usually quoted at standard atmospheric pressure).
Heat for change of state: Q = mL (Eq. 10.13) Latent heat: L = Q/m Q = heat absorbed/released in phase change (J) m = mass changing state (kg) L = latent heat for that phase change (J kg-1)
SI unit: J kg-1 During melting/boiling etc.: temperature stays constant even though heat is absorbed/released.

Types of Latent Heat

Latent heat of fusion (Lf) = for solid ⇄ liquid Latent heat of vaporisation (Lv) = for liquid ⇄ gas Also called heat of fusion and heat of vaporisation.

Temperature vs Heat Graph for Water (at 1 atm)

Worked Example 10.4: Determining Heat of Fusion from Mixing

Problem: Mix 0.15 kg ice at 0 °C with 0.30 kg water at 50 °C. Final temperature = 6.7 °C. Find heat of fusion of ice. Given swater = 4186 J kg-1 K-1. Principle: Heat lost by hot water = heat gained by ice (melt + warm the meltwater), assuming no heat loss to container/surroundings. Heat lost by water (50.0 → 6.7 °C): Heat lost = m sw(θi − θf) = (0.30)(4186)(50.0 − 6.7) = 54376.14 J Heat to melt ice at 0 °C: Qmelt = m Lf = (0.15) Lf Heat to warm melted ice-water (0 → 6.7 °C): Qwarm = m sw(θf − θi) = (0.15)(4186)(6.7 − 0) = 4206.93 J Energy balance: 54376.14 = (0.15)Lf + 4206.93 Lf = (54376.14 − 4206.93)/0.15 = 3.34 × 105 J kg-1
Common exam errors (mixing + phase change):
  • Melting at 0 °C: must include mLf (no temperature rise while melting).
  • After melting: include heating of meltwater from 0 °C to final T using msΔT.
  • Keep temperature differences consistent; in energy balance handle signs properly, or use magnitudes but state directions clearly.

Worked Example 10.5: Heating Ice Below 0 °C to Steam at 100 °C

Problem: Heat needed to convert 3 kg of ice at −12 °C into steam at 100 °C (1 atm). Given:
  • sice = 2100 J kg-1 K-1
  • swater = 4186 J kg-1 K-1
  • Lf,ice = 3.35 × 105 J kg-1
  • Lsteam = 2.256 × 106 J kg-1
Method: split into 4 physical stages (find each Q, then add):
  1. Warm ice: −12 °C → 0 °C
  2. Melt at 0 °C: ice → water (still 0 °C)
  3. Warm water: 0 °C → 100 °C
  4. Vaporise at 100 °C: water → steam (still 100 °C)
Step 1: ice (−12 → 0 °C) Q1 = m siceΔT = (3)(2100)[0 − (−12)] = 75600 J Step 2: melt at 0 °C Q2 = m Lf = (3)(3.35 × 105) = 1005000 J Step 3: water (0 → 100 °C) Q3 = m swaterΔT = (3)(4186)(100) = 1255800 J Step 4: vaporise at 100 °C Q4 = m Lsteam = (3)(2.256 × 106) = 6768000 J Total heat: Q = Q1 + Q2 + Q3 + Q4 = 75600 + 1005000 + 1255800 + 6768000 = 9.1 × 106 J
Exam tip: For multi-stage heating, always check you’ve included:
  • Heating solid below melting point (use ssolid)
  • Latent heat at melting point
  • Heating liquid to boiling point (use sliquid)
  • Latent heat at boiling point
Miss one stage ⇒ big error (especially the vaporisation step).

Heat Transfer (Overview)

Heat transfer = energy transfer due to a temperature difference, either between two systems or within different parts of the same system. 3 modes:
  • Conduction
  • Convection
  • Radiation

Conduction

Conduction = heat transfer between two adjacent parts of the same body due to a temperature difference.
  • Idea/example: heat one end of a metal rod in a flame → the other end gets hot due to conduction along the rod.
  • Gases are poor thermal conductors.
  • Liquids have conductivity in between solids and gases.

Steady-State Heat Flow Through a Bar (Experimental Law)

Metallic bar: length L, uniform area A. Ends touch reservoirs at temperatures TC (hot) and TD (cold), with TC > TD. Sides fully insulated ⇒ no heat exchange through sides.
After some time, steady state happens:
  • Temperature drops uniformly from hot end → cold end.
  • Heat taken from hot reservoir per second = heat given to cold reservoir per second ⇒ constant heat current.
Rate of heat flow (heat current) by conduction (Eq. 10.14): H = K A (TC − TD) / L H = heat current (J s-1 or W) K = thermal conductivity (W m-1 K-1) A = cross-sectional area (m2) TC, TD = end temperatures (K or °C; only ΔT matters) L = length (m)
Thermal conductivity (K) = proportionality constant in conduction law; bigger K ⇒ faster heat conduction.
SI unit of K: J s-1 m-1 K-1 = W m-1 K-1. K changes slightly with temperature, but often treated as constant over normal ranges.

Thermal Conductivities: Comparative Understanding and Applications

  • Metals usually have large K ⇒ good conductors.
  • Wood, glass wool have small K ⇒ good insulators.
  • Copper-coated cooking pots: high K of copper spreads heat fast ⇒ uniform cooking.
  • Plastic foams: insulate mainly by trapping air pockets (gases are poor conductors).
  • Concrete roofs: homes get hot in summer since concrete’s K isn’t small enough; adding earth/foam insulation reduces heat transfer → cooler rooms.
  • Nuclear reactors: heat transfer must be very effective to remove huge fission energy fast enough to avoid overheating.

Thermal conductivities K (J s-1 m-1 K-1) of some materials:
  • Metals: Silver 406; Copper 385; Aluminium 205; Brass 109; Steel 50.2; Lead 34.7; Mercury 8.3
  • Non-metals: Insulating brick 0.15; Concrete 0.8; Body fat 0.20; Felt 0.04; Glass 0.8; Ice 1.6; Glass wool 0.04; Wood 0.12; Water 0.8
  • Gases: Air 0.024; Argon 0.016; Hydrogen 0.14

Problem (steady-state conduction, two rods in series): Find the temperature at the steel–copper junction (figure referenced). Data:

  • Length of copper rod = 10.0 cm
  • Temperature of furnace end = 300 °C
  • Temperature of other end = 0 °C

  • Kcopper = 385 J s-1 m-1 K-1

Steady-State Heat Conduction Through Composite Rods (Series Combination)

Why insulate the rods (Fig. 10.15 idea)? Insulation cuts heat loss from the sides, so heat flows only along the length (1-D heat flow). Steady state: at any cross-section, heat entering a small element = heat leaving. Otherwise its temperature would change (not steady). So the heat current is the same at every cross section along the whole composite rod.

Junction Temperature Condition (Two rods in series)

Two different materials joined end-to-end + sides insulated ⇒ in steady state, same heat current through both. Junction temperature comes from equating conduction rates of the two segments.
Equal heat currents through two segments (steel = 1, copper = 2): \(\dfrac{K_1 A_1 (T_\text{hot}-T)}{L_1}=\dfrac{K_2 A_2 (T-T_\text{cold})}{L_2}\) Symbols: \(K_1, K_2\): thermal conductivities of segment 1 and 2 \(A_1, A_2\): cross-sectional areas \(L_1, L_2\): lengths \(T_\text{hot}\): temperature at hot end \(T_\text{cold}\): temperature at cold end \(T\): steady-state junction temperature

Worked Result (Steel–Copper rod with given data)

Let junction temperature be \(T\) (steady state). Hot end \(=300^\circ\text{C}\), cold end \(=0^\circ\text{C}\): \(\dfrac{K_1 A_1 (300- T)}{L_1}=\dfrac{K_2 A_2 (T-0)}{L_2}\) Given: \(A_1=2A_2,\; L_1=15.0\text{ cm},\; L_2=10.0\text{ cm},\; K_1=50.2\text{ J s}^{-1}\text{ m}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1},\; K_2=385\text{ J s}^{-1}\text{ m}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}\). Substitute: \(\dfrac{50.2\times 2 \times (300-T)}{15}=\dfrac{385\times T}{10}\) Solve: \(T=44.4^\circ\text{C}\)
Exam tip: Composite rods (series) ⇒ always equate heat currents in each segment (steady state). Don’t equate temperature drops unless \(K A / L\) matches for the segments.

Example 10.7: Iron–Brass Compound Bar (End-to-end)

Given Data and Steady-State Condition

Iron bar: \(L_1=0.1\text{ m}\), \(A_1=0.02\text{ m}^2\), \(K_1=79\text{ W m}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}\) Brass bar: \(L_2=0.1\text{ m}\), \(A_2=0.02\text{ m}^2\), \(K_2=109\text{ W m}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}\) End temps: \(T_1=373\text{ K}\) (iron free end), \(T_2=273\text{ K}\) (brass free end) Let junction temperature = \(T_0\).
Series bars @ steady state: same heat current through each piece \(H=H_1=H_2\)

(i) Junction Temperature of the Two Bars

Conduction heat current: \(H_1=\dfrac{K_1A_1(T_1-T_0)}{L_1}\), \(\;\;H_2=\dfrac{K_2A_2(T_0-T_2)}{L_2}\) Symbols: \(H_1,H_2\): heat currents in bar 1 & 2 \(T_0\): junction temperature
Set \(H_1=H_2\): \(\dfrac{K_1 A_1 (T_1-T_0)}{L_1}=\dfrac{K_2 A_2 (T_0-T_2)}{L_2}\) Here \(A_1=A_2=A\) and \(L_1=L_2=L\) ⇒ \(K_1(T_1-T_0)=K_2(T_0-T_2)\)
Junction temp (if \(A_1=A_2\) and \(L_1=L_2\)): \(T_0=\dfrac{K_1T_1+K_2T_2}{K_1+K_2}\) Symbols: \(T_0\): junction temperature \(K_1,K_2\): thermal conductivities \(T_1,T_2\): end temperatures
Compute: \(T_0=\dfrac{79\times 373+109\times 273}{79+109}=315\text{ K}\)
Result (i): \(T_0=315\text{ K}\)

(ii) Equivalent Thermal Conductivity of the Compound Bar

Total length \(=L_1+L_2=2L\), area \(=A\). Define equivalent conductivity \(K'\) so same heat current \(H'\) flows for same \(T_1, T_2\).
Equivalent single-bar conduction: \(H'=\dfrac{K'A(T_1-T_2)}{L_1+L_2}=\dfrac{K'A(T_1-T_2)}{2L}\) Symbols: \(H'\): heat current in equivalent bar \(K'\): equivalent thermal conductivity \(A\): cross-sectional area \(T_1-T_2\): overall temperature difference \(2L\): total length
For two series bars with equal \(A\) and equal lengths \(L\):
Equivalent conductivity (series, equal \(L\) & equal \(A\)): \(K'=\dfrac{2K_1K_2}{K_1+K_2}\) Symbols: \(K_1,K_2\): conductivities of segments \(K'\): equivalent conductivity of combined length \(2L\)
Compute: \(K'=\dfrac{2\times 79\times 109}{79+109}=91.6\text{ W m}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}\)
Result (ii): \(K'=91.6\text{ W m}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}\)

(iii) Heat Current Through the Compound Bar

Heat current via equivalent conductivity: \(H'=\dfrac{K'A(T_1-T_2)}{2L}\) Symbols: \(H'\): heat current (W) \(K'\): equivalent conductivity \(A\): area \(T_1-T_2\): temperature difference \(2L\): total length
Substitute \(K'=91.6\text{ W m}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}\), \(A=0.02\text{ m}^2\), \(T_1-T_2=373-273=100\text{ K}\), \(2L=2\times 0.1=0.2\text{ m}\): \(H'=\dfrac{91.6\times 0.02\times 100}{0.2}=916.1\text{ W}\)
Result (iii): \(H'=916.1\text{ W}\)
Common mistake: In series, don’t “add conductivities”. Use same heat current + (generally) thermal resistance \(R=\dfrac{L}{KA}\). The special result \(K'=\dfrac{2K_1K_2}{K_1+K_2}\) works here only because lengths and areas are equal.

Convection (10.9.2)

Convection: heat transfer by actual bulk motion of matter. Possible where? only in fluids (liquids + gases).

Types of Convection: Natural and Forced

Natural convection: flow happens on its own due to density differences; gravity is key. Forced convection: flow driven by an external agent (e.g. pump, fan, etc.).

Natural Convection: Mechanism (Heating from below)

When a fluid is heated from below:
  • Bottom (hot) layer expands → becomes less dense.
  • Because of buoyancy, hot fluid rises.
  • Top cold fluid sinks to replace it, gets heated, rises… cycle repeats.
Convection vs conduction: convection = bulk transport of fluid chunks; conduction = energy passed by microscopic collisions (no bulk flow).

Forced Convection: Examples and Human Body

  • Forced-air heating in houses
  • Human circulatory system
  • Automobile engine cooling system
Human body: the heart works like a pump → blood circulation transfers heat by forced convection → helps keep uniform body temperature.

Natural Convection Phenomena: Sea Breeze (Day and Night) and Convection Cycle

  • Day: land heats faster than big water bodies because:
    • water has higher specific heat capacity
    • mixing currents spread heat through a large volume
  • Air touching warm land heats by conduction, expands, becomes less dense, and rises.
  • Cool air moves in to fill the gap → causes winds; near coasts → sea breeze.
  • Cool air sinks, setting up a thermal convection cycle that carries heat away from land.
  • Night: land cools faster; water surface stays warmer → cycle reverses.

Natural Convection Phenomena: Trade Winds

Trade wind: steady surface wind blowing from north-east towards the equator. Reasonable explanation:
  • Equator and poles get unequal solar heating.
  • Air near equator (surface) is hot; upper-atmosphere air near poles is cool.
  • Without Earth’s rotation, convection loop would be:
    • air rises at equator and moves aloft towards poles
    • air sinks at poles and returns along surface towards equator
  • Earth’s rotation effect: changes the convection flow because:
    • equatorial air has eastward speed \(\approx\) 1600 km/h
    • near poles, eastward speed \(\approx\) 0
  • So air sinks not at poles but near 30° N latitude, then flows back to equator → gives the observed trade-wind pattern.
Exam tip: For natural convection, write the chain: heating → expansion → density ↓ → buoyancy rise → circulation. Also mention: “only in fluids” + “gravity essential”.

10.9.3 Radiation

Radiation (heat transfer) = energy transfer by electromagnetic (EM) waves; no material medium needed.
  • Conduction + convection need a medium → can’t transfer heat through vacuum.
  • Radiation works through vacuum (e.g. Sun heating Earth across space).
We feel heat from a nearby fire quickly even though air conducts poorly and convection takes time → that fast heating is due to radiation.
Radiant energy = energy carried by EM waves during radiation heat transfer. In an EM wave, electric and magnetic fields oscillate in space and time.
  • EM waves have different wavelengths, but all travel in vacuum at the same speed: c = 3 × 108 m s–1.
  • So radiation needs no medium and can be very fast.
Thermal radiation = EM radiation emitted by a body because of its temperature (e.g. red-hot iron, filament lamp). All bodies (solids, liquids, gases) emit radiant energy.

Absorption and colour; everyday applications

When thermal radiation hits a body: some is reflected, some absorbed.
Absorbing power depends on colour:
  • Black surfaces are better absorbers and also better emitters than light-coloured surfaces.
  • Summer: wear white/light colours → absorb least solar heat.
  • Winter: wear dark colours → absorb more heat → warmer.
  • Cooking utensils: bottoms blackened → maximum absorption from fire → better heating of food.

Dewar flask (Thermos bottle): minimising heat transfer

Dewar flask / thermos is built to minimise heat transfer between contents and surroundings.
  • Construction: double-walled glass vessel; inner + outer walls silvered (silver coating).
  • Radiation reduction:
    • Radiation from the inner wall is reflected back into the contents.
    • The outer wall reflects back incoming radiation.
  • Conduction + convection reduction: space between walls is evacuated (vacuum).
  • Support: flask rests on an insulator like cork.
  • Use: stops hot liquids (e.g. milk) cooling; also keeps cold things (e.g. ice) cold.
Exam pitfall: Thermos reduces all 3 modes: vacuum → conduction & convection, silvering → radiation, insulating supports → fewer conduction paths.

10.9.4 Blackbody Radiation

Blackbody thermal radiation spectrum: at any temperature, emission is not one wavelength; it’s a continuous spectrum (short → long wavelengths). Energy depends on wavelength; the curve of radiation energy per unit area per unit wavelength vs wavelength changes with temperature.

Wien’s Displacement Law

From blackbody curves: wavelength of maximum emission λm decreases as temperature increases.
Wien’s Displacement Law: λm T = constant
  • λm = wavelength at which emitted energy is maximum
  • T = absolute temperature (K)
  • constant = 2.9 × 10–3 m K (Wien’s constant)
  • Heated iron colours: as T ↑, λm ↓ → colour shifts dull red → reddish yellow → white hot.
  • Astrophysics: use Wien’s law to estimate surface temperatures (Moon, Sun, stars) from λm.
Worked examples (as in text):
  • Moon: maximum intensity near λm = 14 μm = 14 × 10–6 m.
    • λmT = 2.9 × 10–3 ⇒ T ≈ (2.9 × 10–3)/(14 × 10–6) ≈ 200 K.
  • Sun: maximum at λm = 4753 Å = 4753 × 10–10 m = 4.753 × 10–7 m.
    • T ≈ (2.9 × 10–3)/(4.753 × 10–7) ≈ 6060 K.
    • Note: this is the Sun’s surface temperature, not its interior temperature.
Unit traps: convert μm and Å to meters before using Wien’s law; use T in kelvin (not °C).

Universality of blackbody curves

Blackbody curves are universal: depend only on temperature, not on size, shape, material. Historical note (concept): explaining blackbody radiation triggered the early-20th-century quantum revolution.

Stefan–Boltzmann Law and Emissivity

Radiation transfers energy over large distances, even through vacuum. The total electromagnetic energy radiated by a body at absolute temperature T depends on:
  • size (surface area),
  • ability to radiate (emissivity),
  • and most strongly on temperature.

Perfect radiator (blackbody): total power emitted

Stefan–Boltzmann law (perfect radiator): H = AσT4
  • H = energy emitted per unit time (radiant power) (W)
  • A = surface area (m2)
  • T = absolute temperature (K)
  • σ = Stefan–Boltzmann constant = 5.67 × 10–8 W m–2 K–4
Found experimentally by Stefan, later proved theoretically by Boltzmann.

Real bodies: emissivity

Emissivity (e) = dimensionless fraction showing how well a real body radiates compared to a perfect radiator at the same T.
  • e = 1 for a perfect radiator
  • Most real bodies have e < 1
Stefan–Boltzmann law (real body): H = AeσT4
  • e = emissivity (dimensionless)
  • Other symbols = same as above
Example (tungsten lamp): for tungsten, e ≈ 0.4. Given T = 3000 K, A = 0.3 cm2 = 0.3 × 10–4 m2, H = 0.3 × 10–4 × 0.4 × 5.67 × 10–8 × (3000)4 = 60 W.
Conversion tip: cm2 → m2: multiply by 10–4. Common mistake: forget this, so power comes out wrong by 104.

Net radiation exchange with surroundings

A body at temperature T both emits and absorbs radiation from surroundings at temperature Ts. Net loss depends on the difference of T4 terms.
Net radiant heat loss (perfect radiator): H = σA (T4 − Ts4)
  • T = body temperature (K)
  • Ts = surroundings temperature (K)
  • H, σ, A = as defined above
Net radiant heat loss (emissivity e): H = eσA (T4 − Ts4)
  • e = emissivity of the body
  • T, Ts, σ, A, H = as defined above
Example: heat radiated by the human body
  • Body surface area A ≈ 1.9 m2
  • Room temperature = 22 °C ⇒ Ts = 295 K
  • Skin temperature ≈ 28 °C ⇒ T = 301 K
  • Skin emissivity e ≈ 0.97 (in the relevant EM region)
H = 5.67 × 10–8 × 1.9 × 0.97 × {(301)4 − (295)4} = 66.4 W
  • 66.4 W is more than half the body’s energy production rate at rest (≈ 120 W).
  • Arctic clothing idea: add a thin shiny metallic layer next to skin → reflects body radiation → cuts radiative heat loss better than ordinary clothing.

10.10 Newton’s Law of Cooling (Activity setup)

Hot water/milk left on a table cools slowly and finally reaches surroundings temperature. To study how fast/slow a body cools while exchanging heat with surroundings, use this setup.

Experimental procedure (calorimeter method)

  1. Put about 300 mL water in a calorimeter with a stirrer; cover with a two-holed lid.
  2. Fix the stirrer through one hole.
  3. Fix a thermometer through the other hole so the bulb is immersed in water.
  4. Note the reading; call it T1. This is the surroundings temperature.
  5. Heat water until it is about 40 °C above room temperature (i.e., above surroundings).
  6. Stop heating by removing the heat source.
  7. Start a stop-watch; note thermometer reading after a fixed interval (e.g., every 1 minute) while stirring gently.
  8. Keep noting water temperature (T2) until it becomes about 5 °C above surroundings temperature.
  9. Then plot (plotting instruction continues on next page).
Experimental care: Stir gently throughout (uniform temperature). Keep thermometer bulb fully immersed, otherwise readings go wrong.

Newton’s Law of Cooling

Newton’s law of cooling: rate of heat loss of a body ∝ (temperature of body − temperature of surroundings) = excess temperature.
  • Valid only for small temperature differences (body vs surroundings).
  • In real cooling: rate is highest at start (large excess temp) and falls with time as the body cools.
  • Heat loss by radiation (→ cooling rate) also depends on:
    • Nature of surface (polished vs dull/black)
    • Area of exposed surface
    These get “packed into” the proportionality constant.

Mathematical statement of the law

Newton’s law of cooling: − dQ/dt = k (T2 − T1)   ...(10.19)
  • Q = heat content / heat lost (J)
  • t = time (s)
  • T2 = body temperature at time t
  • T1 = surroundings temperature (assumed constant)
  • k = +ve constant (depends on area + nature of surface)
  • “−” sign: body is losing heat ⇒ Q decreases with time

Derivation of cooling equation (temperature as a function of time)

Consider a body with:
  • mass = m
  • specific heat capacity = s
  • temperature = T2 (at some instant)
  • surroundings at constant temperature = T1
In a small time dt, temperature falls by dT2 ⇒ heat lost:
dQ = m s dT2
  • m = mass (kg)
  • s = specific heat capacity (J kg−1 K−1)
  • dT2 = small change in body temperature (K or °C difference)
So, rate of heat loss:
dQ/dt = m s (dT2/dt)   ...(10.20)
  • dT2/dt is negative during cooling
Use (10.19) with (10.20):
− m s (dT2/dt) = k (T2 − T1) ⇒ dT2 / (T2 − T1) = − (k/(m s)) dt Let K = k/(m s), then: dT2 / (T2 − T1) = −K dt   ...(10.21)
  • K = k/(m s) (depends on surface/area via k, and on body via m, s)
Integrate:
ln (T2 − T1) = −K t + c   ...(10.22)
  • c = integration constant (fixed by initial condition)
Write in exponential form:
T2 = T1 + C′ e−Kt   ...(10.23)
  • C′ = ec (set by initial excess temperature)
  • So excess temperature (T2−T1) falls exponentially with time
Use of Eq. (10.23): lets you find cooling time across any chosen temperature range (when the law applies). Combined modes: for small ΔT, net cooling by conduction + convection + radiation is ≈ proportional to (T2−T1), so Newton’s law works well in cases like:
  • Heat transfer from a radiator to a room
  • Heat loss through a wall of a room
  • Cooling of a cup of tea
  • Don’t use Newton’s law for large temperature differences (radiation then varies strongly with temperature → linear law fails).
  • For the standard derivation, take T1 constant (surroundings = big heat reservoir).
  • In numericals, use excess temperature (T2−T1), not just T2.

Graphical inference from cooling curve

Plot cooling data as:
  • y-axis: ΔT = T2 − T1
  • x-axis: time t
Inference: cooling rate is high initially and decreases as T2 approaches T1.

Experimental verification of Newton’s law of cooling

Experimental set-up (as described):
  • A double-walled vessel (V) has water between its two walls.
  • A copper calorimeter (C) with hot water is kept inside the vessel.
  • Two thermometers measure:
    • T2 = temperature of water in the calorimeter
    • T1 = temperature of hot water between the walls (acts as surroundings)
  • Note T2 at equal time intervals.
Verification method:
  • Find excess temperature (T2−T1) for each reading.
  • Plot ln(T2−T1) vs time t.
  • You get a straight line with negative slope → supports Eq. (10.22).
From ln(T2−T1) = −Kt + c:
  • Straight-line: y = mx + c where y = ln(T2−T1), x = t
  • Slope m = −K (negative)

Worked Example 10.8 (cooling time between two ranges)

Problem: A pan of hot food cools from 94 °C to 86 °C in 2 min (room temp = 20 °C). Time to cool from 71 °C to 69 °C? Key idea: For small temperature differences: cooling rate ∝ excess temperature (T−Troom). Over short ranges, take average excess temperature:
  • Cooling rate ≈ (ΔT)/(time) ∝ average excess temperature.
  • Same object + same conditions ⇒ proportionality constant K same in both intervals.
Step 1: First interval (94→86 °C)
  • Average temperature = (94+86)/2 = 90 °C
  • Average excess = 90 − 20 = 70 °C
  • Drop = 8 °C in 2 min
Using the text’s proportionality (from Eq. 10.21 idea): (change in temperature)/(time) = K (excess temperature)
  • 8 °C / 2 min = K (70 °C)
Step 2: Second interval (71→69 °C)
  • Average temperature = (71+69)/2 = 70 °C
  • Average excess = 70 − 20 = 50 °C
  • Drop = 2 °C in time = t
So:
  • 2 °C / t = K (50 °C)
Step 3: Remove K (divide equations)
  • (8 °C / 2 min) / (70 °C) = (2 °C / t) / (50 °C)
  • t = 0.7 min = 42 s
  • Here we used average excess temperature (approx over small interval). For exact results, use the exponential law (Eq. 10.23).
  • Always subtract room temperature first to get excess temperature before comparing cooling rates.
