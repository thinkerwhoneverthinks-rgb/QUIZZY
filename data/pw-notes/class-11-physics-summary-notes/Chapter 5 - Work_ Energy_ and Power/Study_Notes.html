Ch 05 : Work Energy and Power

5.1 Introduction

In everyday talk, “work”, “energy”, “power” are loose ideas. In physics, they’re tightly defined:
  • Work: a specific quantity involving a force and the displacement it causes (later defined via scalar product).
  • Energy: closely linked to the capacity to do work.
  • Power: how fast work is done (everyday “powerful” often ≈ fast, but physics uses an exact definition).
To build these ideas, we need a math tool first: the scalar (dot) product of vectors.

5.1.1 The Scalar Product

Many physics quantities are vectors (displacement, velocity, acceleration, force). Beyond +/−, we also need vector multiplication, in two forms:
  • Scalar product (dot product): vector × vector → scalar (this section).
  • Vector product: vector × vector → vector (later, Chapter 6).
Definition of dot product: A·B = AB cosθ   (Eq. 5.1a)
  • A, B = magnitudes of vectors A and B
  • θ = angle between A and B
  • A·B = scalar (magnitude only, no direction)
Geometric meaning (projection idea):
  • A·B = A(B cosθ) = B(A cosθ)
  • B cosθ = component (projection) of B along A
  • A cosθ = component (projection) of A along B
  • So A·B = (size of one vector) × (component of the other along it).
Algebraic properties of scalar product:
  • Commutative: A·B = B·A
  • Distributive: A·(B + C) = A·B + A·C
  • Scalar multiplication: A·(λB) = λ(A·B), λ real
Dot products of unit vectors: î·î = ĵ·ĵ = k̂·k̂ = 1 î·ĵ = ĵ·k̂ = k̂·î = 0
  • î, ĵ, k̂ = mutually perpendicular unit vectors along x, y, z
Dot product (component form): If A = Axî + Ayĵ + Azk̂ and B = Bxî + Byĵ + Bzk̂, then A·B = AxBx + AyBy + AzBz   (Eq. 5.1b)
  • Ax, Ay, Az = components of A
  • Bx, By, Bz = components of B
Magnitude from dot product: A·A = A2 = Ax2 + Ay2 + Az2   (Eq. 5.1c)
  • Since A·A = |A||A|cos0 = A2
Key result: A·B = 0 if A ⟂ B (perpendicular vectors).

Example 5.1: Angle between two vectors; projection using dot product

Given: Force vector: F = (3î + 4ĵ − 5k̂) (unit as given) Displacement vector: d = (5î + 4ĵ + 3k̂) (unit as given) To find:
  • Angle θ between F and d
  • Projection of F on d
Step 1: Dot product F·d = 3×5 + 4×4 + (−5)×3 = 15 + 16 − 15 = 16 (unit) Step 2: Use F·d = |F||d| cosθ Step 3: Magnitudes (self dot) F·F = |F|2 = 32 + 42 + (−5)2 = 9 + 16 + 25 = 50 d·d = |d|2 = 52 + 42 + 32 = 25 + 16 + 9 = 50 So |F| = √50 and |d| = √50. Step 4: Solve for cosθ, then θ cosθ = (F·d)/(|F||d|) = 16/(√50·√50) = 16/50 = 0.32 θ = cos−1(0.32) Projection of F on d:
The projection (scalar component) of F along d: (component of F along d) = |F|cosθ = (F·d)/|d| Here, |d| = √50, so component = 16/√50 (in force units). This comes from: F·d = |d|(|F|cosθ).
  • Don’t mix up projection (a scalar component) with a projection vector. Here it’s the scalar component along the other vector.
  • In A·B = AxBx + AyBy + AzBz, watch signs carefully (e.g. the −5 in F).

5.2 Notions of Work and Kinetic Energy: The Work-Energy Theorem

Start with a standard kinematics result for straight-line motion with constant acceleration:
v2 − u2 = 2as (Eq. 5.2)
  • u = initial speed
  • v = final speed
  • a = constant acceleration (1D)
  • s = distance travelled
Multiply both sides by m/2:
(1/2)mv2 − (1/2)mu2 = mas (Eq. 5.2a) Use Newton II: F = ma ⇒ mas = Fs.
  • m = mass
  • F = (net) force along the line of motion (this 1D case)
Generalise to 3D using vectors (displacement d, acceleration a):
v2 − u2 = 2 a·d Multiply by m/2: (1/2)mv2 − (1/2)mu2 = m a·d = F·d (Eq. 5.2b)
  • a = acceleration vector
  • d = displacement vector
  • F = ma = net force vector
  • a·d, F·d are dot products → only the component along displacement counts
Why these definitions appear:
  • (1/2)mv2 is called kinetic energy, K
  • F·d is called work, W (work done by the force over displacement d)
Work–energy link (constant force over a displacement): Kf − Ki = W (Eq. 5.3)
  • Ki = initial kinetic energy
  • Kf = final kinetic energy
  • W = work done on the body (here: by the net force)
Work–Energy (WE) Theorem: Change in kinetic energy = work done by the net force. Eq. (5.2) is just a special case (constant acceleration/force). Later: varying force.

Example 5.2: Raindrop—work done by gravity and a resistive force (using WE theorem)

Situation: Raindrop falls under gravity (down) + an unknown resistive force (opposes motion). Resistive force ∝ speed (details not needed). Given:
  • m = 1.00 g = 10−3 kg
  • h = 1.00 km = 103 m
  • impact speed v = 50.0 m s−1
  • initial speed u = 0 (starts from rest)
  • g = 10 m s−2 (constant)
(a) Work done by gravitational force First find the change in kinetic energy:
ΔK = Kf − Ki = (1/2)mv2 − (1/2)mu2 Here u = 0 ⇒ ΔK = (1/2)mv2
ΔK = (1/2)×10−3×(50)2 = (1/2)×10−3×2500 = 1.25 J Work done by gravity:
Wg = mgh
  • Wg = work done by gravity
  • m = mass, g = gravitational acceleration, h = vertical drop
Wg = 10−3 × 10 × 103 = 10.0 J (b) Work done by the resistive force WE theorem with two forces (gravity + resistive):
ΔK = Wg + Wr
  • Wr = work done by resistive force
So: Wr = ΔK − Wg = 1.25 − 10.0 = −8.75 J
Meaning: Wr is negative because the resistive force is opposite the motion (it removes mechanical energy).
  • Multiple forces ⇒ always use net work: ΔK = ΣW
  • Negative work ⇒ force has a component opposite displacement (typical: drag/friction)
  • Watch units: 1 g = 10−3 kg, 1 km = 103 m

5.3 Work

Work links a force to the displacement over which it acts. Think of a constant force F on a mass m causing a displacement d in the +x direction (as in the figure). This leads to the exact definition.
Core idea: work by a constant force depends only on the force component along the displacement → captured by the dot product F·d.

Example 5.3: Work in skidding stop; Third-law forces vs work

Work by a force depends on the force and the displacement of the point where it acts. A Newton’s 3rd law pair (equal + opposite forces) can still do different works because the two bodies can have different displacements.
Given: skid displacement d = 10 m, friction magnitude F = 200 N, friction directly opposes motion. (a) Work done by the road on the cycle
  • Force opposite displacement ⇒ θ = 180° = π rad
  • Work by road on cycle:
    Work (constant force): W = F d cos θ (also: W = F·d) W = work (J) F = force magnitude (N) d = displacement magnitude (m) θ = angle between force and displacement
So: Wr = 200 × 10 × cos(π) = 200 × 10 × (−1) = −2000 J
Negative work when force opposes displacement (θ between 90° and 180°; for stopping friction, θ = 180°). This negative work reduces kinetic energy → matches the WE theorem.
(b) Work done by the cycle on the road
  • By Newton’s Third Law, cycle pushes on road with 200 N, opposite to road’s force on cycle
  • But the road doesn’t move (no displacement)
  • So work done by cycle on road: W = 0
Exam trap: “equal and opposite forces” ≠ “equal and opposite work”. Work needs force × displacement (along the force). If displacement is zero, work is zero even if force is big.

Work done by a constant force: meaning, sign, and when work is zero

Work (constant force) = (component of force along the displacement) × (displacement).
Work by constant force: W = (F cos θ)d = F·d W = work done (J) F cos θ = force component in direction of displacement (N) d = magnitude of displacement (m) F·d = dot product (force vector · displacement vector)
No displacement ⇒ no work, even if force is huge (e.g. pushing hard on a rigid wall: W = 0). “Work” in physics ≠ everyday meaning: you may get tired (internal energy used), but mechanical work on the wall is zero.

Conditions for zero work

  • (i) Displacement is zero (d = 0): e.g. weightlifter holds a 150 kg mass steady for 30 s → no work on the load in that time.
  • (ii) Force is zero (F = 0): e.g. block on a smooth horizontal table: no horizontal force (no friction), can still move a long distance → horizontal work is zero.
  • (iii) Force ⟂ displacement (θ = 90° = π/2): cos(π/2) = 0 ⇒ W = 0.
    • e.g. block moves horizontally on smooth table: gravity mg is vertical, displacement is horizontal → gravity does no work.
    • e.g. perfectly circular moon orbit: Earth’s gravity is radial, moon’s instantaneous displacement is tangential → θ = 90° → gravity does no work on the moon.

Positive and negative work (sign of work)

  • If 0° ≤ θ < 90°, then cos θ > 0 ⇒ W is positive.
  • If 90° < θ ≤ 180°, then cos θ < 0 ⇒ W is negative.
  • Common case: friction opposite motion has θ = 180° ⇒ cos 180° = −1 ⇒ negative work.

Dimensions and units of work and energy

From W = Fd, with [F] = [MLT−2] and [d] = [L], so [Work] = [Energy] = [ML2T−2].
  • SI unit: joule (J), named after James Prescott Joule (1811–1869).
  • There are alternative units of work/energy (see Table 5.1).

5.4 Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy = energy due to motion; it tells how much work an object can do because it’s moving.
Kinetic energy: K = (1/2) m v2 (Eq. 5.5) K = kinetic energy (J) m = mass (kg) v = speed (m s−1)
  • K is scalar.
  • Illustrations: KE of fast streams used to grind corn; sailing ships use KE of wind.
  • Typical K values for objects are listed (Table 5.2).

Example 5.4: Bullet emerging with 10% of initial kinetic energy

Given: m = 50.0 g = 0.050 kg, vi = 200 m s−1, bullet emerges with 10% of initial KE. Step 1: Initial KE Ki = (1/2)mvi2 = (1/2)(0.050)(200)2 = 1000 J. Step 2: Final KE Kf = 0.1Ki = 0.1 × 1000 = 100 J. Step 3: Emergent speed vf Kf = (1/2)mvf2 ⇒ vf = √(2Kf/m) = √(2×100/0.050) = 63.2 m s−1.
Key inference: “KE becomes 10%” ≠ “speed becomes 10%”. Since K ∝ v2, speed scales as √K. Here vf/vi = √0.1 ≈ 0.316, so speed drops by about 68% (not 90%).

5.5 Work done by a variable force

Truly constant forces are rare. Often force changes with position: F(x). Then work is found by adding up tiny bits over small displacements.

Approximation by small intervals and summation (Riemann sum)

  • For a small displacement Δx, treat the force as roughly constant at F(x).
  • Small work done:
    Small work element (1D): ΔW = F(x)Δx ΔW = work over small displacement (J) F(x) = force at position x (N) Δx = small displacement (m)
  • Total work from xi to xf ≈ sum of rectangle areas under the F–x graph:
    Approx. work (sum): W ≅ Σ F(x)Δx (Eq. 5.6), summed from xi to xf W = total work (J) F(x) = force at position x (N) Δx = width of each small interval (m) xi, xf = initial and final positions

Exact expression using integration: area under the F–x curve

As Δx → 0, the sum approaches a definite limit = area under the force–displacement curve. That gives the exact work for a variable force.
Work by variable force (1D): W = ∫xixf F(x) dx (Eq. 5.7) W = work done (J) F(x) = force as a function of x (N) dx = infinitesimal displacement (m) xi, xf = initial and final positions
Sign from graph: any part of the graph below the x-axis (force opposite the chosen + direction) gives negative work.

Example 5.5: Work from a changing push and constant friction (area method)

Situation: a woman pushes a trunk on a rough platform.
  • Applied force = 100 N for first 10 m.
  • Then applied force decreases linearly to 50 N by the time total displacement is 20 m.
  • Friction is constant magnitude |f| = 50 N, opposing motion.
Force–displacement plots (conceptual)
  • Applied force F: 100 N flat line from x = 0 → 10 m; then straight line down to 50 N at x = 20 m (note: at x = 20 m, F = 50 N, not zero).
  • Friction force f: constant at −50 N (negative since opposite to motion / chosen + direction).
Work done by the woman (WF) = area under applied-force graph
  • 0 to 10 m: rectangle = 100 × 10 = 1000 J.
  • 10 to 20 m: linear drop 100 N → 50 N, so area is a trapezium:
    • Trapezium area = (1/2)(sum of parallel sides) × (distance)
    • = (1/2)(100 + 50) × 10 = 750 J
So, WF = 1000 + 750 = 1750 J. Work done by friction (Wf) = area under friction graph
  • Constant −50 N over 20 m: Wf = (−50) × 20 = −1000 J.
On force–displacement graphs: work = signed area. Below-axis areas are negative (like friction here).

5.6 Work–Energy Theorem for a variable force (1D proof)

Work–Energy (WE) theorem (big idea): net work on a particle = change in kinetic energy. Here: proof for a variable force in 1D.

Derivation using Newton’s Second Law

Start with kinetic energy:
Kinetic energy: K = (1/2) m v2 K = kinetic energy (J) m = mass (kg) v = speed (m s−1)
Time rate of change of kinetic energy
  • dK/dt = d/dt (1/2 m v2) = m v (dv/dt)
  • Newton’s 2nd law (1D): F = m (dv/dt)
  • So: dK/dt = vF
  • And since v = dx/dt: dK/dt = F (dx/dt)
  • Multiply by dt: dK = F dx

Integral form and final theorem

Integrate from xi to xf (energies Ki to Kf):
Work–energy relation (variable force): Kf − Ki = ∫xixf F(x) dx (Eq. 5.8a) Ki, Kf = initial, final kinetic energies (J) F(x) = force vs position (N) xi, xf = initial, final positions (m)
But by the definition of work for variable force (Eq. 5.7):
Work–Energy Theorem: Kf − Ki = W (Eq. 5.8b) W = total work done by the force from xi to xf (J)
WE theorem = integral form of Newton’s 2nd law. Newton’s law is “instant-by-instant” (force ↔ acceleration). WE theorem adds it up over an interval, so the detailed time info is “integrated over”.

Work–Energy Theorem vs Newton’s Second Law (Scalar vs Vector)

Newton’s 2nd law (2D/3D) is a vector equation (direction matters). WE theorem is a scalar equation, so it does not show the directional info that Newton’s 2nd law contains.

Example 5.6: Work done by a Position-Dependent Retarding Force

Given

  • Mass: m = 1 kg
  • Initial speed: vi = 2 m s−1
  • Rough patch: x = 0.10 m to x = 2.01 m
  • Retarding force (opposes motion) in the patch:
    • Fr(x) = − k/x for 0.10 < x < 2.01 m
    • Fr(x) = 0 for x < 0.10 m and x > 2.01 m
  • Constant: k = 0.5 J

To find

  • Final kinetic energy Kf as it crosses the patch
  • Final speed vf

Method (Work–Energy Theorem with variable force)

Along x with a position-dependent force: W = ∫ F(x) dx. Then WE gives: Kf = Ki + ∫ F(x) dx over the interval.
For integrals like ∫(1/x) dx, the result is ln x (natural log), not log base 10.
ln = natural log (base e): ln X = loge X = 2.303 log10 X.

The Concept of Potential Energy

Potential energy = “stored energy” due to position or configuration. Remove constraints → it can turn into kinetic energy.
  • Examples of “stored energy”: stretched bow-string; fault lines in Earth’s crust (like compressed springs) storing energy released in earthquakes.

Gravitational Potential Energy near Earth’s Surface

  • Gravitational force on mass m: mg downward.
  • Choose upward as positive.
  • Take g ≈ constant near Earth if h ≪ RE (ignore change of g with height).
Raise the ball to height h:
  • External work done against gravity = mgh.
  • This work is stored as gravitational potential energy:
V(h) = mgh
  • V(h) = gravitational potential energy at height h
  • m = mass
  • g = acceleration due to gravity (≈ constant near Earth)
  • h = height above reference (ground)
Here: gravitational PE at height h = negative of the work done by gravity while raising it (equivalently = external work done against gravity).

Force as Negative Derivative of Potential Energy

F = − dV(h)/dh
  • F = gravitational force component along chosen axis (vertical here)
  • V(h) = potential energy as a function of height
  • h = vertical coordinate (upward +)
For gravity near Earth: V(h) = mgh ⇒ dV/dh = mg ⇒ F = −mg Negative sign = force points downward (opposite the + upward axis).

Conversion of Potential Energy to Kinetic Energy (Falling Body)

Drop from height h; just before hitting ground:
v2 = 2gh
  • v = speed just before ground
  • g = acceleration due to gravity
  • h = drop height
Multiply by m/2: (1/2)mv2 = mgh So PE at height h → KE at ground (if no dissipative forces).

When Potential Energy is Applicable (Conservative Forces)

Potential energy works only for forces where work done against the force can be stored and later recovered as kinetic energy when constraints are removed. These are conservative forces.

Mathematical Definition of Potential Energy in One Dimension

For a 1D force F(x), a potential energy function V(x) can be defined if:
F(x) = − dV/dx
  • F(x) = force as a function of position x
  • V(x) = potential energy function
  • x = position coordinate
Integrate from xi to xf: ∫xixf F(x) dx = − ∫xixf (dV/dx) dx = −[V(xf) − V(xi)] ⇒ ∫ F(x) dx = V(xi) − V(xf)

Conservative vs Non-Conservative Forces (Path Dependence)

Conservative force: work depends only on start + end points (NOT the path).
Non-conservative: work/KE depends on other factors (e.g. velocity, specific path).
Illustration (inclined plane result from earlier chapter):
  • Mass m released from rest at height h on a smooth (frictionless) incline reaches bottom with speed √(2gh), independent of the incline angle.
  • So KE at bottom = mgh (depends only on height drop).

Dimensions and Unit of Potential Energy

Potential energy dimensions: [ML2T−2]
SI unit: joule (J) (same as work + kinetic energy).

Change in Potential Energy and Work Done by a Conservative Force

ΔV = − (work done by the force)
  • ΔV = V(xf) − V(xi) = change in potential energy
  • Minus sign: if force does +work, potential energy drops by that amount
ΔV = − F(x) Δx (Eq. 5.9, 1D, small displacement)
  • F(x) = conservative force at position x
  • Δx = small displacement
  • ΔV = corresponding change in potential energy

Conservation of Mechanical Energy

Derivation (1D motion under a conservative force)

For a small displacement Δx under F(x):
  • Work–energy theorem: ΔK = F(x) Δx
  • Conservative force (via potential): −ΔV = F(x) Δx
So: ΔK + ΔV = 0 Δ(K + V) = 0 (Eq. 5.10)
Ki + V(xi) = Kf + V(xf) (Eq. 5.11)
  • K = kinetic energy
  • V(x) = potential energy at position x
  • xi, xf = initial, final positions
K + V(x) = total mechanical energy.
K and V can change with position, but for conservative forces their sum stays constant.

Definitions of a Conservative Force (All are equivalent here)

  • Definition 1 (via potential): F(x) is conservative if it comes from a scalar potential V(x) using Eq. (5.9) / F(x) = −dV/dx. (3D generalisation uses a vector derivative; beyond this book.)
  • Definition 2 (endpoint dependence): work depends only on endpoints. Using energy: W = Kf − Ki = V(xi) − V(xf) Depends only on xi and xf.
  • Definition 3 (closed path): work over any closed path is zero. From Eq. (5.11), if xi = xf, then Ki = Kf ⇒ net work = 0.

Statement of the Principle

Total mechanical energy is conserved if the forces doing work are conservative.

Illustration Mentioned

  • Gravity example: ball of mass m dropped from cliff height H shows PE → KE while total mechanical energy stays constant (if no non-conservative work).
  • Spring force is taken up in the next section (as noted).

Conservation of Mechanical Energy (Ball at Heights 0, h, H)

Total mechanical energy under a conservative force: E = K + U, where K = kinetic energy, U = potential energy. If only conservative forces do work (or non-conservative forces do zero net work), then E is conserved: E = constant.

Mechanical energies at the three heights

At height H (top): EH = mgH (Eq. 5.11a) m = mass of ball g = acceleration due to gravity H = height above ground level (ground = zero level)
At height h (intermediate): Eh = mgh + (1/2)mvh2 (Eq. 5.11b) h = intermediate height above ground vh = speed at height h
At ground level (height 0): E0 = (1/2)mvf2 (Eq. 5.11c) vf = speed just before reaching ground
A constant force is a special case of a position-dependent force F(x).
Near Earth’s surface, gravity is ~constant and conservative ⇒ mechanical energy is conserved in free fall (ignore air resistance).

Applying conservation: results for speeds

From EH = E0: mgH = (1/2)mvf2 ⇒ vf2 = 2gH vf = speed at ground level H = release height
This matches the standard free-fall result.
From EH = Eh: mgH = mgh + (1/2)mvh2 ⇒ vh2 = 2g(H − h) (Eq. 5.11d) vh = speed at height h
Energy picture as height changes:
  • At H: energy is all potential.
  • At h: energy is part potential + part kinetic.
  • At ground: energy is all kinetic (ideal model).
Common exam slip: writing v = 2gH instead of v2 = 2gH. Always keep track of the square when using energy.

Example 5.7: Bob on a String Completing a Vertical Semicircle with Slack at the Top

Bob (mass m) on a light string (length L) is given horizontal speed v0 at the lowest point A. It moves in a vertical circle. String goes slack only at the very top point C ⇒ tension at C is zero.

Forces and energy reasoning

  • External forces on bob: weight (mg) + tension T in string.
  • Tension does no work: motion is tangential, tension is radial (⊥ to displacement).
  • So only gravity does work ⇒ mechanical energy conserved.
  • Take gravitational PE = 0 at the bottom point A.

(i) Finding the required initial speed v0

Energy at A: E = (1/2)mv02   (Eq. 5.12) v0 = speed at lowest point A
At the top point C:
  • Rise from A to C = 2L ⇒ PE at C = mg(2L).
  • Speed at C = vC.
  • Slack at C ⇒ TC = 0.
Energy at C: E = 2mgL + (1/2)mvC2   (Eq. 5.13)
Newton’s 2nd law (radial) at top C, with TC=0: mg = mvC2/L   (Eq. 5.14) vC = speed at top point C
Sub Eq. (5.14) into Eq. (5.13):
  • (1/2)mvC2 = (1/2)m(gL) = (1/2)mgL
  • So E = 2mgL + (1/2)mgL = (5/2)mgL
Energy conserved ⇒ set energy at A = energy at C:
(1/2)mv02 = (5/2)mgL ⇒ v02 = 5gL ⇒ v0 = √(5gL)
“Slack only at the top” means: at C set TC=0, then use the centripetal condition at the top to get vC2 = gL.

(ii) Speeds at points B and C

Speed at C (from Eq. 5.14):
vC = √(gL)
Speed at B (B is height L above A, i.e. halfway up the diameter):
  • PE at B (relative to A) = mgL
  • Total energy at B: E = mgL + (1/2)mvB2
Equate with energy at A and use v02 = 5gL:
  • (1/2)mv02 = mgL + (1/2)mvB2
  • (1/2)m(5gL) = mgL + (1/2)mvB2
  • (5/2)mgL − mgL = (1/2)mvB2 ⇒ (3/2)mgL = (1/2)mvB2
  • vB2 = 3gL ⇒ vB = √(3gL)
vB = √(3gL)

(iii) Ratio of kinetic energies at B and C

Kinetic energies:
  • KB = (1/2)mvB2 = (1/2)m(3gL)
  • KC = (1/2)mvC2 = (1/2)m(gL)
KB/KC = vB2/vC2 = (3gL)/(gL) = 3

Nature of motion after reaching C

  • At C, string slack; bob’s velocity is horizontal (to the left).
  • If string is cut at C: bob does projectile motion with horizontal projection (like kicked off a cliff).
  • If string is not cut: bob can keep following the circle and complete the revolution.
Exam trap: Don’t set tension = 0 everywhere. Statement says slack only on reaching top point C ⇒ set T = 0 only at C.

5.9 The Potential Energy of a Spring

Spring force is variable, position-dependent, and (for an ideal spring) conservative. Setup: block on a smooth horizontal surface, attached to a light (massless) spring fixed to a wall.

Hooke’s law (force law for an ideal spring)

Hooke’s law: Fs = −kx Fs = spring force on the block k = spring constant (unit: N m−1) x = displacement from equilibrium ( + extension, − compression )
  • Large k → spring is stiff.
  • Small k → spring is soft.
  • Sign idea: force is always restoring → points back toward equilibrium (opposite to x).

Work done by the spring force (extension from 0 to xm)

Pull the block outward until extension reaches xm (xm > 0). Work done by the spring force:
Work by spring force: Ws = ∫0xm Fs dx = −∫0xm kx dx = −(1/2)kxm2   (Eq. 5.15) Ws = work done by spring force xm = maximum extension from equilibrium
Same result = (signed) area under the Fs–x graph (triangle).
The external pulling force does positive work (acts with the displacement while overcoming the spring force):
Work by external force (pulling): W = +(1/2)kxm2   (Eq. 5.16)

Compression case and sign consistency

  • Compression: displacement xc is negative (xc < 0).
  • Spring still does negative work if the block is pushed further into compression.
  • Magnitude stays (1/2)k xc2 since xc2 > 0.
Compression (to xc < 0): Ws = −(1/2)k xc2, and external work = +(1/2)k xc2

Work done from an initial displacement xi to final displacement xf

General work by spring force: Ws = −∫xixf kx dx = −(1/2)k(xf2 − xi2)   (Eq. 5.17) xi = initial displacement xf = final displacement
Spring work depends only on endpoints xi, xf (not the path) ⇒ classic sign of a conservative force.

Work in a cycle (returning to the same displacement)

Cyclic return to same position: if xf = xi, then Ws = −(1/2)k(xi2 − xi2) = 0   (Eq. 5.18)
So, work by the spring force over a closed cycle is zero.
We’ve shown the spring force is conservative because:
  • Position-dependent: Fs = −kx.
  • Work depends only on start/end positions (Eq. 5.17).
  • Work over a closed path is zero (Eq. 5.18).

Spring potential energy function

Define spring potential energy V(x) with V = 0 at equilibrium (x = 0).
Spring potential energy: V(x) = (1/2)kx2   (Eq. 5.19) V(x) = potential energy stored in spring at displacement x
Consistency check with force: −dV/dx = −d/dx [(1/2)kx2] = −kx = Fs.

Energy conservation for a released block and maximum speed

Block (mass m) pulled to xm and released from rest. For any x between −xm and +xm, mechanical energy stays constant:
Energy at general position x: (1/2)mv2 + (1/2)kx2 = (1/2)kxm2 v = speed when displacement is x xm = maximum amplitude (release extension)
Max kinetic energy (max speed) happens at equilibrium x = 0:
At x = 0: (1/2)mvm2 = (1/2)kxm2 ⇒ vm = xm√(k/m) vm = maximum speed (at equilibrium)
Dimensional check: k/m has units (N m−1)/kg = (kg s−2)/kg = s−2 = T−2, so √(k/m) has units T−1. Multiply by xm (length) → speed.
As it moves: K and V keep swapping back and forth, but total E = K + V stays constant (matching parabolic K and V curves).
Don’t mix up x (instantaneous displacement) vs xm (max extension/amplitude). In the energy equation, x changes; xm is fixed for that motion.

Example 5.8: Car Colliding with a Mounted Spring (Maximum Compression)

Smooth road (no dissipative work): at maximum compression the car is momentarily at rest, so initial kinetic energy → entirely into spring potential energy.

Given data and speed conversion

  • Mass of car: m = 1000 kg
  • Speed: 18.0 km h−1 = 5 m s−1
  • Spring constant: k = 5.25 × 103 N m−1
Handy conversion: 36 km h−1 = 10 m s−1 ⇒ 18 km h−1 = 5 m s−1.

Energy method to relate compression to initial kinetic energy

Initial kinetic energy of the car:
K = (1/2)mv2 K = kinetic energy v = speed of the car before collision
Numerically:
  • K = (1/2)(1000)(52) = 1.25 × 104 J
At maximum compression xm, spring potential energy equals this kinetic energy:
V = (1/2)kxm2 V = spring potential energy at maximum compression xm = maximum compression
Conservation of mechanical energy:
  • (1/2)kxm2 = (1/2)mv2 (use this to solve for xm)
At maximum compression: v = 0 (turning point) → all energy is spring potential; don’t include any kinetic term there.

Remarks on Conservative Forces and Potential Energy

Idealisation for spring–block / car + spring problems: spring is massless, surface has negligible friction (unless friction is explicitly added).

Key remarks (exam-focused)

  • (i) Energy method gives NO time: Energy can give max compression / max height etc., but not the time taken. For time, solve using Newton’s 2nd law.
  • (ii) Not every force is conservative: Friction is non-conservative. If non-conservative forces act, mechanical energy isn’t conserved → modify the energy equation (see Ex 5.9 + modified formula later).
  • (iii) Zero of PE is your choice: Reference level for potential energy is arbitrary → choose what’s convenient.
    • Spring: usually set V(x)=0 at x=0 (unstretched spring).
    • Constant gravity near Earth: often set V=0 at Earth’s surface.
    • Universal gravitation: often take V=0 at infinity.
Once you pick the PE reference (zero), stick to it consistently. Don’t swap reference levels mid-solution (“cannot change horses in midstream”).

Work–Energy Theorem with Friction (Non-Conservative Force): Example 5.9

Problem statement

Same spring-compression setup as Example 5.8, but now include kinetic friction with µ = 0.5. Find the maximum compression of the spring.

Physical reasoning (forces and choice of method)

  • While compressing: both spring force and friction oppose the motion (oppose compression).
  • Friction is non-conservative ⇒ use work–energy theorem, not plain “mechanical energy conserved”.
Work–Energy Theorem: Wnet = ΔK Wnet = net work by all forces ΔK = Kf − Ki

Applying the theorem

  • Change in kinetic energy: At max compression, object stops momentarily ⇒ vf = 0.
Stopping: ΔK = 0 − (1/2) m v2 = −(1/2) m v2 m = mass v = initial speed just before compression (from Ex 5.8)
  • Work done during compression distance x:
Spring work: Wspring = −(1/2) k x2 k = spring constant x = compression (max compression = xm)
Kinetic friction work: Wfriction = − µ m g x µ = coefficient of kinetic friction m = mass g = gravitational acceleration x = compression distance (distance friction acts)
  • Net work: Wnet = Wspring + Wfriction = −(1/2)k x2 − µ m g x
  • Set Wnet = ΔK, then rearrange:
−(1/2)k x2 − µ m g x = −(1/2) m v2 k = spring constant x = compression µ = friction coefficient m = mass g = gravitational acceleration v = initial speed
Standard form (quadratic in x): (1/2)k x2 + µ m g x − (1/2) m v2 = 0 x = maximum compression x_m (unknown)

Numerical substitution noted in the text

  • Friction term computed: µ m g = 0.5 × 103 × 10 = 5 × 103 N (taking g = 10.0 m s−2).
  • Solve the quadratic; choose the positive root since xm is a positive compression.
Using the given values (from Ex 5.8 data): xm = 1.35 m, which is smaller than the frictionless compression (makes sense: friction dissipates energy).
Quadratic gives 2 roots: reject the negative one (compression distance can’t be negative here).

Modified Mechanical Energy Relation with Non-Conservative Forces

Derivation from WE theorem

  • Let total force = conservative part Fc + non-conservative part Fnc.
  • Work–energy: (Fc + Fnc) · Δx = ΔK.
  • For conservative forces: Fc · Δx = −ΔV.
Including non-conservative work: Δ(K + V) = Fnc · Δx K = kinetic energy V = potential energy Fnc = non-conservative force Δx = displacement along the path segment
Mechanical energy form: ΔE = Fnc · Δx E = K + V
Along path i → f: Ef − Ei = Wnc Ei, Ef = initial/final mechanical energy Wnc = total work by non-conservative forces on that path
Unlike conservative work, Wnc is path-dependent (depends on the route from i to f).

Power

Power = rate of doing work / transferring energy.

Average power

Average power: Pav = W / t Pav = average power W = work done (energy transferred) in time t t = time interval

Instantaneous power

Instantaneous power: P = dW/dt P = instantaneous power dW = work done in time dt
  • Since dW = F · dr, then:
Power delivered by a force: P = F · v F = instantaneous force (vector) v = instantaneous velocity (vector)
Power is a scalar. Dimensions: [M L2 T−3]

Units of power

  • SI: watt (W), with 1 W = 1 J s−1.
  • Also used: horsepower (hp).
Conversion: 1 hp = 746 W hp = horsepower W = watt

Electrical energy consumption and kWh

Electricity bills use kWh. Reminder: kWh is energy, not power.
  • Example shown: 100 W bulb for 10 hours.
Energy used: E = P t E = energy P = power t = time
  • 100 W × 10 h = 1000 Wh = 1 kWh
  • Also: 1 kWh = 103 W × 3600 s = 3.6 × 106 J
kWh → J: 1 kWh = 3.6 × 106 J kWh = kilowatt-hour (energy) J = joule
Don’t write “kW/h” for energy. kW is power; power × time = energy ⇒ kWh.

Power with Resistive Forces: Example 5.10 (Elevator)

Problem statement

Elevator (elevator + passengers) max load m = 1800 kg moves up at constant speed v = 2 m s−1. Friction opposing motion Ff = 4000 N. Find the minimum power delivered by the motor (W and hp).

Reasoning

  • Constant speed ⇒ a = 0 ⇒ net force = 0.
  • So motor force must balance total downward resistive force: weight + friction.
Total downward resistive force: F = m g + Ff F = force motor must balance m = mass (load) g = acceleration due to gravity (here taken as 10 m s−2) Ff = frictional force
  • Compute: F = (1800 × 10) + 4000 = 22000 N
  • Minimum motor power (just balances resistive forces): P = Fv
Power at constant speed against a force: P = F v P = power F = opposing force magnitude (here total downward resisting force) v = speed
  • P = 22000 × 2 = 44000 W
  • In horsepower: 44000 / 746 ≈ 59 hp
For constant-speed lifting with friction, include both mg and friction in the resisting force; using only mg underestimates power.

Collisions

In collisions, use conservation laws to link masses, speeds, and directions after impact. The key conserved quantities (when conditions allow) are momentum and energy.

Set-up of an idealised two-body collision

  • Two masses: m1 and m2.
  • m1 initially moves with speed v1i (i = initial).
  • Often take m2 initially at rest; no loss of generality (just a convenient frame/starting condition).
  • After collision, both masses “fly off” in different directions; there are relationships linking masses, velocities, and angles.
This section sets up using momentum + energy conservation for collisions; the detailed relationships come after this intro set-up.

Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

Linear momentum is conserved in all collisions: total initial momentum of an isolated system = total final momentum.

Impulse argument for momentum conservation

During a collision, the objects exert mutual impulsive forces over time Δt, changing momenta:
Δp1 = F12 Δt Δp2 = F21 Δt Δp1, Δp2: momentum change of particle 1 and 2 during collision F12: force on particle 1 due to particle 2 F21: force on particle 2 due to particle 1 Δt: collision duration
By Newton’s third law, F12 = −F21 at every instant (even if the forces vary during the collision).
So adding impulses:
Δp1 + Δp2 = 0 Meaning: total momentum change of the 2-particle system is zero ⇒ total momentum conserved.

Kinetic energy during collisions: elastic vs inelastic

Kinetic energy is not always conserved in collisions: impact/deformation can turn KE into heat + sound (other forms).
Useful picture: a “compressed spring” between the bodies (stands for deformation):
  • If the “spring” fully returns to its original shape with no energy loss, then initial KE = final KE (but KE during the collision time Δt is not constant).
  • If the deformation isn’t relieved and they move together afterwards, they share a common final velocity.
Elastic collision: total KE conserved (initial = final). Completely inelastic collision: bodies stick together after collision (maximum KE loss allowed while still conserving momentum). Inelastic collision: in-between; deformation partly relieved; some KE lost.
Exam trap: Momentum is conserved in all collisions (isolated system), but kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions.

Collisions in One Dimension

1D (head-on) collision: initial + final velocities of both bodies lie on the same straight line.

Completely inelastic collision in 1D (head-on)

Given head-on setup ⇒ angles are zero: θ1 = θ2 = 0 Momentum conservation:
m1v1i = (m1 + m2)vf m1, m2 = masses v1i = initial velocity of body 1 (body 2 initially at rest here) vf = common final velocity (they stick)
vf = (m1/(m1+m2)) v1i   (Eq. 5.22) vf = final common velocity
Kinetic energy loss in the collision:
ΔK = (1/2)m1v1i2 − (1/2)(m1+m2)vf2 ΔK = KE lost (goes into other forms)
Using Eq. (5.22) for vf, simplifies to:
ΔK = (1/2) (m1m2/(m1+m2)) v1i2 ΔK > 0 (as expected for completely inelastic collision)
Completely inelastic (stick together): momentum gives vf, and KE loss is always positive.

Elastic collision in 1D (head-on)

For θ1 = θ2 = 0, use momentum + KE conservation:
m1v1i = m1v1f + m2v2f   (Eq. 5.23) v1f, v2f = final velocities of bodies 1 and 2
(1/2)m1v1i2 = (1/2)m1v1f2 + (1/2)m2v2f2   (Eq. 5.24)
From Eqs. (5.23) and (5.24):
v2f = v1i + v1f   (Eq. 5.25) Meaning: in 1D elastic collision (target initially at rest), v2f links directly to v1i and v1f.
Put Eq. (5.25) into Eq. (5.23) ⇒ final velocities:
v1f = ((m1 − m2)/(m1 + m2)) v1i   (Eq. 5.26) v1f = final velocity of incident mass m1
v2f = (2m1/(m1 + m2)) v1i   (Eq. 5.27) v2f = final velocity of initially stationary mass m2
1D elastic collision (target at rest): unknowns {v1f, v2f} are fixed uniquely only if you use both momentum + KE conservation.

Special cases (exam favourites)

  • Case I: m1 = m2
    • v1f = 0
    • v2f = v1i
    Interpretation: mass 1 stops; its speed is passed to mass 2.
  • Case II: m2 >> m1
    • v1f ≈ −v1i
    • v2f ≈ 0
    Interpretation: heavy mass barely moves; light mass rebounds (velocity reverses).
Common mistake: using elastic formulas (Eqs. 5.26–5.27) for an inelastic collision. If they stick, use Eq. 5.22, then find energy loss separately.

Example 5.11: Slowing down of neutrons (moderator concept)

In reactors, fast neutrons (~107 m s−1) must be slowed to ~103 m s−1 to increase chance of interaction with 235U (Z = 92) for fission. Moderator: material with light nuclei (e.g. heavy water D2O or graphite) that slows neutrons mainly via elastic collisions.

Energy transfer in a 1D elastic collision with a stationary nucleus

Neutron = particle 1 (mass m1), nucleus = particle 2 (mass m2), initially at rest. Initial KE of neutron:
K1i = (1/2)m1v1i2
From Eq. (5.26): v1f = ((m1−m2)/(m1+m2))v1i. So neutron final KE:
K1f = (1/2)m1v1f2 = (1/2)m1v1i2 ((m1−m2)/(m1+m2))2
Fraction of neutron KE remaining after collision:
f1 = K1f/K1i = ((m1−m2)/(m1+m2))2 f1 = fraction of neutron energy retained
Elastic collision ⇒ energy lost by neutron = energy gained by nucleus:
f2 = K2f/K1i = 1 − f1 = (4m1m2)/(m1+m2)2 f2 = fraction of neutron’s initial energy transferred to nucleus
This also checks out by substituting v2f from Eq. (5.27) into K2f = (1/2)m2v2f2.

Numerical illustrations (given results)

  • Deuterium nucleus: m2 = 2m1
    • f1 = 1/9
    • f2 = 8/9
    Interpretation: ~90% of neutron energy can transfer in one elastic hit.
  • Carbon nucleus: (mass a few times neutron mass)
    • f1 = 71.6%
    • f2 = 28.4%
Practical note: average energy transfer is smaller since perfectly head-on collisions are rare.

One-dimensional vs Two-dimensional Collisions

1D collision (head-on): initial + final velocities of both bodies lie along the same straight line. For small spheres: happens when body 1’s line of travel passes through the centre of body 2 (initially at rest). 2D collision: initial + final velocities lie in a plane (more general).

Collisions in Two Dimensions

Vector momentum conservation and choice of plane

Linear momentum is conserved. Since p is a vector, conservation gives component equations in the {x, y, z} directions. Pick the plane set by the final velocity directions of m1 and m2 as the x–y plane. Then conservation of pz ⇒ the whole collision stays in the x–y plane.

Component equations (m2 initially at rest)

m1v1i = m1v1fcosθ1 + m2v2fcosθ2   (Eq. 5.28) θ1, θ2 = scattering angles of m1, m2 measured from the initial direction (x-axis)
0 = m1v1fsinθ1 − m2v2fsinθ2   (Eq. 5.29)
Usually known: {m1, m2, v1i} Unknown: {v1f, v2f, θ1, θ2} ⇒ 4 unknowns but only 2 momentum equations.
If θ1 = θ2 = 0, Eq. (5.28) collapses to the 1D momentum equation (Eq. 5.23).

Elastic collision condition in 2D

If the collision is elastic, kinetic energy is also conserved → gives one extra equation:
(1/2)m1v1i2 = (1/2)m1v1f2 + (1/2)m2v2f2   (Eq. 5.30)
Even with Eq. (5.30): 3 equations for 4 unknowns. So at least one of {v1f, v2f, θ1, θ2} must be known (measured) to finish the problem. Example approach: measure θ1 experimentally (detector swept from x- to y-axis). With {m1, m2, v1i, θ1} known, solve Eqs. (5.28)–(5.30) for {v1f, v2f, θ2}.
Exam tip: For 2D collisions, always count unknowns vs independent equations. Momentum → 2 component equations in the plane; add energy only if the collision is elastic.

Example 5.12: Elastic collision of equal-mass billiard balls (cue and target)

Two billiard balls: m1 = m2. Ball 1 (cue) hits ball 2 (target, initially at rest). Elastic collision; ignore friction + rotation. Target must go to a corner pocket at θ2 = 37°. Find θ1.

Momentum relation for equal masses

For equal masses, vector momentum conservation becomes:
v1i = v1f + v2f v1i = initial velocity vector (cue ball) v1f, v2f = final velocity vectors (cue + target)
Square it (dot with itself) to connect magnitudes:
v1i2 = (v1f + v2f)·(v1f + v2f) So, v1i2 = v1f2 + v2f2 + 2v1f·v2f
For an elastic collision (equal masses, target initially at rest), energy conservation gives:
v1i2 = v1f2 + v2f2
Compare with the squared-momentum result ⇒
v1f·v2f = 0 So the two final velocity vectors are perpendicular ⇒ θ1 + θ2 = 90°.
Given θ2 = 37°, then:
θ1 = 90° − 37° = 53°
Billiards key result: Elastic collision + equal masses + one initially at rest ⇒ the two outgoing velocity vectors are at right angles.

Glancing Elastic Collision of Equal Masses (One Initially at Rest)

Result proved: In a glancing elastic collision between two equal masses with one initially at rest, they move off at 90° to each other.
Vector/component relation used in the worked proof (as given): vi2 = vf12 + vf22 + 2 vf1 vf2 cos(θ1 + 37°)   (Eq. 5.31) Symbols: vi = initial speed of the incident (moving) mass (other mass starts at rest) vf1, vf2 = final speeds after collision θ1 = one scattering/deflection angle from the collision geometry 37° = given angle from the specific example geometry
Kinetic energy conservation (elastic, m1 = m2, one initially at rest): vi2 = vf12 + vf22   (Eq. 5.32) Symbols: vi = initial speed of the moving mass vf1, vf2 = final speeds of the two masses
Key step: Compare Eq. (5.31) vs Eq. (5.32). First two RHS terms match, so the extra term must be zero: 2 vf1 vf2 cos(θ1 + 37°) = 0 ⟹ cos(θ1 + 37°) = 0 So, θ1 + 37° = 90° ⟹ θ1 = 53°. Physical meaning: cos(...) = 0 ⇒ angle between the outgoing velocity vectors is 90° ⇒ final velocities are perpendicular.
Exam tip: Right-angle scattering only holds if: (i) elastic, (ii) equal masses, (iii) one mass initially at rest. Change any condition → 90° result may fail.

Collisions of Smooth Spheres (Contact Collisions) vs Scattering (Action at a Distance)

Contact collision model (smooth spherical masses): Much simpler if we treat bodies as smooth spheres and assume collision only when they touch. Examples: marbles, carrom, billiards (most real-life collisions are contact).
Scattering: Interaction via forces at a distance (no physical contact) is called scattering. Examples: comet deflected by Sun’s gravity; alpha particle approaching a nucleus then leaving in some direction. What determines outcomes: outgoing speeds/directions depend on:
  • initial velocities,
  • type of interaction,
  • masses,
  • shapes and sizes.
Common confusion: In everyday mechanics, “collision” usually means contact; in atomic/nuclear physics, “collision” is often really scattering from long-range forces.

summary-work energy and power

Work–Energy Theorem

Work–Energy (WE) theorem: change in kinetic energy = net work done on the body.
Kf − Ki = Wnet Symbols: Ki = initial kinetic energy Kf = final kinetic energy Wnet = work done by the net force over the displacement

Conservative Force: Definition and Tests

A force is conservative if (equivalently, both):
  1. Path independent: work depends only on the end points {xi, xf}, not the route taken.
  2. Zero closed-loop work: work over any closed path is 0 (back to start).

Potential Energy Function for a Conservative Force (1D)

For a 1D conservative force, define V(x) so that force is the negative gradient (in 1D: negative derivative).
F(x) = − dV(x)/dx Symbols: F(x) = force as a function of position x V(x) = potential energy as a function of x dV/dx = derivative of V with respect to x
V(xf) − V(xi) = − ∫xixf F(x) dx Symbols: xi, xf = initial and final positions F(x) = conservative force function V(xi), V(xf) = potential energies at xi and xf

Conservation of Mechanical Energy

Conservation of mechanical energy: total mechanical energy stays constant if only conservative forces do work.
Mechanical energy = kinetic + potential. If non-conservative forces (e.g. friction) do work, mechanical energy is not conserved.

Gravitational Potential Energy Near Earth’s Surface

V(x) = m g x Condition: valid when change of g with height is ignored (near Earth’s surface). Symbols: V(x) = gravitational potential energy at height x m = mass of particle g = acceleration due to gravity (taken constant) x = height above chosen reference level

Elastic Potential Energy of a Spring

V(x) = (1/2) k x2 Symbols: V(x) = elastic potential energy k = spring (force) constant x = extension (or compression magnitude) from equilibrium

Scalar (Dot) Product of Two Vectors

Definition: dot product of vectors A and B is a scalar, written A·B, defined as:
A · B = AB cos θ Symbols: A = magnitude of vector A B = magnitude of vector B θ = angle between A and B
  • A·B can be +, −, or 0 depending on θ.
  • Meaning: A·B = (magnitude of one) × (component of the other along the first).
Dot products of unit vectors: î·î = ĵ·ĵ = k̂·k̂ = 1 î·ĵ = ĵ·k̂ = k̂·î = 0
Algebraic laws: scalar products follow:
  • Commutative: A·B = B·A
  • Distributive: A·(B + C) = A·B + A·C

Points to Ponder (High-Yield Exam Notes)

1) Work Done Must Specify the Force and Displacement

Saying “calculate the work done” is incomplete unless you state:
  • which force (one force or a set of forces),
  • on which body,
  • over what displacement.

2) Work is a Scalar; Can be Positive or Negative

  • Work is a scalar → can be + or −.
  • Mass and kinetic energy are positive scalars.
  • Work by friction/viscous force on a moving body is negative (opposes motion).

3) Newton’s Third Law: Forces Cancel but Works Need Not

Newton’s third law (mutual forces): F12 + F21 = 0 Symbols: F12 = force on body 1 due to body 2 F21 = force on body 2 due to body 1
Even if F12 + F21 = 0, the works may not cancel: W12 + W21 ≠ 0 (in general), though sometimes it can be. Reason: work depends on force and displacement of the point of application; the two bodies can move by different amounts while forces act.

4) WE Theorem Can Be Used Without Knowing Detailed Force Law

Sometimes W (so ΔK) can be found even when the exact force law isn’t known (as in the referenced WE-theorem example).

5) WE Theorem and Newton’s Second Law are Not Independent

  • WE theorem is basically a scalar form of Newton’s 2nd law.
  • Mechanical energy conservation is a result of WE theorem when only conservative forces do work.

6) Frames of Reference: Inertial and Non-Inertial

  • WE theorem works in all inertial frames.
  • In non-inertial frames, include pseudo forces in the net force for the work calculation.

7) Potential Energy Zero Level is Arbitrary (Up to a Constant)

For a conservative force, potential energy is defined only up to an added constant. Choosing V = 0 is just a convention:
  • For gravitational PE mgh, V = 0 often at the ground.
  • For spring PE (1/2)kx2, V = 0 at the equilibrium position.

8) Not Every Force Has a Potential Energy

Friction is non-conservative: work by friction over a closed loop is not zero, so there’s no single-valued potential energy function for friction.

9) Momentum Conservation vs Kinetic Energy During Collision

  • In a collision, total linear momentum is conserved at each instant.
  • Kinetic energy conservation (even if collision is elastic) applies after the collision ends; it does not generally hold at every instant during contact.
  • Physical reason: bodies deform; they can be momentarily at rest relative to each other while in contact.
Exam pitfall: “Elastic” does not mean KE is constant throughout the collision. KE is conserved only between initial and final states, not necessarily during the interaction time.
