CHAPTER NINE

9.1 Introduction

Fluids: Liquids + gases can flow → called fluids. Flowing ability is the basic feature that separates them from solids.
Why study fluids? Fluids are everywhere: Earth is surrounded by air + ~2/3 surface is water. Water is essential for life; mammals are mostly water, and biological processes (incl. plants) happen via fluids. So knowing fluid behaviour/properties matters.

How fluids differ from solids; what is common to liquids and gases

  • Shape: A fluid has no definite shape of its own (unlike a solid).
  • Volume:
    • Solids and liquids: have a fixed volume (i.e., volume under atmospheric pressure).
    • Gases: occupy the entire volume of the container.
  • Effect of stress/pressure on volume: Volume of solid/liquid/gas depends on the stress/pressure on it. (Even solids can have volume changed by stress.)
  • Compressibility comparison: Solids + liquids: volume change with external pressure is small → much lower compressibility than gases. Gases: large volume change with pressure.

Shear stress: key mechanical distinction

Key fluid property: Fluids offer very little resistance to shear stress → even tiny shear stress changes their shape.
  • Solids under shear stress: shear can change shape while keeping volume fixed.
  • Fluids under shear stress: resistance is extremely small → fluid changes shape easily (i.e., flows).
  • Magnitude comparison: shearing stress in fluids is about 106 times smaller than in solids.
Exam tip: “Fixed volume” (solids/liquids) isn’t absolute—means approximately constant at atmospheric pressure. Actually, volume depends on external pressure for all states.

9.2 Pressure

Everyday motivation: force + area

  • Sharp needle pierces skin easily; blunt object with bigger contact area (e.g., spoon back) doesn’t, even with same force.
  • Elephant on a man’s chest can crack ribs; but a large, light, strong wooden plank spreading the area (circus trick) can save him.
Pressure: “impact” when force acts over an area; same force → smaller area ⇒ larger pressure.

Force exerted by a fluid at rest is normal to surfaces

A fluid at rest exerts force always perpendicular (normal) to any surface touching it.
Reasoning (must know): If there were any parallel component, then (Newton’s 3rd law) the surface would exert an equal/opposite parallel force on the fluid → fluid would start flowing along the surface. But fluid is at rest, so no such flow → force must be purely normal.

Measuring pressure: idealised piston-spring device

  • Ideal pressure device: an evacuated chamber with a spring calibrated to measure force on a piston.
  • Put it at a point in the fluid: fluid pushes piston inward, spring pushes outward until balance; spring reading gives the force due to fluid
Average pressure Pav = F / A
  • Pav: average pressure over the area
  • F: magnitude of normal force by fluid on piston/surface
  • A: area over which the force acts
Pressure at a point P = limΔA→0 (ΔF / ΔA)
  • P: pressure at a point
  • ΔF: normal force on a tiny area element
  • ΔA: tiny area element (shrinks to zero in the limit)
Pressure is a scalar. Use only the normal component of force, not the full force vector.

Dimensions and units of pressure

  • Dimensions: [M L−1 T−2]
  • SI unit: N m−2 = pascal (Pa), named after Blaise Pascal (1623–1662) (fluid pressure studies).
  • Common unit: atmosphere (atm) = atmospheric pressure at sea level:
    • 1 atm = 1.013 × 105 Pa

Density: definition, properties, and variation with pressure

Density ρ = m / V
  • ρ: density of the fluid
  • m: mass of fluid
  • V: volume occupied
  • Dimensions of density: [M L−3]
  • SI unit: kg m−3
  • Nature: positive scalar quantity.
  • Liquids: almost incompressible → density ~constant at all pressures.
  • Gases: density changes a lot with pressure (large variation).

Density of water at 4°C and relative density

  • Density of water at 4°C (277 K): 1.0 × 103 kg m−3
Relative density: (density of substance) ÷ (density of water at 4°C). Dimensionless + positive scalar.
  • Example: Relative density of aluminium = 2.7 ⇒ density of aluminium = 2.7 × 103 kg m−3.

STP (standard conditions)

STP: standard temperature 0°C and standard pressure 1 atm.
Common exam mistakes:
  • Using total force instead of the normal component in pressure calculations.
  • Mixing up Pa (N m−2) with N m−1 or other units.
  • Forgetting relative density has no units and is w.r.t. water at 4°C (not room temp).

Example

The two thigh bones (femurs), each of cross-sectional area 10 cm2 support the upper part of a human body of mass 40 kg. Estimate the average pressure sustained by the femurs.
Average pressure on a support surface: Pressure = normal force per unit area (force must be normal to the area).
Average pressure: Pav = F/A Pav = average pressure (Pa = N m−2) F = normal force on the surface (N) A = area over which the force acts (m2)
Given: Two femurs, each cross-sectional area = 10 cm2; mass supported = 40 kg.
  1. Total area supporting the body: A = 2 × 10 cm2 = 20 cm2 = 20 × 10−4 m2
  2. Force due to weight (take g = 10 m s−2): F = mg = 40 × 10 = 400 N Acts vertically downward → normal to the horizontal femur cross-sections.
  3. Average pressure: Pav = F/A = 400/(20 × 10−4) = 2 × 105 N m−2 = 2 × 105 Pa
Exam tip: Convert cm2 → m2: 1 cm2 = 10−4 m2. Also use only the normal force component for pressure (not tangential).

9.2.1 Pascal’s Law

Pascal’s law (fluid at rest): Pressure at a point in a static fluid is same in all directions. Also, pressure is same at all points on the same height (same horizontal level).
Pascal’s observation: In a fluid at rest, pressure is same at all points if they’re at the same height. Demonstration idea (right-angled prism element ABC-DEF):
  • Pick a tiny prismatic fluid element inside a static fluid so it’s effectively at the same depth everywhere (ignore gravity variation across it).
  • In a static fluid, forces from surrounding fluid on each face are normal to that face (no shear stress).
  • Let pressures on three faces be Pa, Pb, Pc giving normal forces Fa, Fb, Fc on areas Aa, Ab, Ac.
Equilibrium of forces on the prism (inclined face at angle θ):
  • Fb sinθ = Fc
  • Fb cosθ = Fa
Geometrical relations between face areas:
  • Ab sinθ = Ac
  • Ab cosθ = Aa
Equality of pressure in all directions: Fa/Aa = Fb/Ab = Fc/Ac Pa = Pb = Pc Fa, Fb, Fc = normal forces on faces a, b, c Aa, Ab, Ac = areas of faces a, b, c Pa, Pb, Pc = pressures on faces a, b, c
Key consequences:
  • Pressure is not a vector. Pressure causes force, but pressure itself has no direction; at a point it has the same value in all directions (static fluid).
  • Force due to pressure is always normal to any area in/around a static fluid, مهما كانت orientation.
Pressure same in a horizontal plane (static fluid):
  • Imagine a fluid element like a horizontal bar with uniform cross-section, in equilibrium.
  • Horizontal forces on the two ends must balance ⇒ pressures at both ends are equal.
  • If pressure differed along the same horizontal level, there’d be a net force → flow starts.
  • So, no flow ⇒ same pressure everywhere in a horizontal plane.
Common mistake: Saying “pressure acts downward.” In a static fluid, pressure at a point is same in every direction; only the force on a surface has a direction (normal to that surface).

9.2.2 Variation of Pressure with Depth

Setup: Fluid at rest in a container. Two points: point 1 is height h above point 2. Pressures are P1 (upper) and P2 (lower). Take a cylindrical fluid element (base area A, height h) between the two points.
  • Horizontal forces cancel (static).
  • Vertical forces balance the weight of the cylinder.
Vertical forces:
  • Top downward force due to pressure: P1A
  • Bottom upward force due to pressure: P2A
  • Weight of fluid element: mg
Pressure difference balances weight : (P2 − P1)A = mg P1, P2 = pressures at upper/lower points (Pa) A = cross-sectional area (m2) m = mass of fluid element (kg) g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2)
Using density: If ρ is density, then m = ρV = ρ(hA).
Hydrostatic pressure difference : P2 − P1 = ρgh ρ = fluid density (kg m−3) g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2) h = vertical separation / depth difference (m)
Fluid open to atmosphere: If upper point is the free surface open to air, then P1 = Pa and P2 = P (pressure at depth h).
Absolute pressure at depth h : P = Pa + ρgh P = absolute pressure at depth h (Pa) Pa = atmospheric pressure at surface (Pa) ρ = fluid density (kg m−3) g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2) h = depth below free surface (m)
Gauge pressure: Extra pressure above atmospheric pressure. Gauge pressure = P − Pa = ρgh
What pressure depends on (hydrostatics):
  • Depends on: depth h, density ρ, g.
  • Does not depend on: cross-sectional area, base area, or container shape (A cancels).
  • So pressure is same at all points on the same horizontal level (same depth).
Hydrostatic paradox: Three connected vessels A, B, C (different shapes) filled with water to the same height. pressure at the bottom is the same under each section as pressure depends only on depth (ρgh), not shape.

Example 9.2: What is the pressure on a swimmer 10 m below the surface of a lake?

Given: h = 10 m, ρ = 1000 kg m−3, g = 10 m s−2, Pa = 1.01 × 105 Pa. Using Eq. (9.7): P = Pa + ρgh = 1.01 × 105 + (1000)(10)(10) = 2.01 × 105 Pa ≈ 2 atm
Exam tip: Unless they explicitly ask for gauge pressure, give absolute pressure: P = Pa + ρgh.

9.2.3 Atmospheric Pressure and Gauge Pressure

Atmospheric pressure at a point: Equals the weight of an air column of unit cross-sectional area from that point up to the top of atmosphere. At sea level: 1.013 × 105 Pa (= 1 atm).
Torricelli’s method (Evangelista Torricelli, 1608–1647): Mercury barometer
  • Long glass tube (closed at one end) is filled completely with mercury.
  • Tube is inverted into a trough of mercury.
  • Space above mercury column has only mercury vapour; its pressure P is very small → neglected.
  • So pressure at the top (Point A) ≈ 0.
  • Pressure inside mercury at point B (same level as point C in trough) must equal outside pressure there = atmospheric pressure Pa.
Barometer relation : Pa = ρgh Pa = atmospheric pressure (Pa) ρ = density of mercury (kg m−3) g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2) h = height of mercury column (m)
Common mistake: Confusing absolute vs gauge. For open fluids: absolute P = Pa + ρgh, while gauge = ρgh.

Pressure Units, Barometer and Manometer

Atmospheric pressure from mercury barometer

At sea level, the mercury column height in a barometer is about 76 cm ⇒ this is 1 atmosphere (1 atm).
Pressure is often quoted as a mercury-column height: cm of Hg or mm of Hg.
Torr: Pressure equal to 1 mm of Hg is called 1 torr (Torricelli).
1 torr = 133 Pa torr: millimetre of mercury pressure unit Pa: pascal (SI), N m−2
Uses: mm of Hg and torr are widely used in medicine & physiology.
Bar & millibar: common pressure units in meteorology.
1 bar = 105 Pa bar: practical pressure unit Pa: SI unit of pressure

Open tube manometer (pressure difference)

Open tube manometer: U-tube with a suitable liquid to measure pressure difference. One end open to atmosphere; other end connected to the system.
Choosing the manometer liquid:
  • Low density (e.g. oil) → for small pressure differences (bigger height difference).
  • High density (e.g. mercury) → for large pressure differences (height stays manageable).
Same level rule (U-tube): Pressure is same at the same horizontal level in the same continuous fluid (both arms).
In an open-tube manometer: pressure P at point A = pressure at point B (same level in manometric fluid). The height difference h gives the gauge pressure.
Gauge pressure: Pg = P − Pa = ρgh Pg: gauge pressure (above atmospheric) P: absolute pressure (system) Pa: atmospheric pressure ρ: density of manometer liquid g: acceleration due to gravity h: level difference of the liquid columns

Compressibility: liquids vs gases

Liquids: density changes very little over big pressure/temperature ranges ⇒ treat ρ ≈ constant ⇒ liquids ~ incompressible (for these problems). Gases: density changes a lot with pressure/temperature ⇒ generally not incompressible.

Example 9.3: The density of the atmosphere at sea level is 1.29 kg/m3. Assume that it does not change with altitude. Then how high would the atmosphere extend?

Given: air density at sea level ρ = 1.29 kg m−3, assume it stays constant with altitude. Find height h such that pressure = sea-level atmospheric pressure.
Hydrostatic relation: ρgh = P (here P = atmospheric pressure) ρ: density of air (assumed constant) g: acceleration due to gravity h: height of air column P: pressure due to column (≈ 1.01 × 105 Pa)
Use ρgh = 1.01 × 105 Pa: 1.29 × 9.8 × h = 1.01 × 105 h = 7989 m ≈ 8 km
Reality check: Actually, air density decreases with height, and g also decreases. So the atmosphere extends (with falling pressure) to > 100 km.
Exam pitfall: The “8 km atmosphere height” is from the false constant-density assumption—don’t quote it as the real thickness.
Sea-level atmospheric pressure is not always exactly 760 mm Hg. A drop by 10 mm Hg or more suggests an approaching storm.

Example At a depth of 1000 m in an ocean (a) what is the absolute pressure? (b) What is the gauge pressure? (c) Find the force acting on the window of area 20 cm × 20 cm of a submarine at this depth, the interior of which is maintained at sealevel atmospheric pressure. (The density of sea water is 1.03 × 103 kg m-3, g = 10 m s–2.)

Given: depth h = 1000 m, sea water density ρ = 1.03 × 103 kg m−3, g = 10 m s−2, atmospheric pressure Pa = 1.01 × 105 Pa. Find: (a) absolute pressure, (b) gauge pressure, (c) force on a 20 cm × 20 cm submarine window when inside is kept at sea-level atmospheric pressure.
Absolute pressure at depth: P = Pa + ρgh P: absolute pressure at depth Pa: atmospheric pressure at surface ρ: density of water g: acceleration due to gravity h: depth below surface
(a) Absolute pressure P = 1.01 × 105 + (1.03 × 103) × 10 × 1000 = 1.01 × 105 + 1.03 × 107 = 104.01 × 105 Pa ≈ 104 atm
Gauge pressure: Pg = P − Pa = ρgh Pg: gauge pressure P: absolute pressure Pa: atmospheric pressure ρ, g, h: as above
(b) Gauge pressure Pg = ρgh = (1.03 × 103) × 10 × 1000 = 103 × 105 Pa ≈ 103 atm
Force from pressure difference: F = Pnet A F: net force on the surface Pnet: net pressure difference across the surface A: area of the surface
(c) Force on the window
  • Outside pressure: Pout = Pa + ρgh
  • Inside pressure kept at: Pin = Pa
  • Net pressure: Pnet = Pout − Pin = ρgh = Pg
Area: A = (20 cm × 20 cm) = (0.2 m × 0.2 m) = 0.04 m2 F = PgA = (103 × 105) × 0.04 = 4.12 × 105 N
Exam pitfall: For forces on submarine walls/windows use the pressure difference (usually the gauge pressure if inside ≈ atmospheric), not the absolute pressure.

9.2.4 Hydraulic Machines (Pascal’s Law Applications)

Pressure transmission in a confined fluid (Pascal’s law form)

If you apply external pressure anywhere on a fluid inside a closed vessel, that pressure is transmitted undiminished and equally in all directions through the fluid.
Demo idea: horizontal cylinder + several vertical tubes → liquid levels equal. Push piston → pressure increases → liquid rises to the same level in all tubes ⇒ pressure spreads uniformly.

Hydraulic lift: principle, force multiplication, mechanical advantage

Hydraulic lift: Two pistons with an incompressible liquid between them. Force on the small piston makes pressure in the fluid, which acts on the big piston → bigger output force.
Input pressure: P = F1/A1 P = fluid pressure F1 = force on small piston A1 = area of small piston
Output force (large piston): F2 = PA2 = (F1/A1)A2 = F1(A2/A1) F2 = upward force on large piston A2 = area of large piston F1, A1, P = as above
Mechanical advantage (hydraulic lift): MA = A2/A1 MA = force multiplication factor A2 = large piston area A1 = small piston area
Change the force on the small piston → platform on big piston moves up/down; force gets multiplied by A2/A1.

Example Two syringes of different cross-sections (without needles) filled with water are connected with a tightly fitted rubber tube filled with water. Diameters of the smaller piston and larger piston are 1.0 cm and 3.0 cm respectively. (a) Find the force exerted on the larger piston when a force of 10 N is applied to the smaller piston. (b) If the smaller piston is pushed in through 6.0 cm, how much does the larger piston move out?

Setup: Two syringes (no needles), different cross-sections, filled with water + connected by a tight tube also filled with water.
  • Small piston diameter: d1 = 1.0 cm
  • Large piston diameter: d2 = 3.0 cm
  • Applied force (small piston): F1 = 10 N
Find: (a) force on large piston, (b) displacement of large piston if small piston is pushed in by 6.0 cm.
(a) Pascal’s law ⇒ same pressure in connected fluid ⇒ F1/A1 = F2/A2.
Area from diameter: A = π(d/2)2 A = cross-sectional area d = piston diameter
(a) Force on larger piston
  • A1 = π(0.5 cm)2
  • A2 = π(1.5 cm)2
  • So, A2/A1 = (1.5/0.5)2 = 32 = 9
F2 = F1(A2/A1) = 10 × 9 = 90 N
(b) Treat water as perfectly incompressible ⇒ volume in = volume out.
Volume continuity (incompressible fluid): A1L1 = A2L2 L1 = inward displacement of small piston L2 = outward displacement of large piston A1, A2 = piston areas
(b) Displacement of larger piston L2 = (A1/A2)L1 = (1/9) × 6.0 cm = 0.67 cm (= 0.67 × 10−2 m)
Exam tip: In syringe / hydraulic lift Qs: force multiplies by A2/A1, but displacement reduces by A1/A2 (volume conservation). Note: Atmospheric pressure acts on both pistons → cancels out, so ignore it in pressure comparison.

Example In a car lift compressed air exerts a force F1 on a small piston having a radius of 5.0 cm. This pressure is transmitted to a second piston of radius 15 cm (Fig 9.7). If the mass of the car to be lifted is 1350 kg, calculate F1. What is the pressure necessary to accomplish this task? (g = 9.8 ms–2).

Given: r1 = 5.0 cm (small piston), r2 = 15 cm (large piston), m = 1350 kg, g = 9.8 m s−2. Find: input force F1 on small piston + required pressure.
Pascal relation (lift): F1/A1 = F2/A2 F2 = load on large piston (= car weight = mg) A1, A2 = piston areas (= πr2)
Weight lifted: F2 = mg m = car mass g = gravitational acceleration
F2 = 1350 × 9.8 = 13230 N Area ratio: A1/A2 = (r1/r2)2 = (5/15)2 = (1/3)2 = 1/9 So F1 = F2(A1/A2) = 13230/9 = 1470 N ≈ 1.5 × 103 N
Pressure needed: P = F1/A1 P = pressure of compressed air (passed through the fluid system) F1 = force on small piston A1 = small piston area (= πr12)
A1 = π(0.05 m)2 = π × 2.5 × 10−3 ≈ 7.85 × 10−3 m2 P = 1470 / (7.85 × 10−3) ≈ 1.87 × 105 Pa
Required pressure is almost 2× atmospheric pressure (since atm ≈ 1.01 × 105 Pa).

Hydraulic brakes (application of Pascal’s law)

Hydraulic brakes: Small pedal force pushes a master piston (master cylinder) → creates pressure in brake oil. This pressure acts on wheel pistons of larger area → gives a large retarding force.
Key advantage: Pedal pressure is transmitted equally to all wheel cylinders → equal braking effort on all wheels.

9.3 Streamline Flow

Fluid dynamics and types of flow (steady vs non-smooth)

Fluid dynamics: study of fluids in motion.
Observation: turn on a tap slowly → flow starts smooth; increase speed → flow can become non-smooth.
Steady flow: at any fixed point, the velocity of fluid particles passing that point stays constant with time.
Steady flow ≠ same velocity everywhere. Velocity can change from place to place, but at one fixed point, successive particles have the same velocity over time.
In steady (streamline) flow: each particle follows a smooth path, and paths do not cross.

Streamline: definition and meaning

Streamline: the path followed by a fluid particle in steady flow. Geometrical meaning: a streamline is a curve whose tangent at any point points along the fluid velocity at that point.
If a particle moves along a path (e.g. curve PQ), that curve shows its motion with time; in steady flow, such paths are streamlines.

Streamlines, Steady Flow and the Equation of Continuity

Streamline: curve in a flowing fluid where the tangent at any point gives the direction of velocity there (a “flow map”). Steady (streamline) flow: the streamline pattern stays fixed in time (flow map doesn’t change).
No two streamlines can cross. If they crossed, a particle at the intersection would have two possible directions → flow can’t be steady.
Why streamlines look closer in narrow regions: If we drew one streamline per particle, we’d get infinitely many. Instead, take cross-sections (planes ⟂ to flow) at P, R, Q, chosen so their boundaries are the same set of streamlines. Then the same set of fluid particles passes each section in the same time. Let areas be AP, AR, AQ; speeds vP, vR, vQ; densities ρP, ρR, ρQ.
Mass crossing a section in time Δt: Δm = ρ A v Δt ρ = density at that section A = cross-sectional area normal to flow v = speed at that section Δt = small time interval
So, in the same time Δt:
  • ΔmP = ρPAPvPΔt
  • ΔmR = ρRARvRΔt
  • ΔmQ = ρQAQvQΔt
Conservation of mass in flow: mass flowing out = mass flowing in (for the same streamtube bounded by streamlines).
General continuity (mass conservation) along a streamtube: ρPAPvPΔt = ρRARvRΔt = ρQAQvQΔt ρ = density at the section A = cross-sectional area at the section v = speed at the section Δt = same small time interval for each section
Incompressible fluid: density is constant along the flow → ρP = ρR = ρQ.
Equation of continuity (incompressible fluid): APvP = ARvR = AQvQ A = cross-sectional area v = speed of fluid
General form (incompressible): Av = constant A = cross-sectional area of the flow tube/pipe v = flow speed at that section
Volume flux / flow rate: Av. For incompressible steady flow, Av is constant throughout the pipe.
Consequence: narrower pipe (smaller A) → streamlines closer + speed increases; wider pipe → speed decreases. Figure-style reasoning: if AR > AQ, then vR < vQ → fluid is accelerated from R to Q. This acceleration in a horizontal pipe comes with a pressure change (quantified by Bernoulli’s principle).

Laminar and Turbulent Flow; Critical Speed

Laminar flow: smooth; speeds may differ point-to-point, but directions are parallel (streamlines neat + orderly). Turbulent flow: irregular with eddies; velocity + pressure fluctuate with time; streamlines become messy/disorderly.
Critical speed: the limiting speed beyond which steady (laminar) flow breaks down and becomes turbulent. Observation: fast streams over rocks make foamy whirlpool-like regions (“white water rapids”) → turbulence. Example mentioned: a jet of air hitting a flat plate perpendicular to it is turbulent flow.
Don’t say “continuity ⇒ pressure change” directly. Continuity gives the area–speed link. Pressure change comes from energy conservation (Bernoulli) and needs flow to be steady and (ideally) non-viscous + incompressible.

Bernoulli’s Principle

Bernoulli’s equation / principle: for steady flow of an incompressible, non-viscous fluid along a streamline, the total mechanical energy per unit volume stays constant → links pressure, speed, height.
Physical idea: In a pipe with changing cross-sectional area (and maybe changing height), speed changes (from continuity). Speed change ⇒ acceleration ⇒ needs a net force → supplied by pressure differences between regions.
Bernoulli links the pressure difference between two points to:
  • kinetic energy change (speed change), and
  • potential energy change (height/elevation change).
Developed by Daniel Bernoulli (1738).

Derivation (work–energy approach for a moving fluid element)

Take two regions in a pipe: region 1 (near BC) + region 2 (near DE). Consider the fluid initially between B and D.
  • In time Δt, fluid at B moves to C by distance v1Δt.
  • In the same time, fluid at D moves to E by distance v2Δt.
  • Pressures P1, P2 act on areas A1, A2.
Work done by pressure forces:
  • Work done on fluid at left end: W1 = P1A1(v1Δt) = P1ΔV
  • At right end, fluid does work on surroundings ⇒ work done on the fluid is negative: −P2ΔV
Net work on the fluid (by pressure forces): W1 − W2 = (P1 − P2)ΔV P1, P2 = pressures at regions 1 and 2 ΔV = volume that passes in time Δt (same at both ends by continuity)
For incompressible flow, the same ΔV passes through both regions in time Δt (continuity). Mass of this volume:
Mass passing in time Δt: Δm = ρA1v1Δt = ρΔV Δm = mass transported ρ = density (constant for incompressible flow) A1 = area at region 1 v1 = speed at region 1 ΔV = transported volume in time Δt
Energy changes for this transported volume:
Change in gravitational potential energy: ΔU = ρgΔV(h2 − h1) g = acceleration due to gravity h1, h2 = heights (elevations) of regions 1 and 2
Change in kinetic energy: ΔK = (1/2) ρΔV (v22 − v12) v1, v2 = speeds at regions 1 and 2
Apply work–energy to the fluid element:
Work–energy equation (fluid element): (P1 − P2)ΔV = (1/2)ρΔV(v22 − v12) + ρgΔV(h2 − h1) P1, P2 = pressures; ρ = density; v1, v2 = speeds; h1, h2 = heights
Divide by ΔV and rearrange:
Bernoulli between two points: P1 + (1/2)ρv12 + ρgh1 = P2 + (1/2)ρv22 + ρgh2 P = pressure at a point ρ = density of incompressible fluid v = speed of fluid at that point g = acceleration due to gravity h = height (elevation) of the point
General Bernoulli form (along a streamline): P + (1/2)ρv2 + ρgh = constant P = pressure (1/2)ρv2 = kinetic energy per unit volume ρgh = potential energy per unit volume
Verbal statement: Along a streamline, the sum of:
  • pressure energy per unit volume (P),
  • kinetic energy per unit volume ((1/2)ρv2), and
  • potential energy per unit volume (ρgh)
stays constant.

Conditions/limitations for Bernoulli’s equation

  • No friction/energy loss: derivation assumes no dissipation. Real fluids have internal friction between layers at different speeds → energy lost as heat.
  • This internal friction is viscosity. Bernoulli ideally needs zero viscosity (non-viscous).
  • Incompressible fluid: otherwise compression (elastic) energy changes matter and aren’t included.
  • Steady (non-turbulent) flow:
Exam habit: always state assumptions with Bernoulli → steady + incompressible + non-viscous, and applied along a streamline.

Special case: Fluid at rest (hydrostatics from Bernoulli)

If fluid is at rest, v = 0 everywhere ⇒ Bernoulli becomes:
Hydrostatic relation from Bernoulli: P1 + ρgh1 = P2 + ρgh2 Equivalently: (P1 − P2) = ρg(h2 − h1) P1, P2 = pressures at heights h1, h2 ρ = density of liquid; g = acceleration due to gravity
Same as the hydrostatic pressure relation (earlier Eq. 9.6): in a static fluid, pressure difference depends only on height difference.

Speed of Efflux: Torricelli’s Law (start of section)

Efflux: fluid flowing out through an opening. Torricelli’s result (idea stated here): efflux speed from an open tank follows the same-form formula as a freely falling body.
Physical setup described: Tank has liquid of density ρ. Small hole on the side at height y1 from bottom. Free surface at height y2. Air above liquid at pressure P. Use continuity (incompressible flow) between free surface (area A1, speed v1) and the hole (area A2, speed v2):
Continuity for tank efflux: v1A1 = v2A2 Equivalently: v2/v1 = A1/A2 A1 = area of free surface in tank A2 = area of hole (or jet cross-section at exit, idealised) v1 = speed of lowering of free surface v2 = efflux speed at the hole

Torricelli’s Law (Speed of Efflux from a Tank)

Efflux = liquid flowing out through a small hole in a container. Set up Bernoulli between 2 points:
  • Point 1 (at the hole/exit): speed v1, pressure P1
  • Point 2 (free surface/top): speed v2, pressure P (pressure above liquid), height y2
If tank cross-section is huge compared to hole (A2 >> A1): v2 ≈ 0 (free surface ~ at rest).
Bernoulli between points 1 and 2 (used here): P1 + ½ρv12 + ρgy1 = P + ½ρv22 + ρgy2 P1 = pressure at hole (often atmospheric), i.e. Pa if hole opens to air P = pressure on free surface (Pa for open tank; can be much larger if pressurised) ρ = fluid density v1 = efflux speed at hole v2 = free-surface speed (≈ 0 if A2 >> A1) g = acceleration due to gravity y1, y2 = heights (vertical coordinates) of points 1 and 2
For a jet into air at the hole: P1 = Pa (atmospheric). Take v2 = 0 and define h = y2 − y1 (height drop from surface to hole). Then:
Efflux relation : (P − Pa)/ρ = ½v12 + gh P = pressure at liquid surface inside tank Pa = atmospheric pressure h = depth of hole below free surface (vertical separation)
Pressurised tank: if P >> Pa and 2gh is negligible, efflux speed mainly comes from tank pressure (relevant to rocket propulsion).
Open tank (most common): tank open to air ⇒ P = Pa. Put into Eq. (9.14):
Torricelli’s law : v1 = √(2gh) v1 = efflux speed from hole g = acceleration due to gravity h = vertical depth of hole below free surface
Meaning: v1 = √(2gh) is exactly the free-fall speed after dropping height h. So efflux speed = free-fall speed from surface level down to the hole level.
Exam tip: v2 ≈ 0 only if A2 >> A1. If areas are similar, the surface moves ⇒ you cannot set v2 = 0.

Dynamic Lift (Bernoulli Explanation)

Dynamic lift = force on a moving body (wing / hydrofoil / spinning ball) due to motion through a fluid, caused by pressure differences linked to different fluid speeds (Bernoulli).

Spinning and Non-spinning Ball (Magnus Effect)

(i) Ball moving without spin:
  • Streamlines are symmetric (same above and below).
  • So speed at matching points above/below is the same.
  • Hence pressure above = pressure below ⇒ no net up/down force from pressure difference.
(ii) Ball moving with spin:
  • Spin makes the ball drag air with it (more if the surface is rough).
  • In the ball’s frame, air flows backward; because of spin:
    • air speed above the ball (relative to ball) becomes larger
    • air speed below the ball becomes smaller
  • Streamlines get crowded above (high speed) and rarified below (low speed).
  • High speed ⇒ low pressure above; low speed ⇒ high pressure below ⇒ net upward force.
Magnus effect = dynamic lift on a spinning body moving through a fluid, due to pressure difference from unequal speeds on opposite sides.
In sports (cricket/tennis/baseball/golf), the curve/deviation from a simple parabola is partly explained by Bernoulli lift (Magnus effect).

Aerofoil and Lift on an Aircraft Wing

Aerofoil = shaped solid that produces upward dynamic lift when moving horizontally through air.
  • Wing is set so streamlines crowd more above than below.
  • Crowding above ⇒ higher air speed over the top surface.
  • Higher speed on top ⇒ lower pressure above than below.
  • Net upward lift balances weight in level flight.

Worked Example A fully loaded Boeing aircraft has a mass of 3.3 × 105 kg. Its total wing area is 500 m2. It is in level flight with a speed of 960 km/h. (a) Estimate the pressure difference between the lower and upper surfaces of the wings (b) Estimate the fractional increase in the speed of the air on the upper surface of the wing relative to the lower surface. [The density of air is ρ = 1.2 kg m–3]

Given:
  • Mass, m = 3.3 × 105 kg
  • Total wing area, A = 500 m2
  • Speed of aircraft (take as average air speed), vav = 960 km/h = 267 m s−1
  • Density of air, ρ = 1.2 kg m−3
  • Level flight ⇒ lift = weight
(a) Pressure difference between lower and upper wing surfaces
Lift balance: ΔP · A = mg ΔP = Plower − Pupper (pressure difference giving lift) A = total wing area m = aircraft mass g = acceleration due to gravity
Compute: ΔP = (3.3 × 105 × 9.8) / 500 = 6.5 × 103 N m−2
So only about 6.5 kPa pressure difference is needed for level flight support.
(b) Fractional increase in air speed over upper surface relative to lower surface
Assume small height difference between top/bottom wing surfaces is negligible in Bernoulli ⇒ gravity term cancels.
Bernoulli pressure–speed across wing (same height): ΔP = ½ρ (v22 − v12) v2 = air speed over upper surface v1 = air speed under lower surface
Use difference of squares: v22 − v12 = (v2 − v1)(v2 + v1) Define average speed: vav = (v2 + v1)/2 So (v2 + v1) = 2vav, hence: ΔP = ½ρ (v2 − v1)(2vav) = ρ vav (v2 − v1)
Fractional speed difference (used here): (v2 − v1)/vav = ΔP/(ρ vav2) vav = (v2 + v1)/2
Substitute: (v2 − v1)/vav = 6.5×103 / (1.2 × 2672) ≈ 0.08
Conclusion: air speed above the wing needs to be only about 8% higher than below to produce the lift.
Common mistake: mixing up vav with aircraft speed without stating the assumption. Here they explicitly take vav = 960 km/h (267 m/s) as the average air speed around the wing.

Viscosity

Viscosity = internal friction of a fluid → resists relative motion of its layers (real / non-ideal fluid effect).

Physical Picture: Shearing Between Layers (Parallel Plates)

  • Fluid (e.g. oil) trapped between two glass plates:
    • Bottom plate fixed.
    • Top plate pulled at constant speed v.
  • No-slip (as used here): fluid touching a surface moves with the same speed as the surface.
    • Top layer moves with speed v.
    • Bottom layer is at rest.
  • Middle layers: speed increases uniformly from 0 → v.
  • Each layer is dragged forward by layer above + pulled back by layer below → net shearing force between layers.
  • Replace oil with honey → need more force for same v → honey is more viscous.
Laminar flow = smooth layered flow; layers slide like pages of a book.

Viscous Flow in a Pipe (Velocity Distribution)

  • In a tube/pipe with viscous (laminar) flow:
    • Speed is maximum on the axis.
    • Speed decreases gradually towards the wall.
    • Speed becomes zero at the wall.
    • On any coaxial cylindrical surface, speed is the same (constant).

Shear Strain and Strain Rate in Fluids

  • A fluid element initially ABCD becomes AEFD after time Δt due to shear.
  • Shear strain = Δx/l (dimensionless), where:
    • Δx = relative sideways shift between layers in time Δt
    • l = separation between layers (film thickness)
  • In flowing fluids, strain keeps increasing with time → stress depends on rate of strain (strain rate), not strain itself.
  • Strain rate = (Δx/l)/Δt = Δx/(lΔt) = v/l

Coefficient of Viscosity (η): Definition, Unit, Dimensions

Coefficient of viscosity : η = (shearing stress)/(strain rate) η (“eta”) = coefficient of viscosity Shearing stress = tangential force per unit area between layers Strain rate = v/l (simple parallel-layer velocity gradient)
SI unit: poiseuille (Pl) Also written as: N s m−2 or Pa s Dimensions: [M L−1 T−1]
  • Thin liquids (water, alcohol) → low viscosity.
  • Thick liquids (coal tar, blood, glycerine) → high viscosity.
  • From the referenced data (Table 9.2 mentioned in text):
    • Blood is more viscous (“thicker”) than water.
    • Relative viscosity of blood, η/ηwater, stays constant from 0°C to 37°C.
Temperature dependence:
  • Viscosity of liquids decreases as temperature increases.
  • Viscosity of gases increases as temperature increases.

Worked Example A metal block of area 0.10 m2 is connected to a 0.010 kg mass via a string that passes over an ideal pulley (considered massless and frictionless), as in Fig. 9.13. A liquid with a film thickness of 0.30 mm is placed between the block and the table. When released the block moves to the right with a constant speed of 0.085 m s-1. Find the coefficient of viscosity of the liquid.

Setup: Metal block pulled over a table with a thin liquid film between them (film thickness given). Block is tied over an ideal pulley to a hanging mass. When released, block moves at constant speed ⇒ net force = 0 ⇒ pull = viscous resistive force. Given:
  • Area, A = 0.10 m2
  • Hanging mass, m = 0.010 kg
  • Film thickness, l = 0.30 mm = 3.0 × 10−4 m
  • Constant speed, v = 0.085 m s−1
  • Ideal pulley (massless, frictionless) ⇒ at constant speed: T = mg.
Key idea (constant speed): acceleration = 0 ⇒ forces balance.
  • Tension T = mg (hanging mass also steady-speed).
  • Viscous drag on block = T.
Shearing stress = F/A Strain rate = v/l η = (shearing stress)/(strain rate): η = (F/A) / (v/l) = (F l)/(A v) F = tangential viscous force = T = mg (here) l = film thickness A = contact area v = relative sliding speed
Substitute:
  • F = mg = 0.010 × 9.8 = 0.098 N
  • η = (0.098 × 3.0×10−4) / (0.10 × 0.085)
η ≈ 3.46 × 10−3 Pa s ( = N s m−2)
Common mistakes:
  • Not converting mm → m for film thickness.
  • Using η = (F/A)/v instead of dividing by strain rate v/l.
  • Forgetting “constant speed” ⇒ viscous force = mg.

Viscosity: Worked Example (Shear in a Fluid)

Shearing a fluid → apply tangential force → creates a velocity gradient between layers. Viscosity tells how strongly the fluid resists that relative sliding.
  • Physical reasoning in this setup: Block moves right due to string tension. In steady motion, tension equals the weight of the hanging mass ⇒ T = mg.
  • Shearing force on the fluid: Same tangential force is transmitted through the block/fluid contact ⇒ F = T = mg.
  • Numerical evaluation: with m = 0.010 kg,
Shear force from hanging mass: F = T = mg F = shear (tangential) force on fluid (N) T = tension (N) m = suspended mass (kg) g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2)
  • So F = 0.010 × 9.8 = 9.8 × 10−2 N.
  • Shear stress on the fluid: shear stress = F/A (unit: N m−2 = Pa), where A is the contact area.
  • Strain rate: velocity gradient between layers (rate of shear deformation).
  • Newton’s law of viscosity: η = (shear stress)/(strain rate). Final value in the worked solution: η = 3.46 × 10−3 Pa s.
Exam tip: Keep the chain neat: find F first (often mg via tension) → then shear stress = F/A → then strain rate (a velocity gradient) → then η = stress/strain-rate. Units must end as Pa s (= N s m−2 = kg m−1 s−1).

Viscosities of Some Fluids

Viscosity depends strongly on the type of fluid and on temperature. For many liquids, viscosity falls as temperature rises.
  • Water: 20°C → 1.0 mPl; 100°C → 0.3 mPl
  • Blood: 37°C → 2.7 mPl
  • Machine oil: 16°C → 113 mPl; 38°C → 34 mPl
  • Glycerine: 20°C → 830 mPl
  • Honey: (temperature not stated) → 200 mPl
  • Air: 0°C → 0.017 mPl; 40°C → 0.019 mPl
Common mistake: Don’t assume viscosity always decreases with temperature for every fluid; gases (e.g. air here) show a slight increase with temperature in the data.

(9.5.1) Stokes’ Law

When a body moves through a fluid, it drags nearby layers → layers slip past each other → viscosity gives a retarding (drag) force on the body.
  • Examples: raindrop falling; pendulum bob swinging (air drag due to viscosity).
  • Key observation: viscous drag ∝ velocity and acts opposite to motion.
  • Drag depends on:
    • η = viscosity of fluid
    • a = radius of (spherical) body
    • v = speed of body through fluid
  • Sir George G. Stokes (1819–1903): viscous drag on a sphere (derivation not needed here).
Stokes’ law (sphere):   F = 6πηav   F = viscous drag (N), opposite to motion η = coefficient of viscosity (Pa s) a = radius (m) v = speed relative to fluid (m s−1)
Here the retarding force is directly proportional to v (not v2).

Terminal Velocity of a Sphere in a Viscous Medium

A sphere falling in a viscous medium: starts accelerating due to gravity → speed rises → drag rises → eventually net force = 0 → acceleration becomes 0 → constant speed = terminal velocity (vt).
  • For a sphere (e.g., raindrop) falling in a fluid:
    • Downward: weight = (4π/3)a3ρg
    • Upward: buoyant force = (4π/3)a3σg, and viscous drag = 6πηav
  • At terminal velocity: viscous drag + buoyant force = weight.
Terminal condition:   6πηa vt = (4π/3)a3(ρ − σ)g vt = terminal velocity (m s−1) ρ = density of sphere (kg m−3) σ = density of fluid (kg m−3) Other symbols as earlier
Terminal velocity:   vt = 2a2(ρ − σ)g / (9η)   (Eq. 9.18) vt = terminal velocity (m s−1) a = radius (m) ρ = density of sphere (kg m−3) σ = density of fluid (kg m−3) g = acceleration due to gravity (m s−2) η = viscosity (Pa s)
  • vt ∝ a2 (depends on square of radius)
  • vt ∝ (ρ − σ) (depends on density difference)
  • vt ∝ 1/η (inversely proportional to viscosity)
Common mistake: writing (ρ + σ) instead of (ρ − σ). Buoyancy reduces effective weight → must use sphere density − fluid density.

Example The terminal velocity of a copper ball of radius 2.0 mm falling through a tank of oil at 20°C is 6.5 cm s-1. Compute the viscosity of the oil at 20°C. Density of oil is 1.5 ×103 kg m-3, density of copper is 8.9 × 103 kg m-3.

  • Given:
    • vt = 6.5 cm s−1 = 6.5 × 10−2 m s−1
    • a = 2.0 mm = 2 × 10−3 m
    • g = 9.8 m s−2
    • ρ (copper) = 8.9 × 103 kg m−3
    • σ (oil) = 1.5 × 103 kg m−3
  • Method: rearrange Eq. (9.18) to find η.
Viscosity from terminal velocity:   η = 2a2(ρ − σ)g / (9vt) η, a, ρ, σ, g, vt as defined earlier
  • Substitute values (as in text) →
  • η = 9.9 × 10−1 kg m−1 s−1 = 0.99 Pa s.
Unit check: kg m−1 s−1 = Pa s, since 1 Pa = 1 N m−2 = 1 kg m−1 s−2.

(9.6) Surface Tension : Introduction and Everyday Phenomena

Surface tension is linked to the free surface (liquid interface). Liquids: definite volume, no definite shape → when poured, they form a free surface, and this surface has extra energy.
Surface tension is about liquids only (gases don’t have free surfaces).
  • Phenomena explained by surface tension:
    • Oil and water don’t mix.
    • Water wets humans but not ducks (wetting).
    • Mercury doesn’t wet glass, but water sticks to it.
    • Oil rises up a cotton wick against gravity.
    • Sap and water rise to the top of leaves of a tree.
    • Paint brush hairs: don’t cling when dry; dip in water + take out → form a fine tip.

(9.6.1) Surface Energy

Surface energy: surface molecules have higher potential energy than bulk molecules because they have fewer neighbours pulling them in.
  • Molecular attraction holds the liquid together. A molecule deep inside is attracted by neighbours → has negative potential energy.
  • Bulk uniformity: average PE of interior molecules is same (on average) because surroundings are similar.
  • Evidence: to separate molecules far apart (evaporate/vaporise) needs large heat of evaporation; for water ≈ 40 kJ/mol.
  • Molecule near surface: only lower half-space has liquid neighbours → negative PE is smaller than bulk, roughly half.
  • Consequence: surface molecules have extra energy → liquid tries to have minimum surface area allowed by conditions.
  • Increasing surface area costs energy: energy must be supplied; many surface effects come from this.
  • Energy of a surface molecule: ~ half the energy needed to remove it completely → about half the heat of evaporation.
  • Nature of the surface: perfectly sharp surface can’t exist (molecules are moving). Density falls quickly to zero across thickness ~ a few molecular sizes (around z = 0 in schematic).
Exam phrasing: “Liquid minimises surface area” → justify: “surface molecules have higher energy; reducing surface area reduces number of surface molecules, so energy decreases.”

(9.6.2) Surface Energy and Surface Tension

Film Experiment: Relation Between Surface Energy and Surface Tension

  • Take a horizontal liquid film ending in a light bar that can slide on parallel guides (film has two surfaces).
  • Shift the bar by a small distance d → surface area increases → surface energy increases → need to do work against an internal force F.
  • Work done by applied force: W = Fd.
  • If surface energy per unit area = S, extra area made = 2dl (two surfaces; each gains area dl; bar length = l) → extra energy stored = S(2dl).
Work ↔ surface energy (film): S(2dl) = Fd   (Eq. 9.19) S = surface energy per unit area (J m−2) = surface tension (N m−1) F = force needed to move the bar (N) d = small displacement of the bar (m) l = length of movable bar in contact with film (m) 2dl = increase in total surface area (m2) since film has two surfaces
Surface tension from force on a film: S = F/(2l)   (Eq. 9.20) S = surface tension (N m−1) F = force on the movable bar (N) l = length of bar (m)
Meaning of S: Surface tension S = (i) surface energy per unit area of the interface, and also = (ii) force per unit length exerted by the fluid on the movable boundary.
Common mistake: missing the factor 2 for a film (because there are two surfaces). For a single interface, the relation won’t have the 2.

Surface Energy of an Interface (Two Materials)

Surface energy is, in general, the energy of the interface between two materials (liquid–gas, liquid–liquid, liquid–solid) → depends on both materials.
  • If molecules of the two materials attract → surface energy decreases.
  • If they repel → surface energy increases.

Key Observations About Surface Tension

  1. Definition + dual meaning: Surface tension is force per unit length (≡ surface energy per unit area) acting in the plane of the interface between a liquid and any other substance. Also: it’s the extra energy molecules at the interface have vs molecules inside the liquid.
  2. Forces on an imagined line (inside the surface): Pick a point on the interface (away from edges) and draw an imaginary line. Equal & opposite surface tension forces, magnitude S per unit length, act perpendicular to the line but in the plane of the interface → line stays in equilibrium. Microscopic view: molecules on both sides pull the line towards themselves → “tension”.
  3. At a boundary/edge of the interface: If the line is at the end of the interface, only one side pulls → unbalanced force S per unit length acting inwards → tries to reduce surface area.

Surface Tension Values and Temperature Dependence

Surface tension depends on temperature. Like viscosity, surface tension of a liquid generally decreases as temperature increases.
  • Helium: −270°C → surface tension = 0.000239 N/m; heat of vaporisation = 0.115 kJ/mol
  • Oxygen: −183°C → surface tension = 0.0132 N/m; heat of vaporisation = 7.1 kJ/mol
  • Ethanol: 20°C → surface tension = 0.0227 N/m; heat of vaporisation = 40.6 kJ/mol
  • Water: 20°C → surface tension = 0.0727 N/m; heat of vaporisation = 44.16 kJ/mol
  • Mercury: 20°C → surface tension = 0.4355 N/m; heat of vaporisation = 63.2 kJ/mol
Exam linkage: Higher surface tension usually means stronger intermolecular cohesion; heats of vaporisation help link surface effects to energy needed to separate molecules.

Measurement of Surface Tension (Glass Plate Method)

When does a fluid “stick” to a solid? Adhesion happens when surface energy between fluid–solid is less than the sum of surface energies for solid–air and fluid–air. Then net attraction exists between the solid and the liquid.
Experimental idea (schematic): Suspend a flat vertical glass plate as one arm of a balance. Raise a vessel of liquid until it just touches the plate’s lower horizontal edge. Surface tension pulls the plate slightly downward. Add extra weights to the other side until the plate just clears the liquid surface again. Let the additional weight needed be W. This extra downward pull is from surface tension along the contact line.
Surface tension (liquid–air) by plate method: Sla = W/(2l) = mg/(2l)    Sla = surface tension of liquid–air interface W = extra weight needed to rebalance (force) m = extra mass added (so W = mg) g = acceleration due to gravity l = length of the horizontal edge of plate in contact with liquid 2l because surface tension acts along both sides of the plate edge (two liquid–air contact lines)
Common error: writing W/l instead of W/(2l). Factor 2 is needed because there are two contact lines (front + back).

9.6.3 Angle of Contact

Definition and Physical Meaning

Angle of contact (θ): angle between the tangent to the liquid surface at the contact point and the solid surface, measured inside the liquid.
Key point: Near the line where a liquid meets another medium (solid/air etc.), the surface is usually curved. θ depends on the specific liquid–solid pair.

Wetting vs Droplet Formation

Value of θ decides if a liquid:
  • Wets/spreads on the solid (small θ), or
  • Forms droplets (large θ).
Examples:
  • Water on a lotus leaf → droplets (poor wetting).
  • Water on a clean plastic plate → spreads more (better wetting).

Interfacial Tensions and Equilibrium at the Contact Line

At the contact line, three interfaces meet → three interfacial tensions:
  • Sla: liquid–air
  • Ssa: solid–air
  • Ssl: solid–liquid
These surface forces must be in equilibrium at the contact line.
Interfacial tension balance (equilibrium): Sla cosθ + Ssl = Ssa    (Eq. 9.22) Sla = liquid–air surface tension Ssl = solid–liquid interfacial tension Ssa = solid–air interfacial tension θ = angle of contact (measured inside the liquid)

Acute vs Obtuse Contact Angle: Conditions and Interpretation

Obtuse (θ > 90°): if Ssl > Sla (e.g., water–leaf). Liquid molecules attract each other strongly, weak attraction to solid → making solid–liquid surface costs more energy → liquid doesn’t wet (droplets). Acute (θ < 90°): if Ssl < Sla (e.g., water–plastic). Strong liquid–solid attraction lowers Ssl → cosθ increases → θ decreases → better wetting/spreading.
Non-wetting examples: water on waxy/oily surfaces; mercury on almost any surface. Strong wetting examples: water on glass or plastic; kerosene oil on virtually anything (spreads easily).

Applications: Wetting and Water Proofing Agents

  • Wetting agents (soaps, detergents, dyeing substances): make θ smaller (decrease θ) → liquid penetrates/acts better.
  • Waterproofing agents: make θ larger between water and fibres (increase θ) → less wetting.
Exam tip: Always mention θ is measured inside the liquid—many sign/confusion errors come from taking the wrong side.

9.6.4 Drops and Bubbles

Why Drops/Bubbles Are Spherical

A liquid–air interface has surface energy. For fixed volume, the shape with minimum surface energy has the least surface area. A sphere has minimum area for a given volume → so (when gravity is negligible) free drops/bubbles are spherical.
Sphericity is clearest for small drops (spray/jet) and soap bubbles, where gravity is weak compared to surface tension.

Excess Pressure Inside a Spherical Drop

Take a spherical drop (radius r) in equilibrium. Let radius increase by a small Δr.
Increase in surface energy (drop): [4π(r + Δr)2 − 4πr2] Sla = 8πrΔr Sla    (Eq. 9.23) r = initial radius Δr = small increase in radius Sla = liquid–air surface tension
This surface-energy increase is balanced by work gained in expansion against the pressure difference (inside vs outside).
Work done by pressure difference: W = (Pi − Po) 4πr2Δr    (Eq. 9.24) Pi = pressure inside the drop Po = pressure outside the drop 4πr2Δr = approx. volume increase (small Δr)
Equilibrium: (surface-energy increase) = (work by pressure difference) ⇒
Excess pressure inside a liquid drop: Pi − Po = 2Sla/r    (Eq. 9.25) Sla = liquid–air surface tension r = radius of drop
General rule (liquid–gas curved interface): pressure is higher on the concave side than on the convex side. Example: an air bubble in a liquid has higher pressure inside.

Excess Pressure Inside a Soap Bubble (Two Interfaces)

A bubble has two interfaces (inner + outer surfaces), so the pressure difference is double compared to a single interface.
Excess pressure inside a soap bubble: Pi − Po = 4Sla/r    (Eq. 9.26) Pi = pressure inside the bubble Po = pressure outside the bubble Sla = surface tension of the liquid film (liquid–air interface) r = radius of the bubble
Exam trap: 2S/r for a drop (one surface) vs 4S/r for a soap bubble (two surfaces). Mixing these is very common.
Practical implication: Need extra air pressure inside → that’s why you must blow hard (but not too hard) to form a soap bubble.

9.6.5 Capillary Rise

Origin of Capillary Rise

Capillary rise happens due to pressure difference across a curved liquid–air interface. In narrow tubes, surface tension can lift water against gravity.
Meaning of “capillary”: from Latin capilla = hair; hair-thin tubes give very large rise.

Capillary Tube Setup (Beginning of Derivation)

Consider a vertical capillary tube of circular cross-section, radius a, dipped into an open vessel of water. Inside the tube the surface forms a meniscus, and the contact angle between water and the tube matters (derivation continues beyond this portion).

Capillarity: Pressure Difference at a Curved Meniscus and Capillary Rise/Fall

Curved liquid surface (meniscus) at a liquid–gas interface ⇒ pressure difference across it due to surface tension. For water in a glass capillary, meniscus is usually concave (acute contact angle), so liquid pressure just under the meniscus is less than atmospheric.

Pressure jump across the meniscus (capillary tube)

Pressure difference across top meniscus (water–air in a capillary): (Pi − P0) = (2S/r) = 2S/(a secθ) = (2S/a)cosθ    (Eq. 9.27) Pi = pressure of liquid just inside the tube at the meniscus (air–water interface) P0 = atmospheric pressure (outside, above the meniscus) S = surface tension of the liquid–gas interface r = radius of curvature of the meniscus a = radius of the capillary tube θ = angle of contact (measured inside the liquid at the solid boundary)
For a concave meniscus (water in glass): θ acute ⇒ cosθ > 0 ⇒ (Pi − P0) > 0 (per Eq. 9.27 sign convention). So the pressure just under the meniscus differs from atmospheric by an amount set by surface tension + curvature. Text point to remember (water in glass): pressure inside the tube at the meniscus is less than atmospheric; hydrostatic balance then explains the rise of the column.

Hydrostatic balance giving capillary rise

Same-level points in connected liquid have equal pressure (A and B at same horizontal level): P0 + hρg = Pi = PA    (Eq. 9.28) h = capillary rise (height of liquid column above outside free surface) ρ = density of the liquid g = acceleration due to gravity PA = pressure at point A (same level as B)
Capillary rise from surface tension (combine Eqs. 9.27 & 9.28): hρg = (Pi − P0) = (2S cosθ)/a    (Eq. 9.29) h = capillary rise (positive for rise, negative for depression) S = surface tension θ = angle of contact a = capillary radius ρ = liquid density g = gravitational acceleration
  • Capillary rise is due to surface tension (explicitly stated).
  • From Eq. 9.29: h ∝ 1/a ⇒ smaller capillary radius → bigger rise.
  • Typical rise ≈ a few cm for fine capillaries.

Numerical illustration: water in a fine capillary

Water on clean glass: θ small (acute) ⇒ often take cosθ ≈ 1 for estimates ⇒ h ≈ 2S/(ρga).
Given a = 0.05 cm = 5×10−4 m, S = 0.073 N m−1, ρ = 103 kg m−3, g = 9.8 m s−2:
h = 2S/(ρga) h = 2×(0.073) / [(103)(9.8)(5×10−4)] h = 2.98×10−2 m = 2.98 cm

Capillary depression (mercury case)

Convex meniscus (e.g. mercury in glass): θ obtuse ⇒ cosθ < 0. From Eq. 9.29, h becomes negative ⇒ level in capillary is lower than outside (capillary depression).
Exam pitfall: always check sign of cosθ. Acute θ (wetting) → rise; obtuse θ (non-wetting) → depression.

Example

The lower end of a capillary tube of diameter 2.00 mm is dipped 8.00 cm below the surface of water in a beaker. What is the pressure required in the tube in order to blow a hemispherical bubble at its end in water? The surface tension of water at temperature of the experiments is 7.30 × 10-2 Nm-1. 1 atmospheric pressure = 1.01 × 105 Pa, density of water = 1000 kg/m3, g = 9.80 m s-2. Also calculate the excess pressure.
Excess pressure across a curved interface comes from surface tension. Gas bubble in a liquid: one liquid–gas surface ⇒ excess pressure = 2S/r. Liquid drop/bubble in a gas: two liquid surfaces ⇒ excess pressure = 4S/r.
Given: capillary diameter = 2.00 mm ⇒ radius a = 1.00 mm = 10−3 m. Depth below water surface = 8.00 cm = 0.08 m. Surface tension S = 7.30×10−2 N m−1. Atmospheric pressure = 1.01×105 Pa. ρ = 1000 kg m−3. g = 9.80 m s−2.

Step 1: Pressure outside the bubble at the capillary tip

Po = Patm + ρgh Po = 1.01×105 + (0.08)(1000)(9.80) = 1.01784×105 Pa Po = pressure in water just outside the bubble at the given depth Patm = atmospheric pressure ρgh = hydrostatic pressure due to depth h in the liquid

Step 2: Excess pressure due to surface tension

Excess pressure for a gas bubble in a liquid: ΔP = 2S/r ΔP = 2×(7.30×10−2) / (10−3) = 146 Pa ΔP = Pi − Po r = radius of the bubble
Bubble is hemispherical at the capillary end ⇒ take bubble radius = capillary radius: r = 1.00 mm.

Step 3: Pressure required inside the tube (inside the bubble)

Pi = Po + 2S/r Pi = 1.01784×105 + 146 = (1.01784 + 0.00146)×105 Pa Pi ≈ 1.02×105 Pa (rounded to three significant figures)
Common mistake: using 4S/r here. That’s for a liquid drop/bubble in air (two surfaces), not a gas bubble in a liquid (one surface).
