Ch07: Gravitation

7.1 Introduction

Gravitation (everyday idea): Objects naturally get pulled towards Earth.
  • Throw something up → it falls back down
  • Walking/moving uphill feels harder than downhill
  • Raindrops fall from clouds to Earth
Galileo (1564–1642): Noted that all bodies (whatever their mass) fall with the same constant acceleration (ideal conditions).
  • Often said to have shown this publicly
  • Definitely did experiments with bodies rolling down inclined planes
  • Got a value of acceleration due to gravity close to later accurate values
Astronomical observations: Since ancient times people watched stars + planets.
  • Stars: positions look unchanged year after year (fixed background)
  • Planets: show regular motion against the star background (so key for motion models)
Ptolemy’s model (≈ 2000 years ago): Geocentric idea → stars, Sun, and planets all revolve around Earth. Back then, the only “allowed” celestial motion was thought to be circular motion.
  • To fit observations: used complex circles-on-circles
  • Planets move in circles whose centres move in larger circles (epicycle-type description)
Indian contributions: Similar theories appeared ~400 years later. A cleaner idea—Sun at the centre, planets revolve around it = heliocentric model—was already mentioned by Aryabhata (5th century A.D.) in his treatise.
Copernicus (1473–1543): Proposed a clear heliocentric model: planets move in circles around a fixed central Sun.
  • Church discredited the theory
  • Galileo supported it and was prosecuted by the state for his beliefs
Tycho Brahe (1546–1601): Danish nobleman; recorded planetary observations (naked-eye) throughout his life. Johannes Kepler (1571–1640): Used Brahe’s data to get three elegant laws = Kepler’s laws.
  • Newton knew these laws
  • They helped enable the big step towards the universal law of gravitation

7.2 Kepler’s laws

Kepler’s First Law: Law of Orbits

Statement: Planets move in elliptical orbits, with the Sun at one focus. Significance: Breaks from Copernicus’ circular-only idea. (Circle = special case of ellipse.)
Ellipse (string method picture):
  • Pick 2 fixed points F1, F2.
  • Tie a string with ends pinned at F1 and F2.
  • Pull string taut with a pencil tip; move pencil while keeping it taut → closed curve is an ellipse.
  • Any point T on ellipse satisfies: TF1 + TF2 = constant.
  • F1, F2 are the foci (spelt “focii” in the text).
Orbit-diagram terms (Fig. 7.1a idea):
  • Perihelion (P): closest point to the Sun.
  • Aphelion (A): farthest point from the Sun.
  • Join F1F2 and extend to cut ellipse at P and A.
  • Centre (O): midpoint of PA.
  • Semi-major axis: PO = AO = ½AP.
  • Circle special case: foci merge into one; semi-major axis becomes the radius.

Kepler’s Second Law: Law of Areas

Statement: The Sun–planet line sweeps equal areas in equal times.
Physical meaning (from observation): Planets move slower when farther from the Sun and faster when nearer. The equal-area rule captures this changing speed along the orbit.

Kepler’s Third Law: Law of Periods

Statement: T2 is proportional to a3, where a is the semi-major axis of the ellipse.
Kepler’s law of periods (proportional): T2 ∝ a3 T = time period of revolution a = semi-major axis of orbit
Check using planet data (Table 7.1 idea): For 8 planets, Q = T2/a3 comes out ~ constant → supports Kepler’s 3rd law.
  • a in units of 1010 m
  • T in years (y)
  • Q in units of 10−34 y2 m−3
Values (a, T, Q): Mercury (5.79, 0.24, 2.95); Venus (10.8, 0.615, 3.00); Earth (15.0, 1, 2.96); Mars (22.8, 1.88, 2.98); Jupiter (77.8, 11.9, 3.01); Saturn (143, 29.5, 2.98); Uranus (287, 84, 2.98); Neptune (450, 165, 2.99). Near-constant Q ≈ 3 ⇒ T2 ∝ a3.
Exam tip: In Kepler’s 3rd law (planets about the Sun), a = semi-major axis (NOT perihelion/aphelion distance). Only for a circle does a = radius.

Law of Areas as a consequence of conservation of angular momentum

Central force: force on the planet acts along the Sun–planet line (along radius vector r). Gravity is a central force ⇒ angular momentum is conserved.
Set-up: Put Sun at origin. Planet has:
  • position vector r
  • momentum p
  • velocity v
  • mass m
Area swept in small time Δt: ΔA = ½ (r × v Δt)   (Eq. 7.1) ΔA = area swept by radius vector in time Δt r = position vector (Sun → planet) v = planet velocity × = cross product (magnitude = r v sinθ) Δt = small time interval
Areal velocity: ΔA/Δt = ½ (r × p)/m = L/(2m)   (Eq. 7.2) p = linear momentum (= m v) L = angular momentum about Sun (= r × p) m = mass of planet
Key reasoning: Central force acts along r ⇒ torque about origin = 0 ⇒ L constant. Then from ΔA/Δt = L/(2m), areal velocity is constant ⇒ equal areas in equal times (Kepler’s 2nd law).
Common mistake: Writing “equal distances in equal times.” Kepler’s 2nd law is equal areas, not equal arc lengths.

Example 7.1 (Using angular momentum and the law of areas)

Problem set-up: Speed at perihelion P = vP, Sun–planet distance SP = rP. Relate {rP, vP} to aphelion {rA, vA}. Also decide if time to traverse arcs BAC and CPB are equal (figure context).
Step 1: Angular momentum at perihelion & aphelion
  • At perihelion P: LP = mp rP vP, since (by inspection) rP ⟂ vP at closest/farthest points.
  • At aphelion A: LA = mp rA vA.
Step 2: Use angular momentum conservation (central force) mp rP vP = mp rA vA ⇒ vP/vA = rA/rP. Since rA > rP ⇒ vP > vA (faster near perihelion).
Step 3: Time for arcs BAC vs CPB (use Kepler’s 2nd law)
  • Area SBAC (aphelion side) is larger than area SBPC (perihelion side), per diagram context.
  • Kepler’s 2nd law: equal areas ↔ equal times ⇒ larger area needs more time.
Conclusion: Time for BAC is longer than for CPB.
Exam tip: At perihelion/aphelion, use L = m r v only after stating r ⟂ v there; otherwise stick to L = r × p.

7.3 Universal Law of Gravitation

Newton’s motivation (legend + goal): Apple story = spark for a universal gravity law linking terrestrial gravitation + Kepler’s laws.
Newton’s reasoning with the Moon: Moon moves in an (approx.) circular orbit of radius Rm → needs centripetal acceleration, supplied by Earth’s gravity.
Centripetal acceleration of the Moon: am = Vm2/Rm = 4π2Rm/T2   (Eq. 7.3) am = Moon’s centripetal acceleration due to Earth’s gravity Vm = Moon’s orbital speed Rm = Moon’s orbital radius T = time period of Moon’s revolution π = 3.14159...
Speed–period relation (circular orbit): Vm = 2πRm/T Vm = orbital speed of the Moon Rm = orbital radius T = orbital time period
Numerical facts used by Newton (as stated):
  • Moon’s time period: T ≈ 27.3 days
  • Moon’s orbital radius: Rm ≈ 3.84 × 108 m
Putting these into Eq. (7.3) gives am that’s much smaller than g at Earth’s surface, even though both are due to Earth’s pull. (Sets up: gravity weakens with distance.)

Inverse-square dependence and origin of the Universal Law

Earth’s gravitational effect decreases with distance from Earth’s centre. If it follows an inverse-square dependence with distance R, then gravitational acceleration also obeys: g ∝ 1/R2.
Inverse-square scaling (Earth): g ∝ 1/R2 g = acceleration due to gravity at distance R from Earth’s centre R = distance from Earth’s centre
This inverse-square idea explains: (i) why gravity drops with height, and (ii) how Newton linked the Moon’s orbital acceleration to surface gravity (matches g ≈ 9.8 m s−2), pushing him toward the Universal Law of Gravitation.

Newton’s Universal Law of Gravitation

Statement (Newton): Every body attracts every other body with a force ∝ product of their masses, and ∝ 1/(distance)2. Quoted from Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy (Principia).

Scalar form (magnitude)

Newton’s law (magnitude): F = G m1 m2 / r2   (Eq. 7.5) F = magnitude of gravitational force between two point masses G = universal gravitational constant m1, m2 = interacting point masses r = distance between the two masses

Vector form and direction (attractive nature)

Newton’s law (vector form): F = − (G m1 m2 / r2) r̂ Equivalently: F = − G m1 m2 r̂ / r3 F = force on m2 due to m1 r = r2 − r1 (displacement from m1 to m2) r = |r| (separation distance) r̂ = unit vector from m1 to m2
Gravity is attractive: the “−” sign means F points along −r̂, i.e. from m2 towards m1 (towards the attracting mass).

Action–reaction pair (Newton’s third law)

Mutual forces: $\overrightarrow{\mathrm{F}}_{12}$ = − $\overrightarrow{\mathrm{F}}_{21}$ $\overrightarrow{\mathrm{F}}_{12}$ = force on body 1 due to body 2 $\overrightarrow{\mathrm{F}}_{21}$ = force on body 2 due to body 1

Applying the law to many particles and extended bodies

Eq. (7.5) is for point masses. Real objects are extended, so different parts pull in different directions. Net force = add all tiny contributions vectorially (often needs calculus).

Principle of superposition (vector sum)

Superposition: Total gravitational force on a mass from several masses = vector sum of the forces from each mass acting alone.
Net force on m1 due to m2, m3, m4: F1 = F21 + F31 + F41 with Fk1 = G mk m1 / rk12 (along the line joining them, towards mk) rk1 = distance between masses k and 1

Two special results for a hollow spherical shell (uniform density)

(1) Outside a hollow spherical shell: Attraction between a uniform hollow spherical shell and a point mass outside it is the same as if all the shell’s mass were concentrated at the centre. Qualitative reason: Perpendicular components cancel; only components along the centre–mass line add → net force along the radius line.
(2) Inside a hollow spherical shell: Force due to a uniform hollow spherical shell on a point mass inside the shell is zero. Qualitative reason: Pulls from different regions act in different directions and cancel fully.
These shell results let us treat a spherically symmetric body (e.g. Earth) as if its mass is at the centre for points outside. For points inside, only mass at smaller radius contributes (outer shells give zero).

Worked Example 7.2: Three equal masses at vertices of an equilateral triangle

Problem setup

3 equal point masses (each = m) fixed at vertices A, B, C of an equilateral triangle. A mass 2m is placed at the centroid G. Given (geometry): AG = BG = CG = 1 m.

(a) Net gravitational force on mass 2m at the centroid

Use superposition + resolve into x- and y-components. From the geometry:
  • Angle between GC and +x-axis = 30°
  • Angle between GB and −x-axis = 30°
Force on (2m) due to mass at A: FGA = (G m(2m) / GA2) ĵ GA = distance from G to A ĵ = unit vector along +y (chosen along GA in the diagram’s axes)
Force on (2m) due to mass at B: FGB = (G m(2m) / GB2) (− î cos30° − ĵ sin30°) GB = distance from G to B î = unit vector along +x
Force on (2m) due to mass at C: FGC = (G m(2m) / GC2) (+ î cos30° − ĵ sin30°) GC = distance from G to C
Resultant (superposition): FR = FGA + FGB + FGC
Put GA = GB = GC = 1 m. Then x-components cancel:
  • From B: −(2Gm2)cos30° in x
  • From C: +(2Gm2)cos30° in x
y-components also cancel because:
  • Up from A: +(2Gm2) in y
  • Down from B and C: −(2Gm2)sin30° − (2Gm2)sin30° = −(2Gm2) in y
Final answer (a): FR = 0
You can spot FR = 0 by symmetry too: 3 equal forces separated by 120° (centroid of equilateral triangle) ⇒ resultant = 0.

(b) If the mass at vertex A is doubled

Doubling mass at A breaks symmetry, so the neat cancellation is gone. Redo by superposition: keep B and C forces the same, but replace m at A by 2m (so force due to A doubles). The resultant points along the unbalanced direction (along GA in the chosen axes).
Exam approach: write FR = FGA(with 2m at A) + FGB + FGC, cancel x first (B and C still cancel in x), then add y-components.

The gravitational constant (G) and Cavendish experiment

Historical determination

G in Newton’s law is measurable experimentally. First measured by Henry Cavendish (1798) using a torsion balance.

Cavendish torsion balance: idea and torques

A light bar AB with 2 small lead spheres at the ends is hung by a fine wire. 2 large lead spheres are placed near the small ones on opposite sides. Each large sphere attracts its nearby small sphere → equal and opposite forces on the bar → no net force but a net torque (wire twists). Move the large spheres to the other side → torque reverses → bar rotates through a small measurable angle.
The wire gives a restoring torque proportional to twist angle θ: restoring torque = τ θ, where τ is the torsion constant (restoring couple per unit angle). τ can be found independently by applying a known torque and measuring θ.
Force between one big and neighbouring small sphere: F = G M m / d2 (Eq. 7.6) F = gravitational attraction (pair) M = mass of big sphere m = mass of small sphere d = centre-to-centre separation G = gravitational constant
Equilibrium of torques: (F)(L) = τ θ ⇒ G M m L / d2 = τ θ (Eq. 7.7) L = length of bar AB (lever arm as stated: torque from F is F × L) τ = restoring couple per unit twist angle θ = equilibrium twist angle
Measure θ; with M, m, d, L known and τ known independently → compute G from Eq. (7.7).
Accepted value: G = 6.67 × 10−11 N m2 kg−2 (Eq. 7.8) G = universal gravitational constant

Acceleration due to gravity of the Earth

Earth treated as concentric spherical shells

Model Earth as many concentric spherical shells (tiny at centre → biggest at surface).
  • Point outside Earth: outside every shell ⇒ each shell acts like its mass is at the centre ⇒ same as all Earth’s mass at the centre.
  • Point inside Earth: shells with radius bigger than the point’s distance give zero net force (shell theorem); only enclosed mass matters.

Force at a point inside Earth at distance r from centre

Take a point mass m at P, distance r from Earth’s centre:
  • Shells with radius > r: P is inside them ⇒ net gravitational force on m = 0.
  • Shells with radius ≤ r: together make a solid sphere of radius r, mass Mr ⇒ acts like Mr is at the centre.
Magnitude of force at radius r (inside): F = G m Mr / r2 (Eq. 7.9) F = gravitational force on m at distance r from Earth’s centre Mr = mass enclosed within radius r m = mass placed at radius r r = distance of m from Earth’s centre

Uniform-density assumption (expressing masses)

Assume Earth has uniform density ρ.
Mass of Earth: ME = (4/3) π RE3 ρ ME = mass of Earth RE = radius of Earth ρ = (uniform) density of Earth
Mass enclosed within radius r: Mr = (4/3) π r3 ρ Mr = mass of sphere of radius r (enclosed mass) r = distance from Earth’s centre ρ = uniform density

Gravitational force on a mass at Earth and the meaning of “Cavendish weighed the Earth”

Gravitational force between Earth and a point mass m at distance r from Earth’s centre (Eq. 7.10) F = (G m ME) / r2 F = magnitude of gravitational force on m (points towards Earth’s centre) G = universal gravitational constant m = mass of the particle/body ME = mass of Earth r = distance of m from Earth’s centre
Gravitational force on Earth’s surface (r = RE) (Eq. 7.11) F = (G ME m) / RE2 RE = radius of Earth
Acceleration due to gravity (g): acceleration caused by Earth’s gravitational force on a body near Earth. Newton’s 2nd law: F = mg.
g on Earth’s surface (Eq. 7.12) g = F/m = (G ME) / RE2 g = acceleration due to gravity at Earth’s surface F = gravitational force on mass m at the surface
Why “Cavendish weighed the Earth”:
  • g can be measured, and RE is known.
  • If G is measured (Cavendish experiment), then from Eq. (7.12):
ME = g RE2 / G So measuring G lets us estimate Earth’s mass.
Don’t mix up g (varies with location; here = Earth’s surface unless stated) with G (universal constant).

7.6 Acceleration due to gravity below and above the surface of Earth

g at a height h above Earth’s surface

At height h: distance from Earth’s centre is r = RE + h So the inverse-square law uses r = RE + h.
Force at height h (Eq. 7.13) F(h) = (G ME m) / (RE + h)2 F(h) = magnitude of force on mass m at height h h = height above Earth’s surface
Acceleration due to gravity at height h (Eq. 7.14) g(h) ≡ F(h)/m = (G ME) / (RE + h)2 g(h) = acceleration due to gravity at height h
Because RE + h > RE ⇒ g(h) < g (surface value).
Small-height approximation: if h ≪ RE, use binomial expansion.
g at small height (binomial approximation) (Eq. 7.15) g(h) ≅ g (1 − 2h/RE) g = (G ME) / RE2 = surface value Valid when h/RE ≪ 1
Eq. (7.15): for small heights, g drops by the factor (1 − 2h/RE).
Binomial used: (1 + x)−2 ≈ 1 − 2x for |x| ≪ 1, with x = h/RE.

g at a depth d below Earth’s surface

At depth d: distance from Earth’s centre is r = RE − d Model Earth as:
  • a smaller solid sphere of radius (RE − d), plus
  • an outer spherical shell of thickness d.
Outer shell’s force on an interior point = zero (a spherical shell gives no net gravitational force inside it). The smaller sphere attracts like all its mass is concentrated at the centre.
Mass of the smaller sphere (uniform density) (Eq. 7.16) Ms/ME = (RE − d)3 / RE3 Ms = mass of inner sphere of radius (RE − d) Uses: mass of sphere ∝ (radius)3
Force at depth d (Eq. 7.17) F(d) = (G Ms m) / (RE − d)2 F(d) = magnitude of gravitational force on m at depth d
Force at depth d after substituting Ms (Eq. 7.18) F(d) = G ME m (RE − d) / RE3
Acceleration due to gravity at depth d (Eq. 7.19) g(d) = F(d)/m = (G ME (RE − d)) / RE3 = g (1 − d/RE) g = (G ME) / RE2 = surface value
Going below the surface (uniform-density model): g decreases linearly with depth by factor (1 − d/RE). Remarkable conclusion: g is maximum at Earth’s surface — it decreases if you go up (height) or down (depth).
“Outer shell gives zero force” applies only for points inside a spherical shell, not outside.

7.7 Gravitational potential energy

Potential energy, work, and conservative nature of gravity

Potential energy = energy stored due to position. If position changes under a force, the change in PE matches the work done by that force . Conservative force = work done is path independent. Gravity is conservative. PE due to gravity = gravitational potential energy.

Near Earth’s surface: constant g approximation

For heights ≪ RE, gravitational force is ~constant: F ≈ mg, directed toward Earth’s centre (downward).
Work done lifting m from h1 to h2 (near Earth) (Eq. 7.20) W12 = Force · displacement = mg (h2 − h1) W12 = work done moving from position 1 (h1) to position 2 (h2) vertically upward g = (approximately constant) acceleration due to gravity near surface
Potential energy function near Earth (Eq. 7.21) W(h) = mgh + W0 W(h) = gravitational potential energy at height h W0 = constant (reference level choice)
Potential energy difference = work done (Eq. 7.22) W12 = W(h2) − W(h1)
W0 cancels in differences → only ΔPE matters physically. If you set h = 0, then W(h=0) = W0 (i.e. PE “at the surface” in that reference choice).
⚠ mgh works only when g is effectively constant (heights ≪ Earth’s radius). For large distances use the inverse-square result below.

At arbitrary distance r from Earth’s centre (r > RE)

Gravitational force magnitude outside Earth (Eq. 7.23) F = (G ME m) / r2 r = distance of the particle from Earth’s centre
Work done lifting m from r = r1 to r = r2 (r2 > r1) (Eq. 7.24) W12 = ∫ F · dr = −G ME m (1/r2 − 1/r1) Equivalently: W12 = G ME m (1/r1 − 1/r2) Negative sign: gravity acts inward, while lifting displacement is outward.
Potential energy at distance r (outside Earth) (Eq. 7.25) W(r) = −(G ME m)/r + W1    (valid for r > RE) W1 = constant (chosen reference; equals PE at infinity if r → ∞)
From Eq. (7.25): r → ∞ ⇒ W(∞) = W1. Convention: choose W(∞) = 0 ⇒ W1 = 0, so W(r) = −G ME m / r (for r > RE). Meaning: PE at a point = work needed to bring the mass from infinity to that point (with W(∞)=0). Only PE differences are physically definite (see Eq. 7.22, 7.24). Absolute zero is just a convention.

Gravitational potential and two-particle gravitational potential energy

Gravitational potential at a point (due to Earth) = potential energy per unit mass at that point.
Potential energy of two masses m1, m2 separated by r (with V = 0 as r → ∞) V = −(G m1 m2)/r V = gravitational potential energy of the pair r = separation between the two masses
Superposition (isolated system): total PE = sum of pairwise PEs over all possible pairs in the system.

Example 7.3: four equal masses at the corners of a square

System: 4 particles, each mass m, at corners of a square of side l.
  • 4 side pairs, separation l
  • 2 diagonal pairs, separation √2 l
Total PE = sum over all distinct pairs using V = −Gm1m2/r.
Total potential energy of the four-mass system U = 4 [−G m2/l] + 2 [−G m2/(√2 l)] U = −(G m2/l) (4 + √2) Numerically: (4 + √2) ≈ 5.41, so U ≈ −5.41 (G m2/l)
Potential at the centre of the square Centre → vertex distance: r = l/√2 Potential (per unit mass) at centre due to 4 masses: U(r) = −4 G m / (l/√2) = −(4√2 G m)/l U(r) = gravitational potential at the centre (PE of a unit mass placed there)
⚠ Don’t mix up:
  • Potential energy of the whole system (depends on pair separations, scales like m2)
  • Potential at a point (per unit mass, scales like m)

7.8 Escape speed

Escape question: can you launch an object fast enough that it never returns (i.e. reaches infinity)? Use conservation of mechanical energy: K + U.
Let the object reach infinity with speed Vf. Let W1 be the gravitational PE at infinity (often later set to 0 by convention, but kept as W1 for now).
Total energy at infinity (Eq. 7.26) E = W1 + (1/2) m Vf2 E = total mechanical energy (conserved) Vf = speed at infinity
Total energy at launch from distance (RE + h) from Earth’s centre (Eq. 7.27) E = (1/2) m Vi2 − (G m ME)/(RE + h) + W1 Vi = initial (launch) speed h = height above Earth’s surface RE = Earth’s radius
Escape speed comes from energy conservation between launch point and infinity. Minimum escape condition usually uses Vf = 0 at infinity (just reaches infinity).

Escape Speed (Escape Velocity)

To reach infinity (escape the gravitational field), total mechanical energy must be ≥ 0 (take GPE = 0 at infinity). By conservation of mechanical energy, the launch energy must be enough so that at infinity the object can have at least zero speed and zero PE.

Energy condition for reaching infinity

Energy equality (conservation): Eqs. (7.26) and (7.27) ⇒ mVi2/2 − GmME/(RE + h) = mVf2/2 Symbols: m = mass of object; Vi = initial speed; Vf = speed at infinity; G = universal gravitational constant; ME = mass of Earth; RE = radius of Earth; h = height above Earth’s surface at launch.
RHS = mVf2/2 is always ≥ 0 (min = 0). So the LHS must be ≥ 0 if escape is to happen.
Condition to reach infinity: mVi2/2 − GmME/(RE + h) ≥ 0 Symbols: same as above.

Minimum (escape) speed at height h

Minimum escape case = when inequality becomes equality (object just reaches infinity with Vf = 0).
Minimum speed to escape from height h: mVi,min2/2 = GmME/(RE + h) Symbols: Vi,min = minimum initial speed for escape; other symbols as above.

Escape speed from Earth’s surface (h = 0)

Escape speed from surface: Vesc = Vi,min = √(2GME/RE) Symbols: Vesc = escape speed; G, ME, RE as defined.
Using g = GME/RE2: Vesc = √(2gRE) Symbols: g = acceleration due to gravity at Earth’s surface; RE = Earth radius.
For Earth: Vesc ≈ 11.2 km s−1. This is the escape speed (often loosely called “escape velocity”).

Escape speed for the Moon and implication for atmosphere

Formula Vesc = √(2gR) works for any spherical body: use that body’s surface g and radius R. Moon: both g and R are smaller than Earth’s ⇒ escape speed ≈ 2.3 km s−1 (≈ 5× smaller than Earth). So the Moon has no atmosphere: gas molecules from the surface with speeds above this can escape the Moon’s gravity.
Exam tip: “Escape speed” depends on GM/R (or gR), not on the escaping mass m (it cancels).

Example 7.4: Projectile between two fixed spheres (neutral point method)

If two gravitational pulls act in opposite directions along a line, there’s a neutral point where net gravitational force is zero. If the projectile just reaches this point with zero speed, then beyond it the stronger pull drags it to the other mass.

Problem setup

Two uniform solid spheres, equal radii R, masses M and 4M, fixed with centre separation 6R. A projectile (mass m) is fired from the surface of the M sphere straight toward the centre of the 4M sphere. Find the minimum speed v so it just reaches the surface of the second sphere.

Step 1: Locate the neutral point N

Let ON = r (distance from centre O of the M-sphere to N). Then distance from N to the centre of the 4M-sphere is (6R − r).
Neutral point condition (forces cancel): GMm/r2 = G(4M)m/(6R − r)2 Symbols: r = ON; 6R − r = distance to the other centre; G = gravitational constant; M, 4M = sphere masses; m = projectile mass.
Cancel G, M, m: (6R − r)2 = 4r2 6R − r = ±2r ⇒ r = 2R or r = −6R
r = −6R doesn’t apply (that point is on the far side). So the neutral point is between the spheres at r = 2R from the lighter sphere’s centre.

Step 2: Use energy conservation between launch point and N

Minimum speed means it just reaches N with speed → 0. Work out total mechanical energy at:
  • Initial point: on surface of M-sphere ⇒ distance to M-centre = R; distance to 4M-centre = 6R − R = 5R.
  • Neutral point N: distance to M-centre = r = 2R; distance to 4M-centre = 6R − 2R = 4R.
Initial mechanical energy Ei: KE = (1/2)mv2 PE due to M: −GMm/R PE due to 4M at 5R: −G(4M)m/(5R) Energy at N, EN: speed ≈ 0 ⇒ only potential energy PE due to M at 2R: −GMm/(2R) PE due to 4M at 4R: −G(4M)m/(4R)
Net force is zero at N, but both gravitational potentials still exist—so include both PEs in energy.
Apply conservation: Ei = EN (1/2)mv2 − GMm/R − (4GMm)/(5R) = −GMm/(2R) − (4GMm)/(4R) Divide by m and simplify: (1/2)v2 − GM/R − 4GM/(5R) = −GM/(2R) − GM/R (1/2)v2 = 4GM/(5R) − GM/(2R) (1/2)v2 = ( (8 − 5) / 10 ) (GM/R) = (3/10)(GM/R)
Minimum launch speed: v = √( (3/5) GM/R ) Symbols: v = minimum projection speed from surface of M; G = gravitational constant; M = smaller sphere mass; R = sphere radius.
Point to note (explicitly stated): speed is zero at N, but it hits the heavier sphere (4M) with non-zero speed. (That impact speed is not found here.)

Earth Satellites

Earth satellites = objects revolving around Earth. Motion is like planets around Sun ⇒ Kepler’s laws apply. Orbits may be circular or elliptic.

Natural and artificial satellites

Moon = Earth’s only natural satellite; orbit nearly circular; period ≈ 27.3 days ≈ its rotation period about its own axis. Since 1957, many artificial satellites launched for telecommunication, geophysics, meteorology, etc.

Satellite in circular orbit of radius (RE + h)

Take orbital radius from Earth’s centre = (RE + h); satellite mass = m; speed = V.
Centripetal force needed: Fc = mV2/(RE + h) Symbols: Fc = centripetal force; m = satellite mass; V = orbital speed; RE = Earth radius; h = height above surface.
Gravitational force by Earth: Fg = GmME/(RE + h)2 Symbols: Fg = gravitational force magnitude; G = gravitational constant; ME = mass of Earth; others as above.
For circular orbit: Fc = Fg ⇒ cancel m:
Orbital speed: V = √(GME/(RE + h)) Symbols: V = satellite speed in circular orbit; G, ME, RE, h as defined.
From V = √(GME/(RE + h)) ⇒ V decreases when h increases.

Special case: orbit close to Earth (h = 0)

Speed for h = 0: V(h=0) = √(GME/RE) = √(gRE) Symbols: g = GME/RE2; others as above.

Time period of a circular satellite orbit

Distance in one round = 2π(RE + h). So time period T = distance/speed:
Time period: T = 2π(RE + h)/V = 2π(RE + h)3/2/√(GME) Symbols: T = orbital period; V = orbital speed; other symbols as defined.
Squaring:
Kepler’s law of periods (Earth satellites): T2 = k (RE + h)3, where k = 4π2/(GME) Symbols: k = constant for a given central mass (Earth here); T = period; (RE + h) = orbital radius.

Very close satellites (h ≪ RE)

If h is tiny compared to RE, neglect h:
Near-Earth orbital period: T0 = 2π √(RE/g) Symbols: T0 = period very close to Earth’s surface; RE = Earth radius; g = surface gravity.
Using g ≈ 9.8 m s−2, RE = 6400 km ⇒ T0 ≈ 85 minutes.

Example 7.5: Moons of Mars and Martian year (Kepler’s third law applications)

(i) Mass of Mars from Phobos orbit

Given: Phobos period T = 7 h 39 min = 459 min; orbital radius R = 9.4 × 103 km. Use Kepler’s 3rd law for a satellite around Mars (central mass Mm):
Mass from orbital data: T2 = 4π2R3/(GMm) ⇒ Mm = 4π2R3/(GT2) Symbols: Mm = mass of Mars; R = Phobos orbital radius; T = Phobos period; G = gravitational constant.
Substitution (as in text) ⇒ Mm = 6.48 × 1023 kg.

(ii) Length of the Martian year

Assume Earth + Mars move in circular orbits about Sun. Mars orbital radius = 1.52 × Earth’s. By Kepler’s 3rd law (about Sun):
Kepler scaling (planets about Sun): (TM/TE)2 = (RMS/RES)3 Symbols: TM = Martian year; TE = Earth year (365 d); RMS = Mars–Sun distance; RES = Earth–Sun distance.
So: TM = (1.52)3/2 × 365 = 684 days.
Planetary orbits are nearly circular (except Mercury and Mars). For Earth, ellipticity is tiny: b/a = 0.99986 (semi-minor/semi-major axis).

Example 7.6: Weighing the Earth (two methods to find ME)

Method 1: Using surface gravity g

Given: g = 9.81 m s−2, RE = 6.37 × 106 m.
Earth’s mass from g: g = GME/RE2 ⇒ ME = gRE2/G Symbols: ME = mass of Earth; g = surface gravity; RE = Earth radius; G = gravitational constant.
Numerical value: ME = 5.97 × 1024 kg.

Method 2: Using Moon as Earth’s satellite (Kepler’s third law)

Given: Moon distance R = 3.84 × 108 m; period T = 27.3 days.
Earth’s mass from Moon’s orbit: T2 = 4π2R3/(GME) ⇒ ME = 4π2R3/(GT2) Symbols: R = Earth–Moon distance; T = Moon’s orbital period; ME, G as defined.
Numerical value: ME = 6.02 × 1024 kg.
Both methods match closely; difference < 1%.

Example 7.7: Converting k units and Moon’s period from k

Convert k from SI to (days, kilometres)

Given: k = 10−13 s2 m−3. Need: seconds → days, metres → kilometres:
  • 1 day = 24×60×60 s
  • 1 km = 1000 m
Result (as in text): k = 1.33 × 10−14 d2 km−3.

Moon’s time period using k

Moon’s distance = 3.84 × 105 km.
Kepler form used: T2 = kR3 Symbols: T = time period; R = orbital radius (from Earth’s centre); k = 4π2/(GME) in chosen units.

Kepler’s law of periods (T2 ∝ a3) also works for elliptical orbits if (RE + h) is replaced by the semi-major axis of the ellipse. Earth lies at one focus.

Energy of an Orbiting Satellite

Kinetic energy in a circular orbit

Using orbital speed: v2 = GME/(RE + h)
Kinetic energy: KE = (1/2)mv2 = GmME/[2(RE + h)] Symbols: KE = kinetic energy; m = satellite mass; v = orbital speed; G = gravitational constant; ME = Earth mass; RE = Earth radius; h = orbital height.

Potential energy (zero at infinity)

Gravitational potential energy: PE = −GmME/(RE + h) Symbols: PE = potential energy (taking PE = 0 at infinity); other symbols as defined.
KE is +, PE is −. Magnitudes: KE = (1/2)|PE|.

Total energy of a circularly orbiting satellite

Total energy: E = KE + PE = −GmME/[2(RE + h)] Symbols: E = total mechanical energy; other symbols as defined.
Total energy for a circular orbit is negative. PE is negative and has 2× the magnitude of the +KE.

Elliptic orbit case (energy interpretation)

For an elliptic orbit: KE and PE change from point to point, but total energy stays constant and is still negative.
Core link to escape: If total energy is ≥ 0, the object can escape to infinity. Satellites stay at finite distance, so total energy cannot be ≥ 0.
