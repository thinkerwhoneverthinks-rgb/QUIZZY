Ch12: Kinetic Theory of Gases

12.1 Introduction

Kinetic theory (of gases): explains gas behaviour by modelling a gas as lots of rapidly moving atoms/molecules.
  • Historical development and motivation
    • 1661 (Boyle): discovered Boyle’s law (later named after him).
    • Early attempts: Boyle, Newton + others tried explaining gases by assuming they’re made of tiny particles.
    • Atomic theory acceptance: the “actual atomic theory” only became established 150+ years later.
    • 19th century development: kinetic theory developed by Maxwell, Boltzmann + others.
  • Why gases can be treated as moving particles
    • In gases, inter-atomic forces (short-range forces important in solids/liquids) can be neglected since molecules are far apart vs solids/liquids.
  • What kinetic theory successfully explains / connects
    • Gives a molecular interpretation of pressure and temperature.
    • Matches the gas laws + Avogadro’s hypothesis.
    • Correctly explains specific heat capacities of many gases.
    • Links measurable properties (e.g. viscosity, conduction, diffusion) to molecular parameters → estimate molecular sizes and masses.

12.2 Molecular Nature of Matter

Atomic hypothesis (Feynman’s statement)

Atomic Hypothesis (Feynman): “All things are made of atoms — tiny particles in perpetual motion, attracting each other when a little distance apart, but repelling when squeezed into one another.”
This one line packs 3 core ideas used later in kinetic theory/molecular physics:
  • Discrete matter → made of atoms.
  • Perpetual motion → thermal motion of particles.
  • Interatomic interaction → attraction at moderate separation, strong repulsion at very short separation.
  • Continuity vs discreteness: many cultures questioned whether matter is truly continuous.
  • Early proponents: Kanada (India) + Democritus (Greece) argued matter is made of indivisible constituents.

Dalton’s atomic theory and laws of chemical combination

John Dalton: modern scientific atomic theory is usually credited to Dalton; he used atoms to explain laws of chemical combination.
Law of definite proportions: a given compound has a fixed mass ratio of its constituents.
Law of multiple proportions: if two elements form more than one compound, then for a fixed mass of one element, the masses of the other that combine are in ratios of small integers.
  • Dalton’s explanation (core postulates here):
    • Smallest constituents of an element are atoms.
    • Atoms of the same element are identical; atoms of different elements differ.
    • A small number of atoms combine to form a molecule of a compound.

Gay-Lussac’s law and Avogadro’s law (hypothesis)

Gay-Lussac’s law (combining volumes): when gases react to form another gas, their volumes are in ratios of small integers.
Avogadro’s law (hypothesis): equal volumes of all gases at equal temperature and pressure contain the same number of molecules.
Key logical link: Avogadro’s law + Dalton’s theory ⇒ explains Gay-Lussac’s law. Reason: at same T,P, equal volumes ⇒ equal number of molecules, so volume ratios = ratios of reacting molecules, which often come as small integers in balanced equations.
Terminology note: since elements often exist as molecules (not single atoms), Dalton’s “atomic theory” is also called the molecular theory of matter.
  • Acceptance: widely accepted now, but even by late 19th century some famous scientists still doubted it.

Direct evidence and typical molecular/atomic sizes and separations

  • Constituents of matter: matter is made of molecules (each has one or more atoms).
  • Seeing atoms/molecules: possible using electron microscopes and scanning tunnelling microscopes.
1 angstrom: 1 Å = 10−10 m Å (angstrom): unit used for atomic-scale lengths m: metre (SI unit)
  • Typical sizes/distances:
    • Atom size: ~ 1 Å (≈ 10−10 m).
    • Solids: atoms closely packed; spacing ~ few Å, roughly 2 Å.
    • Liquids: separation ~ same as solids; atoms not rigidly fixed → can move → liquid flows.
    • Gases: interatomic distances ~ tens of Å.

Mean free path and freedom of motion in gases

Mean free path: average distance travelled by a molecule between collisions.
  • Order of magnitude (gases): mean free path ~ thousands of Å.
  • Consequence: molecules in gases are much freer; can go long distances without colliding.
  • Diffusion/dispersion: if not enclosed, gases spread out (disperse away).

Interatomic forces in solids, liquids, and gases

Why forces matter more in solids/liquids: particles are close → interatomic forces significant; in gases, often negligible as a first approximation.
Interatomic force nature: long-range attraction + short-range repulsion.
  • Attraction: when atoms are a few Å apart.
  • Repulsion: very strong when pushed too close (“squeezed into one another”).

Atomic hypothesis in Ancient India and Greece (context and key claims)

  • Ancient India — Vaiseshika school (Kanada, 6th century B.C.)
    • Atomic model developed in considerable detail.
    • Atoms taken as eternal, indivisible, infinitesimal — the ultimate parts of matter.
    • Discreteness argument: if subdivision never ended, no difference between a mustard seed and the Meru mountain.
    • Four atom types (Paramanu): Bhoomi (earth), Ap (water), Tejas (fire), Vayu (air) — each with its own mass/attributes.
    • Akasa (space): no atomic structure; continuous + inert.
    • Molecules: atoms combine to form different molecules; properties depend on nature + ratio of atoms.
    • Combination examples: 2 atoms → dvyanuka (diatomic); 3 atoms → tryanuka (triatomic).
    • Atomic size estimate: Lalitavistara (2nd century B.C.) gives ~modern order, ~ 10−10 m.
  • Ancient Greece — Democritus (4th century B.C.)
    • Linked to atomic hypothesis; Greek “atom” = indivisible.
    • Atoms differ in shape, size etc.; different combinations → different substances.
    • Illustrative explanations:
      • Water: atoms smooth/round; can’t “hook” → flows easily.
      • Earth: atoms rough/jagged → stick → hard substances.
      • Fire: atoms thorny → painful burns.
    • Limitation: clever but mostly intuitive; not tested/updated by quantitative experiments (key feature of modern science).
Exam traps to avoid:
  • Don’t mix up atomic size (~1 Å) with interatomic spacing: solids/liquids ~ few Å (≈2 Å), gases ~ tens of Å.
  • Mean free path in gases is much larger than spacing: ~ thousands of Å, not just “tens of Å”.
  • Avogadro’s law must say: equal volumes at same T and P have equal number of molecules (not “atoms”).
  • Interatomic force = attraction + repulsion; stating only one part is incomplete.

12.3 Behaviour of Gases

Dynamic equilibrium in a gas: Macroscopic properties can stay constant, but molecules are always moving, colliding, and changing speeds → only average values stay constant.

Why gases are simpler to model than solids/liquids

  • Gas molecules are usually far apart.
  • Intermolecular forces are mostly negligible except during collisions.
  • So at low P and high T (well above liquefaction/solidification), gases approximately follow simple P–V–T relations.

Empirical P–V–T relation for a fixed sample

PV = KT (fixed gas sample; approx. valid at low P, high T) P = pressure V = volume T = absolute temperature (K) K = constant for that sample (depends on amount of gas; changes if molecule number changes)
  • Molecular view: K ∝ N, where N = number of molecules.
  • So write K = Nk.
  • Experiments show k is the same for all gases → the Boltzmann constant kB.
PV = NkBT ⇒ PV/(NT) = constant = kB N = number of molecules kB = Boltzmann constant (universal)
: If two gases have the same P, V, T, then they must have the same N → leads to Avogadro’s hypothesis.

Avogadro’s hypothesis, Avogadro number, and the mole

Avogadro’s hypothesis: At fixed T and P, the number of molecules per unit volume is the same for all gases (equal volumes at same P,T contain equal numbers of molecules).
  • Number of molecules in 22.4 L of any gas at STP = 6.02 × 1023.
  • This is the Avogadro number NA.
  • STP (here): 273 K and 1 atm.
  • Mass of 22.4 L of any gas at STP = its molecular weight in grams.
  • This amount is one mole.
  • Historically: Avogadro used reaction data; kinetic theory explains why it works.

Ideal (perfect) gas equation and the universal gas constant

PV = μRT μ = number of moles R = universal gas constant = NAkB T = absolute temperature (K)
Using kelvin (absolute T): R = 8.314 J mol−1 K−1.
μ = M/M0 = N/NA M = mass of sample containing N molecules M0 = molar mass (mass of 1 mole) NA = Avogadro number
  • From (12.4) + (12.3), ideal gas law in molecular form:
PV = kBNT kB = Boltzmann constant N = number of molecules
  • Divide by V to get pressure form using number density n.
P = kB n T n = number density = molecules per unit volume (= N/V)
Boltzmann constant (SI): kB = 1.38 × 10−23 J K−1.

Density form of the ideal gas equation

ρ = (P M0)/(R T) (equivalently R T ρ / P = M0) ρ = mass density P = pressure M0 = molar mass R = gas constant T = absolute temperature
Exam tip: Always use kelvin in ideal-gas questions. From °C: T(K) = t(°C) + 273 (approx.).

Ideal gas vs real gas; when real gases approximate ideal behaviour

Ideal gas: obeys PV = μRT exactly at all P and T. It’s a model (no real gas is perfectly ideal).
  • Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour, but deviations are small at:
    • low pressures
    • high temperatures
  • Why: molecules far apart → intermolecular forces negligible → near-ideal behaviour.
  • Graph idea (Fig. 12.1): real-gas curves tend towards the ideal-gas line as P ↓ and T ↑.

Boyle’s law (isothermal process)

  • Hold μ and T constant in PV = μRT.
PV = constant (fixed amount of gas, constant T) Meaning: for fixed mass at constant T, P ∝ 1/V.
Boyle’s law works best at high T and low P. Experimental P–V curves approach the ideal hyperbola in these conditions (Fig. 12.2).

Charles’ law (isobaric process)

  • Hold P constant. From the P–V–T relation, V is proportional to absolute T.
Charles’ law: V ∝ T (constant pressure, fixed amount of gas) Meaning: for fixed P, V/T = constant.
  • Graph idea: measured T–V curves get close to the straight line predicted by Charles’ law under suitable conditions.

Mixture of ideal gases and Dalton’s law of partial pressures

  • Take a mixture of non-interacting ideal gases in volume V at temperature T.
  • Mixture has μ1 moles of gas 1, μ2 moles of gas 2, … and total pressure P.
PV = (μ1 + μ2 + …) RT P = total pressure μi = moles of i-th gas
P = (μ1RT)/V + (μ2RT)/V + … P = P1 + P2 + … Pi = partial pressure of i-th gas
Partial pressure P1: P1 = μ1RT / V = pressure gas 1 would exert if it alone filled volume V at the same T.
Dalton’s law: For ideal gas mixtures, P = ΣPi (total pressure = sum of partial pressures).
Common mistake: Dalton’s law assumes ideal / non-interacting gases. At high P or low T, interactions matter → deviations.

Example

The density of water is 1000 kg m–3. The density of water vapour at 100 °C and 1 atm pressure is 0.6 kg m–3. The volume of a molecule multiplied by the total number gives ,what is called, molecular volume. Estimate the ratio (or fraction) of the molecular volume to the total volume occupied by the water vapour under the above conditions of temperature and pressure.
  • Given:
    • Liquid water density: ρwater = 1000 kg m−3
    • Water vapour density at 100 °C, 1 atm: ρvap = 0.6 kg m−3
  • Molecular volume (as stated): “(volume of one molecule) × (total molecules)” → i.e. volume actually occupied by the molecules, ignoring empty space.
  • Task: estimate the fraction = (molecular volume)/(total vapour volume) under these conditions.
  • Reasoning framework: take the same mass: in liquid it’s nearly close-packed (small volume), in gas it occupies a much larger volume; ratio ≈ ρvap/ρwater for the same mass.

Estimating molecular volume and size (water)

Density ↔ volume (fixed mass): For the same mass, if density ↓ then volume ↑. So when liquid → vapour, the volume increases by the inverse of the density drop.
How much of the phase volume is “actual molecule volume”?
  • Assume (roughly) in liquid the molecules are closely packed ⇒ density of a molecule ≈ density of bulk water.
  • So in liquid: fraction (molecular volume / total volume) ≈ 1.
  • If on vaporisation the total volume for the same mass increases by factor k, then in vapour the fraction becomes 1/k.
Given data: vapour volume = (1000/0.6) × liquid volume = 1/(6 × 10−4) times larger ⇒ fraction occupied by molecules in vapour ≈ 6 × 10−4.

Example

Estimate the volume of a water molecule using the data in Example 12.1.
Close-packing idea (liquid/solid): In liquid (or solid) water, molecules are closely packed ⇒ take density of a molecule ≈ density of bulk water.
Molar mass: M(H2O) = (2 + 16) g = 18 g = 0.018 kg Mass of one molecule: m = (mass of 1 mole)/(NA) = (0.018)/(6 × 1023) kg = 3 × 10−26 kg From density: V = m/ρ Sphere model: V = (4/3)πr3 Symbols: m = mass of one molecule; ρ = density; V = volume of one molecule; r = effective radius; π = 3.1416…; NA ≈ 6 × 1023 mol−1.
Given/assumed: ρ (bulk water) ≈ 1000 kg m−3. Step 1: mass of one molecule 1 mole has mass 0.018 kg and contains ~6 × 1023 molecules ⇒ m = (0.018)/(6 × 1023) = 3 × 10−26 kg. Step 2: volume of one molecule V = m/ρ = (3 × 10−26)/(1000) = 3 × 10−29 m3. Step 3: effective radius Using V = (4/3)πr3 ⇒ r ≈ 2 × 10−10 m = 2 Å.
These are order-of-magnitude estimates: molecule ≈ sphere + liquid ≈ closely packed. 1 Å = 10−10 m; many exam answers expect Å-scale molecular sizes.

Estimating interatomic/intermolecular distance in water

Example

What is the average distance between atoms (interatomic distance) in water? Use the data given in Examples 12.1 and 12.2. Given (from earlier): Same mass of water as vapour has 1.67 × 103 times the volume of the same mass as liquid.
Scaling rule: If volume available per molecule increases by factor K, then a typical linear length (separation) scales as K1/3.
Reasoning: Volume per molecule in vapour increases by 1.67 × 103 ≈ 103 ⇒ linear scale increases by (103)1/3 = 10. From Example 12.2: r ≈ 2 Å. New “radius scale” in vapour ≈ 10 × 2 Å = 20 Å. Average distance (≈ twice this scale) ≈ 2 × 20 Å = 40 Å.
Don’t scale distance with the volume factor directly. Use cube-root scaling: distance ∝ V1/3.

Partial pressures in gas mixtures: linking pressure, mole number, molecule number, and density

Example

vessel contains two nonreactive gases : neon (monatomic) and oxygen (diatomic). The ratio of their partial pressures is 3:2. Estimate the ratio of (i) number of molecules and (ii) mass density of neon and oxygen in the vessel. Atomic mass of Ne = 20.2 u, molecular mass of O2 = 32.0 u.
Given: Two non-reactive gases in a vessel: neon (Ne) + oxygen (O2). Partial pressure ratio: PNe : PO2 = 3 : 2. Atomic/molecular masses: Ne = 20.2 u, O2 = 32.0 u.
Partial pressure: Pressure a gas would exert if it alone occupied the vessel at the same V and T. Dalton’s law: For non-reactive gases, Ptotal = ΣPi.
Ideal gas: PV = μRT Amount of substance: μ = N/NA = m/M Symbols: P = pressure; V = volume; μ = moles; R = gas constant; T = absolute temperature; N = number of molecules; NA = Avogadro’s number; m = mass; M = molar mass; ρ = mass density (= m/V).
For two ideal gases in the same vessel, V and T are common ⇒ from PV = μRT, P ∝ μ (at fixed V, T).
Let 1 = neon, 2 = oxygen. With common V, T: P1V = μ1RT and P2V = μ2RT ⇒ (P1/P2) = (μ1/μ2). Given (P1/P2) = 3/2 ⇒ (μ1/μ2) = 3/2. (i) Ratio of number of molecules μ = N/NA ⇒ μ1/μ2 = N1/N2. So, NNe/NO2 = 3/2. (ii) Ratio of mass densities μ = m/M ⇒ μ1/μ2 = (m1/M1)/(m2/M2) = (m1/m2)(M2/M1). Also ρ = m/V, and both gases share the same vessel volume V, so ρ1/ρ2 = (m1/V)/(m2/V) = m1/m2. Hence, ρ1/ρ2 = (μ1/μ2)(M1/M2). Substitute μ1/μ2 = 3/2, M1 = 20.2, M2 = 32.0: ρNe/ρO2 = (3/2) × (20.2/32.0) = 0.947 (approx).
Using μ = m/M: keep m and M in the same units (both kg or both g). Same vessel: don’t mix up number density (molecules/volume) vs mass density (mass/volume).

12.4 Kinetic theory of an ideal gas: assumptions and molecular picture

Kinetic theory idea: A gas has a huge number of molecules (≈ Avogadro scale) in continuous random motion.
  • At ordinary P and T, average intermolecular separation is about 10× (or more) the molecular size (~2 Å).
  • So intermolecular forces are usually negligible; molecules travel in straight lines (Newton’s 1st law) between collisions.
  • Sometimes molecules come close; forces act and velocities change → these interactions are collisions.
  • Molecules collide with each other + container walls; kinetic theory treats collisions as elastic.
Elastic collision ⇒ total kinetic energy is conserved (and total momentum is conserved as usual).

12.4.1 Pressure of an ideal gas from kinetic theory

12.4.1 Derivation setup (cube model and momentum transfer)

Take gas in a cube of side l. Choose axes along the sides. Pick the wall parallel to the yz-plane, area A = l2.
One molecule hitting the wall: Molecule of mass m, velocity components (vx, vy, vz) strikes the wall (normal along x). Elastic collision with a smooth wall ⇒
  • vy, vz unchanged
  • vx reverses: (vx, vy, vz) → (−vx, vy, vz)
Change in molecule momentum (x-dir): Δpx = (−mvx) − (mvx) = −2mvx. So momentum given to the wall per collision = +2mvx (momentum conservation).

Molecules hitting the wall in time Δt

In time Δt, a molecule with x-component vx reaches the wall only if it is within distance vxΔt from it. So only molecules in volume A vx Δt are “eligible” to hit in that time.
Random motion ⇒ on average half move towards the wall, half away. So number striking the wall in time Δt: (1/2) n A vx Δt Here n = number density (molecules per unit volume) for that velocity group.
Total momentum transferred to wall in time Δt (Eq. 12.10): Q = (2 m vx) ( (1/2) n A vx Δt ) Symbols: Q = total momentum transferred; m = molecular mass; vx = x-component of velocity; n = number density; A = wall area; Δt = time interval.
Force + pressure link: Force on wall = (rate of momentum transfer) = Q/Δt. Pressure P = force/area = (Q/Δt)/A.
Pressure due to molecules with x-speed vx (Eq. 12.11): P = Q/(AΔt) = n m vx2 Symbols: P = pressure; n = number density (for that velocity group); m = molecular mass; vx2 = square of x-component.

Velocity distribution and averaging

Real gas: molecules don’t all have the same velocity ⇒ there’s a distribution. So we add up (average) over all velocity groups.
Total pressure using mean of vx2 (Eq. 12.12): P = n m ⟨vx2⟩ Symbols: ⟨vx2⟩ = average of vx2 over molecules; n = overall number density.
Isotropy (no preferred direction): In equilibrium, averages are the same in x, y, z: ⟨vx2⟩ = ⟨vy2⟩ = ⟨vz2⟩.
Component mean-squares vs mean-square speed (Eq. 12.13): ⟨vx2⟩ = (1/3)[⟨vx2⟩ + ⟨vy2⟩ + ⟨vz2⟩] = (1/3)⟨v2⟩ Symbols: v = speed; ⟨v2⟩ = mean of (speed)2.
Kinetic theory pressure equation (Eq. 12.14): P = (1/3) n m ⟨v2⟩ Symbols: P = pressure; n = number density (molecules per unit volume); m = mass of one molecule; ⟨v2⟩ = mean square speed.

Remarks on the derivation (what to remember)

  • Container shape doesn’t matter: Cube is just for convenience. For any vessel, take a tiny planar area element and run the same logic. Final result has no A or Δt.
  • Pascal’s law (Ch. 9): Equilibrium gas ⇒ pressure same everywhere, so the result applies throughout the gas.
  • We “ignore” intermolecular collisions: Not fully justified here, but in steady state, random collisions don’t change the average hit-rate on the wall for given velocity components: statistically, molecules replaced by others with similar components.
Exam trap: don’t write P = nmv2. Correct is P = (1/3)nm⟨v2⟩. The 1/3 is from isotropy (3 equal directional components). Be clear: v = speed; vx, vy, vz = components. Use squares + averages (mean-square), not plain means.

Molecular Collisions and Steady Velocity Distribution

For a steady velocity distribution, collisions must reshuffle velocities so that after collision molecules can end up with many possible velocities (e.g. components (vx, vy, vz)) while the overall statistical distribution stays unchanged.
While finding averages like ⟨vx2⟩ (⇒ pressure), collisions don’t change the result if:
  • collisions are not “too frequent” (in the sense needed for this derivation), and
  • collision time is negligible compared to time between collisions.
Then collisions don’t affect the mean-squared velocity components used in kinetic theory.

12.4.2 Kinetic Interpretation of Temperature

Pressure–Kinetic Energy Relations

$P V=(1 / 3) n V m \overline{v^2}$ $P V=(2 / 3) N x^{1 / 2} m \overline{v^2}$ P = pressure V = volume of gas n = number density (molecules per unit volume) N = number of molecules in sample = nV m = mass of one molecule ⟨v2⟩ = mean square speed (also written as <v2>)
The bracketed term (½ m ⟨v2⟩) = average translational KE per molecule.

Internal Energy of an Ideal Gas (Purely Kinetic)

$E=N \quad(1 / 2) m \overline{v^2}$ E = internal energy of an ideal gas (here: translational part) N = number of molecules m = mass of one molecule ⟨v2⟩ = mean square speed
In this section, E means translational internal energy only. Total internal energy U can also include other degrees of freedom (later in chapter).

Link Between Pressure and Internal Energy

PV = (2/3) E    P = pressure V = volume E = internal energy (translational here)

Temperature as Average Molecular Kinetic Energy

E = (3/2) kB N T    (12.18) or $E / N=1 / 2 m \overline{v^2}=(3 / 2) k_B T$ E = internal energy (translational) N = number of molecules kB = Boltzmann constant T = absolute temperature (kelvin) m = mass of one molecule ⟨v2⟩ = mean square speed
Kinetic meaning of temperature: average translational KE per molecule ∝ absolute temperature $1 / 2 m \overline{\left\langle v^2\right\rangle}=(3 / 2) k_B T$.
  • Average KE per molecule depends only on T.
  • It’s independent of pressure, volume, and the type of ideal gas.
  • kB links macroscopic T (thermo variable) to microscopic molecular KE.
  • From : ideal-gas internal energy depends only on temperature, not on P or V.
  • So kinetic theory lines up with the ideal gas equation + gas laws from it.
Don’t mix up ⟨v⟩ (average speed) with √⟨v2⟩ (rms speed). The temperature link uses ⟨v2⟩.

Mixture of Non-reactive Ideal Gases and Dalton’s Law

$P=(1 / 3)\left[n_1 m_1 \overline{v_1^2}+n_2 m_2 \overline{v_2^2}+\ldots\right]$ P = total pressure of mixture ni = number density of gas i mi = molecular mass of gas i $\left\langle v_i^2\right\rangle$ = mean square speed of molecules of gas i
At equilibrium (same T), different gases have the same average KE per molecule: $1 / 2 m_1 \overline{v_1^2}=1 / 2 m_2 \overline{v_2^2}=(3 / 2) k_B T$
P = (n1 + n2 + …) kB T P = total pressure n1, n2, … = number densities of components kB = Boltzmann constant T = absolute temperature
= Dalton’s law of partial pressures: total pressure = sum of the pressures each gas would exert alone at the same T and V (i.e. depends on total number density).

Root Mean Square Speed (rms) and Typical Molecular Speeds

$\overline{\left\langle v^2\right\rangle}$ = 3 kB T / m $\overline{\left\langle v^2\right\rangle}$ = mean square speed kB = Boltzmann constant T = absolute temperature m = mass of one molecule
Root mean square (rms) speed: $\mathrm{V}_{\mathrm{rms}}=\overline{\mathrm{V}}^2$
  • At T = 300 K, for nitrogen: √⟨v2⟩ ≈ 516 m s−1 (about the speed of sound in air).
  • From ½ m ⟨v2⟩ = (3/2) kBT: at the same T, lighter molecules → bigger vrms.

Worked Examples

Example

A flask contains argon and chlorine in the ratio of 2:1 by mass. The temperature of the mixture is 27 °C. Obtain the ratio of (i) average kinetic energy per molecule, and (ii) root mean square speed vrms of the molecules of the two gases. Atomic mass of argon = 39.9 u; Molecular mass of chlorine = 70.9 u.
Crucial fact: For any ideal gas (mono/di/poly), average KE per molecule = (3/2)kBT → depends only on T, not on gas type.
Given: same T = 27 °C, atomic mass Ar = 39.9 u, molecular mass Cl2 = 70.9 u.
(i) Ratio of average kinetic energy per molecule (Ar : Cl2)
  • Same flask ⇒ same temperature T.
  • Average KE/molecule = (3/2)kBT for both.
  • So KE ratio (Ar : Cl2) = 1 : 1.
(ii) Ratio of rms speeds
½ m vrms2 = (3/2) kB T m = mass of one molecule vrms = root mean square speed T = absolute temperature
  • Fixed T ⇒ vrms2 ∝ 1/m ⇒ vrms ∝ 1/√m.
  • So (vrms,Cl/vrms,Ar)2 = mAr/mCl = MAr/MCl.
  • (vrms,Cl/vrms,Ar)2 = 70.9/39.9 = 1.77.
  • √ gives: vrms,Ar/vrms,Cl = 1.33.
The mixture ratio by mass (2:1) is useless here. For (i) & (ii), only common T and molecular masses matter.

Example

Uranium has two isotopes of masses 235 and 238 units. If both are present in Uranium hexafluoride gas which would have the larger average speed ? If atomic mass of fluorine is 19 units, estimate the percentage difference in speeds at any temperature.
UF6 = U + 6F. Given fluorine atomic mass = 19 u.
Step 1: Compare molecular masses
  • 235U: mass = 235 + 6×19 = 235 + 114 = 349 u.
  • 238U: mass = 238 + 114 = 352 u.
Step 2: Use fixed-temperature kinetic theory
At fixed T, average energy per molecule ½ m⟨v2⟩ is constant ⇒ smaller mass ⇒ higher speed.
  • Speed ratio ∝ 1/√(mass):
v349 / v352 = √(352/349) = 1.0044 v349 = average (or rms-type) speed scale for 349 u molecule v352 = speed scale for 352 u molecule
  • % difference: Δv/v = (1.0044 − 1)×100% = 0.44%.
  • Hence 235UF6 (349 u) has larger average speed.
Application: enrichment by diffusion through porous barrier—lighter (faster) molecules escape slightly more; inefficient → repeat many times.
Diffusion link: diffusion rate ∝ 1/√(molecular mass), since v ∝ 1/√m at fixed T.

Example

(a) When a molecule (or an elastic ball) hits a ( massive) wall, it rebounds with the same speed. When a ball hits a massive bat held firmly, the same thing happens. However, when the bat is moving towards the ball, the ball rebounds with a different speed. Does the ball move faster or slower? (Ch.5 will refresh your memory on elastic collisions.) (b) When gas in a cylinder is compressed by pushing in a piston, its temperature rises. Guess at an explanation of this in terms of kinetic theory using (a) above. (c) What happens when a compressed gas pushes a piston out and expands. What would you observe ? (d) Sachin Tendulkar used a heavy cricket bat while playing. Did it help him in anyway ? (a) Ball hits a massive moving bat: faster or slower rebound?
Elastic hit off a massive wall/bat: speed relative to bat reverses direction, same magnitude.
  • Ball speed in ground (wicket) frame = u (towards bat).
  • Bat moves towards ball with speed V (towards ball) in ground frame.
  • Ball speed relative to bat before = V + u (towards bat).
  • After elastic rebound (massive bat): relative speed = V + u (away from bat).
  • Back to ground frame: rebound speed = V + (V + u) = 2V + u (away from wicket).
  • So ball speeds up if bat is approaching and massive.
If bat isn’t massive, rebound speed may be < u. The “massive bat” assumption makes the relative-speed reversal exact.
(b) Why does temperature rise on compressing gas with a piston?
  • Compression: piston moves inward towards molecules.
  • Elastic hits with an inward-moving piston → molecules rebound faster (same idea as (a)).
  • Average molecular KE increases ⇒ T increases.
(c) What happens when compressed gas expands pushing piston out?
  • Expansion: piston moves outward, away from molecules.
  • Molecules hit a receding piston → rebound slower.
  • Average KE decreases ⇒ T decreases.
  • Observed: gas cools (cooling effects depend on conditions).
(d) Heavy cricket bat and hitting advantage (Sachin Tendulkar case)
  • Heavier bat behaves closer to a “massive” object in collision.
  • So rebound speed of ball is larger (near massive-bat ideal) → better hitting.

12.5 Law of Equipartition of Energy

Translational KE of one molecule (3D) = sum of KE along 3 mutually perpendicular directions (x, y, z).
Kinetic energy of a single molecule εt = (1/2)m vx2 + (1/2)m vy2 + (1/2)m vz2 εt: translational kinetic energy (one molecule) m: mass of one molecule vx, vy, vz: velocity components along x, y, z
Thermal equilibrium average: at absolute temperature T, molecular energies (and hence KE) have a definite average over many molecules (equipartition idea).
Average translational kinetic energy per molecule <εt> = (1/2)m<vx2> + (1/2)m<vy2> + (1/2)m<vz2> = (3/2)kBT <εt>: average over molecules (or over time) in equilibrium kB: Boltzmann constant T: absolute temperature (K)
No preferred direction in equilibrium ⇒ x, y, z are symmetric ⇒ average energy splits equally into the 3 translational parts.
Equal sharing among translational components (1/2)m<vx2> = (1/2)kBT (1/2)m<vy2> = (1/2)kBT (1/2)m<vz2> = (1/2)kBT vx, vy, vz: velocity components kBT/2: average energy per translational quadratic term

Degrees of freedom (translational) and “quadratic terms”

Degree of freedom (DOF): number of independent coordinates needed to specify position (or configuration).
  • Constrained to a line → 1 coordinate → 1 DOF
  • Motion in a plane → 2 coordinates → 2 DOF
  • Motion in space → 3 coordinates → 3 DOF
Translation: whole-body motion from one point to another. A molecule free in space has 3 translational DOF.
Each translational DOF gives an energy term with a square (a quadratic term), e.g. (1/2)mvx2. In equilibrium, the average of each quadratic term is (1/2)kBT.

Rotational and vibrational degrees of freedom

Monatomic gases (e.g. argon): only translational DOF. Diatomic gases (e.g. O2, N2): translation + rotation about axes through centre of mass ⇒ rotational DOF.
Diatomic rigid-rotator model: diatomic molecule has two independent rotation axes (⊥ to the bond). Rotation about the bond axis is ignored here: moment of inertia is tiny and (quantum reasons) it becomes “inactive” under ordinary conditions.
Total energy of a rigid diatomic molecule (translation + rotation) ε = εt + εr = (1/2)m vx2 + (1/2)m vy2 + (1/2)m vz2 + (1/2)I1ω12 + (1/2)I2ω22 ε: total energy (one molecule) εt: translational part εr: rotational part I1, I2: moments of inertia about the two independent axes ω1, ω2: angular speeds about those axes
Each rotational DOF contributes one quadratic term of the form (1/2)Iω2, just like translation has (1/2)mv2.
Vibrational mode: atoms oscillate along the interatomic axis (treat as a 1D oscillator). Different from translation/rotation: one vibrational mode contributes two quadratic terms:
  • KE term (depends on (dy/dt)2)
  • PE term (depends on y2)
Energy including vibration (example: CO at moderate T) ε = εt + εr + εv with εv = (1/2)m(dy/dt)2 + (1/2)k y2 εv: vibrational energy y: vibrational coordinate (displacement along interatomic axis) dy/dt: vibrational speed k: force constant of the oscillator m: (effective) mass for the vibration in this model
Vibration key point: one vibrational frequency ⇒ two quadratic terms (KE + PE). But each translational/rotational DOF ⇒ only one quadratic term.

Statement of the law of equipartition of energy

Mode of absorption of energy: each quadratic term in the energy expression is one independent way (“mode”) to store energy. Law of equipartition of energy (Maxwell): in thermal equilibrium at absolute temperature T, energy is equally shared among all quadratic modes; each quadratic mode has average energy (1/2)kBT.
  • Each translational quadratic term ⇒ average energy (1/2)kBT.
  • Each rotational quadratic term ⇒ average energy (1/2)kBT.
  • Each vibrational mode ⇒ total average energy kBT, since it has two terms: 2 × (1/2)kBT.
Proof not needed here; in exams you mainly apply it to find internal energy and specific heats.
Exam trap: Don’t mix up DOF and mode for vibration. One vibrational mode gives two quadratic terms (average energy = kBT), but one translational/rotational DOF gives one quadratic term (average = (1/2)kBT).

12.6 Specific Heat Capacity (from equipartition)

12.6.1 Monatomic gases

Monatomic ideal gas molecule → only 3 translational DOF. Average energy per molecule at temperature T:
  • 3 quadratic translational terms → ⟨E⟩ = 3 × (1/2)kBT = (3/2)kBT
Internal energy of 1 mole of monatomic ideal gas U = (3/2)kBT × NA = (3/2)RT U: internal energy of 1 mole of the gas NA: Avogadro number R: gas constant (= NAkB) T: absolute temperature
Molar specific heat at constant volume (monatomic) Cv = dU/dT = (3/2)R Cv: molar heat capacity at constant volume
Ideal-gas relation between molar heat capacities Cp − Cv = R Cp: molar heat capacity at constant pressure
Molar specific heat at constant pressure (monatomic) Cp = (5/2)R
Ratio of specific heats (monatomic) γ = Cp/Cv = 5/3 γ: adiabatic index (ratio of molar heat capacities)

12.6.2 Diatomic gases

Rigid diatomic molecule (dumbbell model) DOF:
  • 3 translational
  • 2 rotational (about axes ⟂ to bond axis)
Total = 5 DOF → 5 quadratic terms → ⟨E⟩ per molecule = (5/2)kBT.
Internal energy of 1 mole of rigid diatomic ideal gas U = (5/2)kBT × NA = (5/2)RT
Heat capacities (rigid diatomic) Cv = (5/2)R Cp = (7/2)R
Ratio of specific heats (rigid diatomic) γ = 7/5
If the diatomic molecule is not rigid and has one vibrational mode:
  • vibration adds kBT per molecule (2 quadratic terms)
  • total per molecule: (5/2 + 1)kBT = (7/2)kBT
Diatomic gas with one vibrational mode U = (7/2)RT Cv = (7/2)R Cp = (9/2)R γ = 9/7
Exam Tip: For diatomic gases, explicitly specify the molecular model being used:
  • Rigid Rotator (Vibrational modes inactive): 5 DOF → Cv = 5R/2, γ = 7/5
  • Non-Rigid / Vibrating (One vibrational mode active): Contributes additional : + kB T per molecule (or per mole) → Cv = 7R/2, γ = 9/7

12.6.3 Polyatomic gases

General polyatomic molecule has:
  • 3 translational DOF
  • 3 rotational DOF
  • some number f of vibrational modes
Equipartition contributions (per molecule):
  • translation: (3/2)kBT
  • rotation: (3/2)kBT
  • vibration: f·kBT (each vibrational mode gives kBT)
Polyatomic ideal gas (with f vibrational modes active) U = NA[(3/2)kBT + (3/2)kBT + f kBT] Cv = (3 + f)R Cp = (4 + f)R γ = (4 + f)/(3 + f) f: number of vibrational modes contributing at the given temperature
Universality (ideal gases): Cp − Cv = R for any ideal gas (mono/di/poly), regardless of vibrational modes.
Exam trap: Real gases can have vibrational modes “frozen out” (quantum effect) at ordinary T. In this classical treatment, if a vibrational mode is said to be active, count kBT per molecule (not (1/2)kBT).

Law of Equipartition of Energy: Experimental Verification and Limits

The law of equipartition of energy is experimentally well verified for many gases at ordinary temperatures when only translational and (where applicable) rotational modes are counted.
  • Tables of predicted specific heats (ignoring vibrational modes) match measured values well for several gases.
  • For some gases (e.g. Cl2, C2H6, and many other polyatomic gases), experimental specific heats are greater than predictions if vibration is ignored.
  • This mismatch suggests: include vibrational modes for better agreement (esp. polyatomic molecules, and depends on temperature).

Predicted molar specific heats of gases (ignoring vibrational modes)

Ideal gas rule (per mole): Cp − Cv = R, independent of gas type.
  • Monatomic gas (translation only):
    • Cv = 12.5 J mol−1 K−1
    • Cp = 20.8 J mol−1 K−1
    • Cp − Cv = 8.31 J mol−1 K−1
    • γ = Cp/Cv = 1.67
  • Diatomic gas (translation + rotation; vibration ignored):
    • Cv = 20.8 J mol−1 K−1
    • Cp = 29.1 J mol−1 K−1
    • Cp − Cv = 8.31 J mol−1 K−1
    • γ = 1.40
  • Triatomic gas (translation + rotation; vibration ignored):
    • Cv = 24.93 J mol−1 K−1
    • Cp = 33.24 J mol−1 K−1
    • Cp − Cv = 8.31 J mol−1 K−1
    • γ = 1.33
Ignoring vibrational modes often makes predicted values too low, especially for polyatomic gases → usually means vibration is contributing at that temperature.

Example

A cylinder of fixed capacity 44.8 litres contains helium gas at standard temperature and pressure. What is the amount of heat needed to raise the temperature of the gas in the cylinder by 15.0 °C ? (R = 8.31 J mo1–1 K–1).
At constant volume, heat needed: Q = μ Cv ΔT (molar form) No boundary work (no volume change), so no extra work term.

Given

  • Volume of cylinder = 44.8 L
  • Gas = helium (monatomic)
  • Initially at STP: T = 273 K, P = 1 atm = 1.01 × 105 Pa
  • Temperature rise ΔT = 15.0 °C = 15.0 K
  • R = 8.31 J mol−1 K−1

Step 1: Use molar volume at STP to find moles

From PV = μRT ⇒ at STP, 1 mol of ideal gas occupies 22.4 L (molar volume).
  • Moles in 44.8 L: μ = 44.8 / 22.4 = 2 mol

Step 2: Choose the correct specific heat (fixed volume)

  • Helium is monatomic:
    • Cv = (3/2)R
    • Cp = Cv + R = (3/2)R + R = (5/2)R
  • Volume fixed ⇒ use Cv.

Step 3: Compute heat required

Q = μ Cv ΔT Q = heat supplied (J) μ = number of moles (mol) Cv = molar specific heat at constant volume (J mol−1 K−1) ΔT = temperature rise (K)
  • Q = 2 × (3/2)R × 15.0 = 45R
  • Q = 45 × 8.31 = 374 J
⚠ Do not use Cp when volume is fixed. Constant pressure heating → Cp; constant volume heating → Cv.

12.6.4 Specific Heat Capacity of Solids (Equipartition Approach)

Model: atoms as 3D oscillators

In a solid, each atom vibrates about its mean position. For harmonic oscillation (1D), equipartition gives:
  • Avg KE = (1/2)kBT
  • Avg PE = (1/2)kBT
  • Total per 1D oscillator = kBT
In 3D: average energy per atom = 3kBT.

Internal energy of 1 mole of solid

  • For 1 mole, number of atoms N = NA.
  • Total energy:
U = 3 kB T × NA = 3RT U = internal energy per mole (J mol−1) kB = Boltzmann constant T = absolute temperature (K) NA = Avogadro constant R = gas constant (= kBNA)

Specific heat of solid at constant pressure (≈ constant volume)

  • At constant pressure: ΔQ = ΔU + PΔV.
  • For solids, ΔV is negligible ⇒ ΔQ ≈ ΔU.
C = (ΔQ/ΔT) = (ΔU/ΔT) = 3R C = molar specific heat capacity of the solid (J mol−1 K−1) Q = heat supplied (J) U = internal energy (J) T = temperature (K) R = gas constant
Predicts for many solids near ordinary temps: C ≈ 3R Experiments mostly match at room temp; carbon is an exception.
⚠ ΔQ = ΔU only because ΔV ≈ 0 for solids. Don’t use this for gases.

Mean Free Path

Why diffusion is slow despite high molecular speeds

  • Gas molecules move very fast (≈ speed of sound), yet diffusion across a room is slow; smoke clouds hang around.
  • Reason: molecules have finite size and collide often → they don’t go straight for long; paths keep getting deflected.

Collision model: hard spheres of diameter d

Model molecules as spheres of diameter d. A chosen molecule with average speed <v> collides if another molecule’s centre comes within distance d.
  • In time Δt, collision volume swept:
    • Volume swept = πd2 <v> Δt
  • If n = number density (molecules per unit volume), expected collisions in time Δt:
    • Collisions = n π d2 <v> Δt
  • Collision rate = n π d2 <v>

Mean time between collisions (τ)

τ = 1 / (n π <v> d2) τ = mean time between successive collisions (s) n = number density (m−3) <v> = average speed of molecules (m s−1) d = molecular diameter (m)

Mean free path (l): basic derivation

$$ \tau=\frac{1}{\left(\mathrm{n} \pi<\mathrm{v}>\mathrm{d}^2\right)} $$ l = mean free path (m) <v> = average speed (m s−1) τ = mean collision time (s) n = number density (m−3) d = molecular diameter (m)

Correction for motion of all molecules (relative speed)

  • The above assumes other molecules are at rest.
  • Actually, all molecules move → collision rate depends on average relative speed <vr>.
  • So replace <v> in τ with <vr>.
  • More exact result (standard mean free path):
l = 1 / ( √2 π n d2 ) l = mean free path (m) n = number density (m−3) d = molecular diameter (m)
l ∝ 1/n and l ∝ 1/d2 So: denser gas or bigger molecules ⇒ smaller mean free path.

Numerical estimate at STP (air)

  • Given average speed <v> = 485 m s−1
  • At STP, number density:
n = (0.02 × 1023) / (22.4 × 10−3) = 2.7 × 1025 m−3 n = number density (m−3)
  • Take molecular diameter d = 2 × 10−10 m
  • Results:
    • τ = 6.1 × 10−10 s
    • l = 2.9 × 10−7 m ≈ 1500 d
  • In a highly evacuated tube, n is tiny ⇒ l can be comparable to tube length.

Example

Estimate the mean free path for a water molecule in water vapour at 373 K. Use information from Exercises 12.1 and Eq. (12.41) above.

Key ideas used

  • Take water vapour molecular diameter same as air: d same as in the STP estimate.
  • For fixed pressure: number density n is inversely proportional to T ⇒ n ∝ 1/T.

Number density at 373 K

  • Use n(273 K) = 2.7 × 1025 m−3:
n(373 K) = (273/373) × 2.7 × 1025 ≈ 2 × 1025 m−3 n = number density (m−3) T = absolute temperature (K)

Mean free path

  • Using the earlier STP-based estimate relationship (Eq. 12.41 context), mean free path:
  • l ≈ 4 × 10−7 m
Mean free path ≈ 100× the interatomic distance (~ 40 Å = 4 × 10−9 m) found earlier. This big l is typical of gases → gases can’t be confined without a container.

Why mean free path matters

  • Kinetic theory links macroscopic transport (viscosity, heat conductivity, diffusion) to microscopic stuff like molecular size.
  • Historically, these links were used to estimate molecular sizes.
