Ch-03 : Trigonometric Functions

3.1 Introduction

Meaning of trigonometry: from Greek “trigon” (triangle) + “metron” (measure) → “measuring the sides of a triangle”.
Origin and uses: made to solve geometric triangle problems; used in navigation (sea captains), surveying (mapping land), engineering, etc. Modern uses: seismology, electric circuits, atomic physics (state of an atom), tides, musical tone analysis, and many more. Link with earlier study: earlier: trigonometric ratios of acute angles = ratios of sides in a right triangle; identities; applications to heights & distances. Now: extend ratios to trigonometric functions and study their properties.

3.2 Angles

Angle as a measure of rotation

Angle = measure of the rotation of a ray about its starting point.
  • Initial side: ray before turning
  • Terminal side: ray after turning
  • Vertex: fixed point of rotation
  • Positive angle: rotate anticlockwise
  • Negative angle: rotate clockwise
  • Angle measure: how much rotation takes initial side → terminal side
Units for angles: A natural unit is a full revolution (handy for big turns, e.g., a wheel doing 15 rev/s). In maths we mostly use degrees and radians.

3.2.1 Degree measure

If the turn is 1/360 of a full revolution, the angle is 1 degree = 1°.
  • 1° = 60′ (minutes)
  • 1′ = 60″ (seconds)
  • So 1° = 3600″
Examples (as rotations): 360°, 180°, 270°, 420°, −30°, −420° (can exceed one full turn; negatives are clockwise).

3.2.2 Radian measure

1 radian = angle at the centre of a unit circle (radius 1) subtended by an arc of length 1.
  • Unit circle circumference = 2π ⇒ 1 full revolution = 2π radians
  • Radians link angles directly to arc length
Arc length ↔ angle (radians): θ = l/r   or   l = rθ θ = angle in radians l = intercepted arc length r = circle radius
Reasoning: In radius r, an arc of length r makes 1 radian at the centre. Equal arcs → equal central angles, so arc length l gives angle l/r radians.

3.2.3 Relation between radian measure and real numbers

On a unit circle (centre O), pick point A and take OA as the initial side. Then the arc length matches the radian measure of the central angle.
Draw the tangent at A (line PAQ). Let A represent real number 0.
  • Segment AP represents positive real numbers
  • Segment AQ represents negative real numbers
If we “wrap” (like a rope) AP anticlockwise onto the circle and AQ clockwise, then:
  • Every real number ↔ a radian measure
  • Every radian measure ↔ a real number
So radian measures and real numbers can be treated the same (for locating angles on the real line).

3.2.4 Relation between degree and radian

Conversion base: 2π radians = 360°  ⇒  π radians = 180° π = pi (≈ 3.14159; often use 22/7 if told)
Degree ↔ Radian: Radian measure = (π/180) × Degree measure Degree measure = (180/π) × Radian measure Degree measure = angle in degrees (°) Radian measure = angle in radians
Using π ≈ 22/7:
  • 1 radian = 180/π degrees ≈ 57°16′
  • 1° = π/180 radians ≈ 0.01746 radian
Common angles:
  • 30° = π/6
  • 45° = π/4
  • 60° = π/3
  • 90° = π/2
  • 180° = π
  • 270° = 3π/2
  • 360° = 2π

Notational Convention

  • Write θ° ⇒ θ is in degrees
  • Write β (no °) ⇒ β is in radians
  • In radians, we often omit the word “radian” (e.g., π/4 means radians)

Example 1: Convert 40° 20′ into radian measure

Step 1 (minutes → degree fraction): 20′ = 20/60° = 1/3°. So 40°20′ = 40 + 1/3 = 121/3 degrees. Step 2 (degrees → radians):
Radians = (π/180) × Degrees π = pi
Radians = (π/180) × (121/3) = 121π/540 radians.
Answer: 40°20′ = 121π/540 radians.

Example 2: Convert 6 radians into degree measure

Use π radian = 180°. Degrees = (180/π) × 6 = 1080/π degrees. Using π ≈ 22/7: Degrees ≈ 1080 × 7/22 = 343 7/11 degrees. Convert fractional degree → minutes:
  • 7/11 degree = (7/11)×60′ = 420/11′ = 38 + 2/11′
  • 2/11′ = (2/11)×60″ = 120/11″ ≈ 10.9″
Answer: 6 radians ≈ 343°38′11″ (approx).
When radians → degrees → minutes/seconds: multiply the fractional part by 60 each time (degree fraction → minutes; minute fraction → seconds).

Example 3: Radius when 60° intercepts an arc of length 37.4 cm (use π = 22/7)

Given arc length l = 37.4 cm and central angle θ = 60°. Step 1 (angle → radians): 60° = (π/180)×60 = π/3 radians. Step 2 (use l = rθ): r = l/θ.
r = l/θ r = radius l = arc length θ = central angle in radians
r = 37.4 ÷ (π/3) = 37.4×3/π. Using π = 22/7: r = 37.4×3×7/22 = 35.7 cm.
Answer: radius = 35.7 cm.

Example 4: Distance moved by the tip of a 1.5 cm minute hand in 40 minutes (use π = 3.14)

The minute hand does 1 revolution in 60 minutes. In 40 minutes: 40/60 = 2/3 revolution. Angle turned: θ = (2/3)×360° = 240° = (2/3)×2π = 4π/3 radians. Arc distance of the tip: l = rθ = 1.5 × (4π/3) = 2π cm. Using π = 3.14: l = 2×3.14 = 6.28 cm.
Answer: distance moved = 6.28 cm.
For circular motion, use l = rθ with θ in radians. Convert to radians early to avoid slips.

Example 5: Same arc lengths subtend 65° and 110°; find ratio of radii

Let the radii be r1, r2. Arc length l is the same in both circles. Convert angles to radians:
  • θ1 = 65° = (π/180)×65 = 13π/36
  • θ2 = 110° = (π/180)×110 = 22π/36
Since l is same and l = rθ: r1θ1 = r2θ2 r1(13π/36) = r2(22π/36) Cancel π/36: 13r1 = 22r2 ⇒ r1/r2 = 22/13.
Answer: r1 : r2 = 22 : 13.
Equal arc lengths ⇒ radii are inversely proportional to angles (in radians): if l fixed, r = l/θ.

3.3 Trigonometric Functions

Trig for ANY angle (unit circle idea) Use a unit circle (radius 1) centred at origin O. Let P(a, b) be on the circle and ∠AOP = x radians, where A = (1, 0) on the +x-axis. Then:
  • cos x = a (x-coordinate of P)
  • sin x = b (y-coordinate of P)
Since OP = 1 and triangle OMP is right-angled: a² + b² = 1 ⟹ cos²x + sin²x = 1.
Pythagorean identity cos²x + sin²x = 1 cos x = x-coordinate on unit circle at angle x sin x = y-coordinate on unit circle at angle x

Quadrantal angles and standard values of sin and cos

Quadrantal angles: integral multiples of π/2 x = n(π/2), n ∈ Z. One full revolution = 2π radians. Key unit-circle points:
  • A at angle 0: (1, 0)
  • B at angle π/2: (0, 1)
  • C at angle π: (−1, 0)
  • D at angle 3π/2: (0, −1)
Exact values at quadrantal angles
  • cos 0 = 1, sin 0 = 0
  • cos(π/2) = 0, sin(π/2) = 1
  • cos π = −1, sin π = 0
  • cos(3π/2) = 0, sin(3π/2) = −1
  • cos(2π) = 1, sin(2π) = 0

Periodicity of sine and cosine

After one complete turn (2π radians), the terminal point comes back to the same spot ⇒ sin and cos repeat.
Periodicity (sine and cosine) sin(2nπ + x) = sin x, n ∈ Z cos(2nπ + x) = cos x, n ∈ Z n = any integer; Z = set of integers

Zeros of sin x and cos x

When does sin x vanish? sin x = 0 at multiples of π. When does cos x vanish? cos x = 0 at odd multiples of π/2.
Solutions for sin x = 0 sin x = 0 ⟹ x = nπ, n ∈ Z n = any integer
Solutions for cos x = 0 cos x = 0 ⟹ x = (2n + 1)π/2, n ∈ Z n = any integer

Other trigonometric functions (definitions + restrictions)

Other trig functions are built from sin x and cos x. Undefined when the denominator = 0.
cosec x cosec x = 1/sin x, defined only if sin x ≠ 0 ⟹ x ≠ nπ (n ∈ Z) x = angle in radians; n ∈ Z
sec x sec x = 1/cos x, defined only if cos x ≠ 0 ⟹ x ≠ (2n + 1)π/2 (n ∈ Z) x = angle in radians; n ∈ Z
tan x tan x = (sin x)/(cos x), defined only if cos x ≠ 0 ⟹ x ≠ (2n + 1)π/2 (n ∈ Z) x = angle in radians; n ∈ Z
cot x cot x = (cos x)/(sin x), defined only if sin x ≠ 0 ⟹ x ≠ nπ (n ∈ Z) x = angle in radians; n ∈ Z

Derived identities: 1 + tan²x = sec²x and 1 + cot²x = cosec²x

From sin²x + cos²x = 1:
  • Divide by cos²x (where cos x ≠ 0)
  • Divide by sin²x (where sin x ≠ 0)
Identity involving tan and sec 1 + tan²x = sec²x Valid where tan x and sec x are defined (i.e. cos x ≠ 0)
Identity involving cot and cosec 1 + cot²x = cosec²x Valid where cot x and cosec x are defined (i.e. sin x ≠ 0)

Standard values at key angles (0, π/6, π/4, π/3, π/2, π, 3π/2, 2π)

Same values as 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90° — now in radians + extended to π, 3π/2, 2π.
    0° π6 π4 π3 π2 π 3π 2 2π
    sin 0 12 12 32 1 0 −1 0
    cos 1 32 12 12 0 −1 0 1
    tan 0 13 1 3 Not
    defined    		 0 Not
    defined    		 0
Also: cosec x, sec x, cot x are reciprocals of sin x, cos x, tan x (where defined).
Exam tip: tan x is not defined at x = π/2 and x = 3π/2 since cos x = 0 there.

3.3.1 Sign of trigonometric functions

Even-odd properties from unit circle symmetry

If P(a, b) is at angle x, then the point at angle −x is Q(a, −b). So:
  • cos(−x) = cos x ⇒ cos is even
  • sin(−x) = −sin x ⇒ sin is odd
Parity (even/odd) identities cos(−x) = cos x sin(−x) = −sin x x = any real number

Bounds of sin x and cos x

On the unit circle: −1 ≤ a ≤ 1 and −1 ≤ b ≤ 1, hence:
  • −1 ≤ cos x ≤ 1
  • −1 ≤ sin x ≤ 1

Signs in different quadrants

Quadrant signs using P(a, b) on unit circle:
  • I (0 < x < π/2): a > 0, b > 0
  • II (π/2 < x < π): a < 0, b > 0
  • III (π < x < 3π/2): a < 0, b < 0
  • IV (3π/2 < x < 2π): a > 0, b < 0
So:
  • sin x: + for 0 < x < π, and − for π < x < 2π
  • cos x: + for 0 < x < π/2, − for π/2 < x < 3π/2, + for 3π/2 < x < 2π
Sign table (by quadrant)
  • I: sin +, cos +, tan +, cosec +, sec +, cot +
  • II: sin +, cos −, tan −, cosec +, sec −, cot −
  • III: sin −, cos −, tan +, cosec −, sec −, cot +
  • IV: sin −, cos +, tan −, cosec −, sec +, cot −
ASTC
A — All (I: all +)
S — Sine (II: sin, cosec +)
T — Tangent (III: tan, cot +)
C — Cosine (IV: cos, sec +)

3.3.2 Domain and range of trigonometric functions

Domain and range of sin x and cos x

sin x and cos x are defined for every real x.
  • Domain of y = sin x: R
  • Range of y = sin x: [−1, 1]
  • Domain of y = cos x: R
  • Range of y = cos x: [−1, 1]

Domain and range of cosec x and sec x

  • y = cosec x is undefined where sin x = 0 ⟹ x = nπ
  • Range of cosec x: y ≥ 1 or y ≤ −1
  • y = sec x is undefined where cos x = 0 ⟹ x = (2n+1)π/2
  • Range of sec x: y ≥ 1 or y ≤ −1
Domain and range: y = cosec x Domain = {x : x ∈ R and x ≠ nπ, n ∈ Z} Range = {y : y ∈ R, y ≥ 1 or y ≤ −1}
Domain and range: y = sec x Domain = {x : x ∈ R and x ≠ (2n + 1)π/2, n ∈ Z} Range = {y : y ∈ R, y ≥ 1 or y ≤ −1}

Domain and range of tan x and cot x

  • tan x undefined when cos x = 0 ⟹ x = (2n+1)π/2; its range is all real numbers
  • cot x undefined when sin x = 0 ⟹ x = nπ; its range is all real numbers
Domain and range: y = tan x Domain = {x : x ∈ R and x ≠ (2n + 1)π/2, n ∈ Z} Range = R
Domain and range: y = cot x Domain = {x : x ∈ R and x ≠ nπ, n ∈ Z} Range = R
Common mistake: writing range of sec/cosec as (−1,1). Correct range is (−∞, −1] ∪ [1, ∞) since they’re reciprocals of cos/sin (whose |value| ≤ 1).

Behaviour (increase/decrease) in each quadrant

Monotonic behaviour by quadrant (inside each open quadrant interval):
  • sin x: I increases 0→1; II decreases 1→0; III decreases 0→−1; IV increases −1→0
  • cos x: I decreases 1→0; II decreases 0→−1; III increases −1→0; IV increases 0→1
  • tan x: I increases 0→∞; II increases −∞→0; III increases 0→∞; IV increases −∞→0
  • cot x: I decreases ∞→0; II decreases 0→−∞; III decreases ∞→0; IV decreases 0→−∞
  • sec x: I increases 1→∞; II increases −∞→−1; III decreases −1→−∞; IV decreases ∞→1
  • cosec x: I decreases ∞→1; II increases 1→∞; III increases −∞→−1; IV decreases −1→−∞
What “→ ∞” and “→ −∞” mean here
  • “tan x increases from 0 to ∞ for 0 < x < π/2” = keeps increasing and gets arbitrarily large +ve as x → (π/2)−.
  • “cosec x decreases from −1 to −∞ in IV quadrant” = keeps decreasing (more −ve) and gets arbitrarily large in magnitude as x → (2π)−.
∞ and −∞ describe behaviour, not actual real-number outputs.

Periodicity and graphs (sketching idea)

  • sin x and cos x repeat every 2π, so their reciprocals also repeat every 2π:
    • sec x has period 2π
    • cosec x has period 2π
  • tan(π + x) = tan x (shown later), so:
    • tan x has period π
    • cot x (reciprocal of tan x) also has period π
Use these periods + the quadrant behaviour above to sketch all 6 trig graphs.

Example 6: Finding all trig functions given cos x and the quadrant

Given cos x = −3/5, x in the third quadrant

Use sin²x + cos²x = 1 to get sin x (±), then fix the sign using the quadrant. Then use reciprocals/ratios for the rest.
Step 1: sec x sec x = 1/cos x = 1/(−3/5) = −5/3. Step 2: sin x from Pythagorean identity sin²x = 1 − cos²x = 1 − (9/25) = 16/25. So sin x = ±4/5. Step 3: choose sign using quadrant In QIII, sin x is negative ⟹ sin x = −4/5. Step 4: cosec x cosec x = 1/sin x = 1/(−4/5) = −5/4.
Common mistake: taking the square root and forgetting to use the quadrant to pick the correct sign for sin x (or cos x).

Using One Trigonometric Function to Find the Other Five (with Quadrant)

Reciprocal + quotient links
  • tan x = (sin x)/(cos x)
  • cot x = (cos x)/(sin x)
  • sec x = 1/(cos x)
  • cosec x = 1/(sin x)
Example relation used: If tan x = 4/3, then cot x = 3/4 (reciprocals).
Quadrant decides signs of sin x, cos x, tan x (so also cosec, sec, cot). After finding a magnitude (e.g. |sec x|), attach the sign using the quadrant.

Example 7: Given cot x = −5/12, x in second quadrant; find other five functions

Given: cot x = −5/12, x is in QII. Step 1: Convert cot to tan
tan x = 1 / cot x
  • tan x = tangent of angle x
  • cot x = cotangent of angle x
So, tan x = 1/(−5/12) = −12/5. Step 2: Use identity to get sec x
sec² x = 1 + tan² x
  • sec x = secant of x
  • tan x = tangent of x
sec² x = 1 + (−12/5)² = 1 + 144/25 = 169/25 Hence sec x = ±13/5. Step 3: Choose sign using quadrant
In QII, cos x < 0 so sec x < 0.
Therefore, sec x = −13/5. Step 4: Find cos x
sec x = 1 / cos x (so cos x = 1 / sec x)
  • sec x = secant of x
  • cos x = cosine of x
cos x = 1/(−13/5) = −5/13. Step 5: Find sin x using tan x = sin x / cos x
tan x = (sin x)/(cos x) (so sin x = tan x · cos x)
  • tan x = tangent of x
  • sin x = sine of x
  • cos x = cosine of x
sin x = (−12/5) × (−5/13) = 12/13. Step 6: Find cosec x
cosec x = 1 / sin x
  • cosec x = cosecant of x
  • sin x = sine of x
cosec x = 1/(12/13) = 13/12. Step 7: Find remaining function cot x (already given) and/or verify
  • cot x = −5/12 (given)
  • tan x = −12/5 (found)
  • sec x = −13/5 (found)
  • cos x = −5/13 (found)
  • sin x = 12/13 (found)
  • cosec x = 13/12 (found)
Exam tip: If you get sec x = ±13/5 from sec² x, don’t stop—use the quadrant to choose the sign.

Periodicity (Repeating Values) to Evaluate Trigonometric Functions

Periodicity = trig functions repeat after fixed intervals.
  • sin(x + 2π) = sin x
  • cos(x + 2π) = cos x
  • In degrees: sin(θ + 360°) = sin θ, cos(θ + 360°) = cos θ

Example 8: Find sin(31π/3)

Idea: reduce the angle using multiples of 2π. Since 2π = 6π/3, subtract a suitable multiple:
  • 31π/3 = 30π/3 + π/3 = 10π + π/3
sin repeats every 2π, so sin(10π + π/3) = sin(π/3). Therefore, sin(31π/3) = sin(π/3) = √3/2.

Example 9: Find cos(−1710°)

Idea: reduce using cosine period 360°. Add 5 × 360° = 1800°:
  • −1710° + 1800° = 90°
So, cos(−1710°) = cos(90°) = 0.
Common mistake: reducing angles wrongly. You can add/subtract any integer multiple of 2π (or 360°) until you hit a standard angle.

3.4 Trigonometric Functions of Sum and Difference of Two Angles

Trigonometric identities = equations with trig functions that are true for all angles (where both sides make sense). Here: identities for sum & difference of two angles + related results.

Even–Odd Properties (recap used in proofs)

sin(−x) = −sin x
  • x = any angle
cos(−x) = cos x
  • x = any angle

Cosine of Sum: cos(x + y)

cos(x + y) = cos x cos y − sin x sin y
  • x, y = angles
  • sin, cos = usual trig functions
Geometric derivation outline (unit circle): take points on the unit circle:
  • P1(cos x, sin x)
  • P2(cos(x + y), sin(x + y))
  • P3(cos(−y), sin(−y)) = (cos y, −sin y)
  • P4(1, 0)
Triangles P1OP3 and P2OP4 are congruent ⇒ P1P3 = P2P4. Square + distance formula:
  • P1P32 = 2 − 2(cos x cos y − sin x sin y)
  • P2P4² = 2 − 2cos(x + y)
Set them equal ⇒ cos(x + y) = cos x cos y − sin x sin y.

Cosine of Difference: cos(x − y)

cos(x − y) = cos x cos y + sin x sin y
  • x, y = angles
Method: in cos(x + y), replace y with −y:
  • cos(x + (−y)) = cos x cos(−y) − sin x sin(−y)
  • use cos(−y)=cos y and sin(−y)=−sin y ⇒ the term becomes “+ sin x sin y”.

Related Shift Identities with π/2

cos( (π/2) − x ) = sin x
  • x = any angle
Derivation idea: put x → π/2 and y → x in cos(x − y).
sin( (π/2) − x ) = cos x
  • x = any angle
Derivation idea: use sin A = cos(π/2 − A) + the previous identity.

Sine of Sum and Difference

sin(x + y) = sin x cos y + cos x sin y
  • x, y = angles
Derivation idea used: sin(x + y) = cos(π/2 − (x + y)); then apply cosine difference with angles (π/2 − x) and y.
sin(x − y) = sin x cos y − cos x sin y
  • x, y = angles
Method: replace y by −y in sin(x + y).

Standard Transformations from Sum–Difference Identities

cos( (π/2) + x ) = − sin x
  • x = any angle
sin( (π/2) + x ) = cos x
  • x = any angle
cos(π − x) = − cos x
  • x = any angle
sin(π − x) = sin x
  • x = any angle
cos(π + x) = − cos x
  • x = any angle
sin(π + x) = − sin x
  • x = any angle
cos(2π − x) = cos x
  • x = any angle
sin(2π − x) = − sin x
  • x = any angle
Similar results for tan x, cot x, sec x, cosec x come from:
  • tan x = sin x / cos x
  • cot x = cos x / sin x
  • sec x = 1 / cos x
  • cosec x = 1 / sin x

Tangent and Cotangent of Sum and Difference

tan(x + y)

tan(x + y) = (tan x + tan y) / (1 − tan x tan y)
  • x, y = angles
Condition: none of x, y, (x + y) is an odd multiple of π/2 ⇒ cos x, cos y, cos(x + y) ≠ 0, so tan is defined.
Derivation idea: start with tan(x+y)=sin(x+y)/cos(x+y), plug in sum formulas, then divide top & bottom by cos x cos y to reach the tan form.

tan(x − y)

tan(x − y) = (tan x − tan y) / (1 + tan x tan y)
  • x, y = angles
Method: replace y by −y in tan(x + y).

cot(x + y)

cot(x + y) = (cot x cot y − 1) / (cot x + cot y)
  • x, y = angles
Condition: none of x, y, (x + y) is a multiple of π ⇒ sin x, sin y, sin(x + y) ≠ 0, so cot is defined.
Derivation idea: use cot(x+y)=cos(x+y)/sin(x+y), plug in sum formulas, then divide top & bottom by sin x sin y.

cot(x − y)

cot(x − y) = (cot x cot y + 1) / (cot y − cot x)
  • x, y = angles
Condition: none of x, y, (x − y) is a multiple of π.
Method: replace y by −y in cot(x + y).
Sign trap: tan(x+y) denominator is 1 − tan x tan y, but tan(x−y) denominator is 1 + tan x tan y.

Multiple-Angle Identities (2x and 3x)

Double-angle formulas for cosine

cos 2x = cos²x − sin²x
  • x = any angle
cos 2x = 2cos²x − 1
  • x = any angle
cos 2x = 1 − 2sin²x
  • x = any angle
cos 2x = (1 − tan²x)/(1 + tan²x)
  • x = any angle
  • Restriction: x ≠ π/2 + nπ (n ∈ ℤ), so tan x is defined (cos x ≠ 0)
Derivation path shown: start with cos(x+y), put y=x ⇒ cos2x = cos²x − sin²x; rewrite using sin²x + cos²x = 1; then divide top & bottom by cos²x to get the tan form.

Double-angle formulas for sine

sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x
  • x = any angle
sin 2x = (2 tan x)/(1 + tan²x)
  • x = any angle
  • Restriction: x ≠ π/2 + nπ (n ∈ ℤ), so tan x is defined
Derivation path shown: from sin(x+y), put y=x ⇒ sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x; then divide by cos²x to switch to tan.

Double-angle formula for tangent

tan 2x = (2 tan x)/(1 − tan²x)
  • x = any angle
  • Restriction: x ≠ π/2 + nπ (n ∈ ℤ) (tan x defined) and also denominator ≠ 0 (i.e., tan²x ≠ 1) so the final form is defined
Derivation path shown: use tan(x+y) and set y=x.

Triple-angle formulas for sine and cosine

sin 3x = 3 sin x − 4 sin³x
  • x = any angle
Derivation steps shown:
  • sin 3x = sin(2x + x)
  • = sin 2x cos x + cos 2x sin x
  • = (2 sin x cos x) cos x + (1 − 2sin²x) sin x
  • = 2 sin x cos²x + sin x − 2 sin³x
  • Use cos²x = 1 − sin²x:
  • 2 sin x(1 − sin²x) + sin x − 2 sin³x = 3 sin x − 4 sin³x
cos 3x = 4 cos³x − 3 cos x
  • x = any angle
Derivation steps shown:
  • cos 3x = cos(2x + x)
  • = cos 2x cos x − sin 2x sin x
  • = (2cos²x − 1)cos x − (2sin x cos x)sin x
  • = (2cos²x − 1)cos x − 2cos x sin²x
  • Use sin²x = 1 − cos²x:
  • = 2cos³x − cos x − 2cos x(1 − cos²x) = 4cos³x − 3cos x

Triple-angle formula for tangent

tan 3x = (3 tan x − tan³x)/(1 − 3 tan²x)
  • x = any angle
  • Restriction: x ≠ π/2 + nπ (n ∈ ℤ), so tan x is defined; also 1 − 3 tan²x ≠ 0 so the expression is defined
Derivation idea shown: tan 3x = tan(2x + x); use tan-sum + tan 2x = (2 tan x)/(1 − tan²x); simplify to reach the stated form.
Exam tip: know at least one standard form for each multiple-angle identity—esp. the compact “polynomial” ones: sin 3x = 3s − 4s³, cos 3x = 4c³ − 3c, tan 3x = (3t − t³)/(1 − 3t²) where s=sin x, c=cos x, t=tan x.

Sum-to-Product (and Product-to-Sum Setup) Identities

Core sum-to-product results

(i) cos x + cos y = 2 cos((x + y)/2) cos((x − y)/2)
  • x, y = angles
(ii) cos x − cos y = −2 sin((x + y)/2) sin((x − y)/2)
  • x, y = angles
(iii) sin x + sin y = 2 sin((x + y)/2) cos((x − y)/2)
  • x, y = angles
(iv) sin x − sin y = 2 cos((x + y)/2) sin((x − y)/2)
  • x, y = angles
Derivation method given:
  • Start from:
    • cos(x+y) = cos x cos y − sin x sin y … (1)
    • cos(x−y) = cos x cos y + sin x sin y … (2)
  • Add/subtract (1) and (2) to get:
    • cos(x+y) + cos(x−y) = 2 cos x cos y … (3)
    • cos(x+y) − cos(x−y) = −2 sin x sin y … (4)
  • Similarly from:
    • sin(x+y) = sin x cos y + cos x sin y … (5)
    • sin(x−y) = sin x cos y − cos x sin y … (6)
  • Add/subtract (5) and (6) to get:
    • sin(x+y) + sin(x−y) = 2 sin x cos y … (7)
    • sin(x+y) − sin(x−y) = 2 cos x sin y … (8)
  • Then let:
    • x + y = θ and x − y = φ
    • So x = (θ + φ)/2 and y = (θ − φ)/2
  • Sub θ, φ into (3), (4), (7), (8) to get the 4 sum-to-product formulas above (then rename θ, φ back to x, y).

Sum-to-Product Identities (Two Angles)

Turns sums/differences of sin/cos into products. Built from the standard angle sum/difference formulas + substitutions + algebra.
Cosine sum: cos x + cos y = 2 cos((x + y)/2) cos((x − y)/2) x, y: real angles (radians or degrees—just be consistent in a question)
Cosine difference: cos x − cos y = −2 sin((x + y)/2) sin((x − y)/2) x, y: real angles
Sine sum: sin x + sin y = 2 sin((x + y)/2) cos((x − y)/2) x, y: real angles
Sine difference: sin x − sin y = 2 cos((x + y)/2) sin((x − y)/2) x, y: real angles
In the textbook derivation, θ and φ are any real values ⇒ we can swap θ → x and φ → y, so the identities hold for all real x, y.

Product-to-Sum Results (Remark)

Just rearrangements of the sum-to-product identities (super common for simplifying trig products).
(i) 2 cos x cos y = cos(x + y) + cos(x − y) x, y: angles
(ii) −2 sin x sin y = cos(x + y) − cos(x − y) x, y: angles
(iii) 2 sin x cos y = sin(x + y) + sin(x − y) x, y: angles
(iv) 2 cos x sin y = sin(x + y) − sin(x − y) x, y: angles
Common sign slip: cos x − cos y has a minus out front: cos x − cos y = −2 sin((x + y)/2) sin((x − y)/2).

Worked Examples on Identities and Exact Values

Example 10: Evaluate an expression using standard values and a sine-difference idea

Target: prove 3 sin π6 sec π3 − 4 sin 5π6 cot π4 = 1
Use exact values: sin(π/6)=1/2, sec(π/3)=2, cot(π/4)=1. Be careful with sin(π/4): the book rewrites a sine term using sin(π/2 − θ)=cos θ (symmetry/complementary angle) to link to sin(π/6).
Solution We have L.H.S. = 3 sin π6 sec π3 − 4 sin 5π6 cot π4 = 3 × 12 × 2 − 4 sin ( π − π6 ) × 1 = 3 − 4 sin π6 = 3 − 4 × 12 = 1 = R.H.S.
⚠ If the solution uses a “hidden” rewrite (e.g. sin(π/2 − θ)=cos θ), write the identity first, then plug exact values.

Example 11: Exact value of sin 15°

Find sin 15° using sin(A − B).
sin(A − B) = sin A cos B − cos A sin B A, B: angles
sin 15° = sin(45° − 30°) = sin45° cos30° − cos45° sin30° = (1/√2)(√3/2) − (1/√2)(1/2) = (√3 − 1)/(2√2).
Exact form usually accepted: (√3 − 1)/(2√2). (Rationalise if required.)

Example 12: Exact value of tan(13π/12)

Use periodicity + tan addition: 13π/12 = π + π/12 ⇒ tan(13π/12) = tan(π/12).
tan(π + θ) = tan θ θ: angle
tan(A − B) = (tan A − tan B)/(1 + tan A tan B) A, B: angles where denominator ≠ 0
tan(13π/12) = tan(π + π/12) = tan(π/12). Now π/12 = π/4 − π/6, so tan(π/12) = (tan(π/4) − tan(π/6)) / (1 + tan(π/4)tan(π/6)) = (1 − 1/√3)/(1 + 1·1/√3) = ((√3 − 1)/√3)/((√3 + 1)/√3) = (√3 − 1)/(√3 + 1) = 2 − √3.
⚠ tan has period π: tan(π + θ)=tan θ. (sin/cos have period 2π.)

Example 13: Prove a sine ratio identity leading to tangent form

Prove: sin(x + y)/sin(x − y) = (tan x + tan y)/(tan x − tan y).
sin(x + y) = sin x cos y + cos x sin y sin(x − y) = sin x cos y − cos x sin y x, y: angles
LHS = sin(x + y)/sin(x − y) = (sin x cos y + cos x sin y)/(sin x cos y − cos x sin y). Divide top + bottom by cos x cos y: = (tan x + tan y)/(tan x − tan y) = RHS.
Dividing by cos x cos y assumes cos x ≠ 0 and cos y ≠ 0. If cos x=0 or cos y=0, check separately (often both sides are undefined, or it still holds where defined).

Example 14: Prove tan3x·tan2x·tanx identity

Show: tan 3x tan 2x tan x = tan 3x − tan 2x − tan x.
tan(A + B) = (tan A + tan B)/(1 − tan A tan B) A, B: angles where denominator ≠ 0
Since 3x = 2x + x, tan 3x = tan(2x + x) = (tan2x + tan x)/(1 − tan2x·tan x). Multiply both sides by (1 − tan2x·tan x): tan3x − tan3x·tan2x·tan x = tan2x + tan x. Rearrange: tan3x − tan2x − tan x = tan3x·tan2x·tan x. Hence tan3x·tan2x·tan x = tan3x − tan2x − tan x.
⚠ Rearranging is where sign mistakes happen—track the product term tan3x·tan2x·tanx carefully.

Example 15: Prove a cosine sum leading to $\sqrt{2}$cosx

Prove: cos(x + π/4) + cos(x − π/4) = $\sqrt{2}$ cos x.
cos α + cos β = 2 cos((α + β)/2) cos((α − β)/2) α, β: angles
Let α = x + π/4 and β = x − π/4. Then cos(x + π/4) + cos(x − π/4) = 2 cos(((x + π/4) + (x − π/4))/2) · cos(((x + π/4) − (x − π/4))/2) = 2 cos((2x)/2) · cos((π/2)/2) = 2 cos x · cos(π/4) = 2 cos x · (1/√2). The textbook’s intended simplification gives $\sqrt{2}$cos x as RHS (using the chapter’s established identity structure/notation). Hence LHS = RHS.

Example 16: Prove a cotangent expression using sum-to-product

Prove: (cos 7x + cos 5x)/(sin 7x − sin 5x) = cot x.
cos a + cos b = 2 cos((a + b)/2) cos((a − b)/2) sin a − sin b = 2 cos((a + b)/2) sin((a − b)/2) a, b: angles
Numerator: cos7x + cos5x = 2 cos(6x) cos(x). Denominator: sin7x − sin5x = 2 cos(6x) sin(x). So (cos7x + cos5x)/(sin7x − sin5x) = [2 cos6x cos x]/[2 cos6x sin x] = cos x/sin x = cot x.
⚠ Cancel 2cos6x only after noting it might be 0; the identity holds where the original expression is defined.

Example 17: Prove a tangent result from a sine-cosine rational expression

Prove: (sin 5x − 2 sin 3x + sin x)/(cos 5x − cos x) = tan x.
Technique: regroup → use sum-to-product → reduce to sin x / cos x.
Start with LHS: (sin5x − 2sin3x + sin x)/(cos5x − cos x) = [(sin5x + sin x) − 2sin3x]/(cos5x − cos x). Use sin5x + sin x = 2 sin3x cos2x: Numerator = 2sin3x cos2x − 2sin3x = 2sin3x(cos2x − 1). Now cos2x − 1 = −2sin^2 x, so numerator = 2sin3x(−2sin^2 x) = −4 sin3x sin^2 x. Also cos5x − cos x = −2 sin3x sin2x (from cos a − cos b identity), so LHS = [−4 sin3x sin^2 x]/[−2 sin3x sin2x] = [2 sin^2 x]/[sin2x]. Since sin2x = 2 sin x cos x, LHS = (2 sin^2 x)/(2 sin x cos x) = sin x/cos x = tan x = RHS.
⚠ Key substitutions: sin5x + sin x = 2sin3x cos2x and cos5x − cos x = −2sin3x sin2x.

Miscellaneous Examples (Applications & Quadrant Logic)

Example 18: Given sin x and cos y in quadrant II, find sin(x + y)

If sin x = 3/5 and cos y = −12/13, where x and y are in quadrant II, find sin(x + y).
sin(x + y) = sin x cos y + cos x sin y x, y: angles
Given sin x = 3/5. cos²x = 1 − sin²x = 1 − 9/25 = 16/25 ⟹ cos x = ±4/5. Quadrant II ⟹ cos x < 0 ⟹ cos x = −4/5. Given cos y = −12/13. sin²y = 1 − cos²y = 1 − 144/169 = 25/169 ⟹ sin y = ±5/13. Quadrant II ⟹ sin y > 0 ⟹ sin y = 5/13. Now sin(x + y) = (3/5)(−12/13) + (−4/5)(5/13) = −36/65 − 20/65 = −56/65.
Quadrant II signs: sin +, cos −, tan −.

Example 19: Prove an identity involving cos products and sin products

Prove: cos 2x cos x2 cos 3x cos 9x2 = sin 5x sin 5x2.
L.H.S. = 1 2 [ 2 cos 2x cos x 2 − 2 cos 9x 2 cos 3x ] = 1 2 [ cos ( 2x + x 2 ) + cos ( 2x − x 2 ) − cos ( 9x 2 + 3x ) − cos ( 9x 2 − 3x ) ] = 1 2 [ cos 5x 2 + cos 3x 2 − cos 15x 2 − cos 3x 2 ] = 1 2 [ cos 5x 2 − cos 15x 2 ] = 1 2 [ − 2 sin { 5x 2 + 15x 2 2 } sin { 5x 2 − 15x 2 2 } ] = − sin 5x sin ( − 5x 2 ) = sin 5x sin 5x 2 = R.H.S.
Long trig proofs: first push everything into one “family” (all cos or all sin). Convert to products right at the end.

Example 20: Find tan(π/8)

Use the tangent double-angle formula with x = π/8 so that 2x = π/4.
tan 2x = (2 tan x)/(1 − tan²x) x: angle where denominator ≠ 0
Let x = π/8 ⟹ 2x = π/4 and tan(π/4)=1. Then 1 = tan(π/4) = tan(2x) = (2 tan x)/(1 − tan²x). Let y = tan x = tan(π/8). Then 1 = 2y/(1 − y²) ⟹ 1 − y² = 2y ⟹ y² + 2y − 1 = 0. So y = [−2 ± √(4 + 4)]/2 = −1 ± √2. Quadrant I ⟹ tan(π/8) > 0 ⟹ y = −1 + √2 = √2 − 1.
tan(π/8) = √2 − 1.

Example 21: Given tan x and range of x, find sin(x/2), cos(x/2), tan(x/2)

If tan x = 3/4 and π < x < 3π/2, find sin(x/2), cos(x/2), tan(x/2).
x in quadrant III ⟹ sin x < 0, cos x < 0, tan x > 0. Also x/2 is in quadrant II ⟹ sin(x/2) > 0 and cos(x/2) < 0.
sec²x = 1 + tan²x x: angle
sec²x = 1 + (3/4)² = 1 + 9/16 = 25/16. So cos²x = 1/sec²x = 16/25 ⟹ cos x = ±4/5. Quadrant III ⟹ cos x < 0 ⟹ cos x = −4/5. (This is the book’s “Why?”)
sin²x + cos²x = 1 x: angle
sin²x = 1 − cos²x = 1 − 16/25 = 9/25.
sin²(x/2) = (1 − cos x)/2 cos²(x/2) = (1 + cos x)/2 x: angle
sin²(x/2) = (1 − (−4/5))/2 = (1 + 4/5)/2 = (9/5)/2 = 9/10. So sin(x/2) = +3/√10 (positive in quadrant II). (This is the book’s “Why?”) cos²(x/2) = (1 + (−4/5))/2 = (1/5)/2 = 1/10. So cos(x/2) = −1/√10 (negative in quadrant II). (This is the book’s “Why?”) tan(x/2) = sin(x/2)/cos(x/2) = (3/√10)/(−1/√10) = −3.
Half-angle signs come from the quadrant of x/2 (not x). Always place x/2 first using the given range.

Example 22: Prove a cosine-square sum identity with phase shifts

Prove that cosx 2 + cos ( x + π3 ) 2 + cos ( x − π3 ) 2 = 32 . (As per the given textbook expression involving squares; the book’s method is to expand cos^2 terms and simplify using cosine sum identities.)
Book idea: rewrite each cos² as (1 + cos(2·angle))/2, then add + simplify using cos(A+B)+cos(A−B)=2cosAcosB and cos(π)=−1.
cos²t = (1 + cos 2t)/2 t: angle
Following the textbook line:
  • Turn each squared cosine into: (constant) + (cos of double-angle).
  • Group: cos(2x + 2π/3) + cos(2x − 2π/3) = 2cos(2x)cos(2π/3).
  • Use cos(2π/3)=cos(π − π/3)=−cos(π/3)=−1/2, then simplify to reach the RHS stated in the text.
See cos² in a proof? Try cos²t = (1 + cos 2t)/2 first (drops the power fast).

Standard Trigonometric Identities (Addition, Subtraction, Multiple Angles)

Co-function and allied angle identities

Co-function (complementary angle) sin(π/2 − x) = cos x x: angle (in radians)
Shift by π/2 (quadrantal angles) cos(π/2 + x) = − sin x sin(π/2 + x) = cos x x: angle (in radians)
Allied angles with π and 2π cos(π − x) = − cos x sin(π − x) = sin x cos(π + x) = − cos x sin(π + x) = − sin x cos(2π − x) = cos x sin(2π − x) = − sin x x: angle (in radians)
Unit-circle shortcut:
  • (π − x) is in QII → sin +, cos −
  • (π + x) is in QIII → sin −, cos −
  • (2π − x) is in QIV → cos +, sin −

Sine addition and subtraction formulas

Sine of sum sin(x + y) = sin x cos y + cos x sin y x, y: angles
Sine of difference sin(x − y) = sin x cos y − cos x sin y x, y: angles
For sin(x ± y): the middle sign matches the sign between the two product terms. Sum → “+ +” ; Difference → “− −”.

Tangent addition and subtraction (with restrictions)

Why restrictions matter:
  • tan θ undefined when cos θ = 0 → θ is an odd multiple of π/2
  • Also, tan sum/diff has denominators (1 ∓ tan x tan y) → invalid if denominator = 0
Tangent of sum If none of x, y, (x + y) is an odd multiple of π/2, then tan(x + y) = (tan x + tan y)/(1 − tan x tan y) x, y: angles
Tangent of difference If none of x, y, (x − y) is an odd multiple of π/2, then tan(x − y) = (tan x − tan y)/(1 + tan x tan y) x, y: angles
Classic sign trap (tan): denominator uses the opposite sign. “+ upstairs → − downstairs” and “− upstairs → + downstairs”.

Cotangent addition and subtraction (with restrictions)

Why restrictions differ from tan:
  • cot θ undefined when sin θ = 0 → θ is a multiple of π
  • So require: none of x, y, (x ± y) is a multiple of π
Cotangent of sum If none of x, y, (x + y) is a multiple of π, then cot(x + y) = (cot x cot y − 1)/(cot x + cot y) x, y: angles
Cotangent of difference If none of x, y, (x − y) is a multiple of π, then cot(x − y) = (cot x cot y + 1)/(cot y − cot x) x, y: angles
For cot(x − y): denominator order matters → (cot y − cot x), NOT (cot x − cot y).

Double-angle identities

Cosine double-angle (all equivalent forms) cos 2x = cos²x − sin²x = 2cos²x − 1 = 1 − 2sin²x = (1 − tan²x)/(1 + tan²x) x: angle
Pick the cos 2x form based on what you’re given:
  • Given sin x → cos 2x = 1 − 2sin²x
  • Given cos x → cos 2x = 2cos²x − 1
  • Given tan x → cos 2x = (1 − tan²x)/(1 + tan²x)
Sine double-angle sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x = (2 tan x)/(1 + tan²x) x: angle
Tangent double-angle tan 2x = (2 tan x)/(1 − tan²x) x: angle
tan 2x blows up when 1 − tan²x = 0 → tan x = ±1. This matches 2x = π/2 + kπ where tan 2x is undefined.

Triple-angle identities

Sine triple-angle sin 3x = 3 sin x − 4 sin³x x: angle
Cosine triple-angle cos 3x = 4 cos³x − 3 cos x x: angle
Tangent triple-angle tan 3x = (3 tan x − tan³x)/(1 − 3 tan²x) x: angle
For tan 3x, track the pattern: (3t − t³)/(1 − 3t²). Common error: flipping the sign in the denominator.

Sum-to-Product and Product-to-Sum Transformations

Sum-to-product identities

(i) cos x + cos y cos x + cos y = 2 cos((x + y)/2) cos((x − y)/2) x, y: angles
(ii) cos x − cos y cos x − cos y = −2 sin((x + y)/2) sin((x − y)/2) x, y: angles
(iii) sin x + sin y sin x + sin y = 2 sin((x + y)/2) cos((x − y)/2) x, y: angles
(iv) sin x − sin y sin x − sin y = 2 cos((x + y)/2) sin((x − y)/2) x, y: angles
Sum-to-product pattern spotting:
  • cos ± cos → (cos·cos) or (sin·sin); the “−” case has a leading minus
  • sin ± sin → (sin·cos) or (cos·sin); no leading minus
  • Angles become half-sum (x + y)/2 and half-diff (x − y)/2

Product-to-sum identities

(i) Product of cosines 2 cos x cos y = cos(x + y) + cos(x − y) x, y: angles
(ii) Product of sines −2 sin x sin y = cos(x + y) − cos(x − y) x, y: angles
(iii) sin x cos y 2 sin x cos y = sin(x + y) + sin(x − y) x, y: angles
(iv) cos x sin y 2 cos x sin y = sin(x + y) − sin(x − y) x, y: angles
Don’t mix these:
  • 2 sin x cos y → sin(x + y) + sin(x − y)
  • 2 cos x sin y → sin(x + y) − sin(x − y)

Historical Notes and Applications (Height & Distance)

Historical development of trigonometry (India and beyond)

Trigonometry study first started in India. Key ancient Indian mathematicians:
  • Aryabhatta (476)
  • Brahmagupta (598)
  • Bhaskara I (600)
  • Bhaskara II (1114)
Knowledge travelled India → middle-east → Europe. Greeks had begun trig too, but their method was seen as clumsy; once the Indian approach became known, it was widely adopted.
The forerunner of modern trig functions was the “sine of an angle.” Introducing sine is described as the main contribution of the siddhantas (Sanskrit astronomical works) to math history.
More historical points:
  • Bhaskara I (about 600) gave formulae to find sine values for angles > 90°.
  • 16th-century Malayalam work Yuktibhasa gives a proof for expansion of sin(A + B).
  • Exact expressions for sin/cos of 18°, 36°, 54°, 72°, etc., are given by Bhaskara II.

Notation for inverse trigonometric functions

The symbols sin−1x, cos−1x, etc. (for arc sin x, arc cos x, etc.) were suggested by astronomer Sir John F. W. Hersehel (1813).
sin−1x means “inverse sine (arcsin)”, NOT 1/sin x. (Reciprocal is cosec x.)

Thales and height–distance problems (similar triangles)

Thales (about 600 B.C.) is closely linked with height & distance problems. He’s credited with finding the height of a great Egyptian pyramid by comparing shadows of the pyramid and a staff/gnomon of known height, using proportionality.
Shadow proportion + tangent link H/S = h/s = tan(sun’s altitude) H: height of pyramid (or tall object) S: length of pyramid’s shadow h: height of staff/gnomon (known) s: length of staff’s shadow
Reason: sun’s rays make the same angle of elevation with the ground for both objects ⇒ the “height vs shadow” triangles are similar. So height/shadow is constant = tan(altitude).
Thales is also said to have found the distance of a ship at sea using proportional sides of similar triangles. Similarity-based height–distance problems also appear in ancient Indian works.
