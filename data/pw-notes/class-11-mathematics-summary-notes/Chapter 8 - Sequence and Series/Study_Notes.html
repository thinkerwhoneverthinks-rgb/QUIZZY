Ch-08 : Sequence and Series

8.1 Introduction

Sequence (idea): An ordered list of objects/numbers with a clear 1st term, 2nd term, 3rd term, …
  • Real-life examples: population at different times; money deposited over years; depreciated values of a commodity.
Progressions: Sequences that follow a fixed pattern are called progressions.
  • This chapter builds on Arithmetic Progression (A.P.) (done earlier) and also covers: arithmetic mean, geometric mean, relation between A.M. and G.M., and special sums (consecutive natural numbers, squares, cubes).

8.2 Sequences

Meaning of a sequence; terms; nth term

Example (ancestors): If generation gap = 30 years, then over 300 years:
  • Total generations = \(\dfrac{300}{30} = 10\).
  • Ancestors in 1st, 2nd, 3rd, …, 10th generations: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, …, 1024.
  • This ordered list forms a sequence.
Example (successive quotients in division): Dividing 10 by 3 step-by-step gives successive quotients:
  • 3, 3.3, 3.33, 3.333, … (goes on forever) → also a sequence.
Terms of a sequence: The numbers in the sequence are called its terms. We write them as a1, a2, a3, …, an, … where the subscript = position. nth term / general term: The term in the nth position is the nth term, written an. (Also called the general term.)
  • Ancestors sequence: a1=2, a2=4, a3=8, …, a10=1024.
  • Successive quotients: a1=3, a2=3.3, a3=3.33, …, a6=3.33333, etc.

Finite and infinite sequences

  • Finite sequence: Has a fixed (limited) number of terms. Example: ancestors sequence has 10 terms → finite.
  • Infinite sequence: Does not end. Example: 3, 3.3, 3.33, 3.333, … → infinite.

Describing sequences by formulas and rules

Often, we can generate terms using an algebraic formula. Even natural numbers: 2, 4, 6, 8, …
  • a1=2=2×1
  • a2=4=2×2
  • a3=6=2×3
  • a4=8=2×4
  • … a23=46=2×23, a24=48=2×24, …
Even numbers (nth term): an = 2n
  • an = nth term
  • n = natural number (position)
Odd natural numbers: 1, 3, 5, …
Odd numbers (nth term): an = 2n − 1
  • an = nth term
  • n = natural number

Recurrence relations: Fibonacci sequence

Some sequences aren’t best described by a direct formula; instead we use a recurrence rule.
Fibonacci sequence: Given by starting terms + a recurrence relation:
Fibonacci recurrence: a1 = a2 = 1 a3 = a1 + a2 an = an−2 + an−1, for n > 2
  • an = nth term
  • n > 2 → rule applies from the 3rd term onwards

When no nth-term formula exists; sequences as functions

Prime numbers sequence: 2, 3, 5, 7, … has no known nth-term formula (at this level); we may describe it in words.
Not every sequence has a neat explicit algebraic formula. But there must be a rule/scheme to generate a1, a2, a3, … in order (explicit formula, recurrence relation, or verbal rule).
Sequence as a function: Think of a sequence as a function with domain = natural numbers (or part of them).
  • Sometimes write a(n) instead of an.

8.3 Series

Definition of a series and sigma notation

If a1, a2, a3, …, an, … is a sequence, then a1 + a2 + a3 + … + an + … is the series associated with that sequence.
  • Series is finite if the sequence is finite; infinite if the sequence is infinite.
Sigma notation (short summation): a1 + a2 + … + an = ∑k=1n ak
  • ∑ = sigma (summation sign)
  • k = index of summation
  • 1 = starting value of k
  • n = final value of k
  • ak = kth term being added
Remark (exam wording): “The series” means the written sum (the expression), not the final total.
  • Example: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 is a finite series with four terms.
  • Sum of the series = after adding: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16.

Example 1: Finding initial terms from a formula

Question: Write the first three terms of each sequence:
  • (i) an = 2n + 5
  • (ii) \(a_n = \dfrac{n-3}{4}\)
Solution (i): Put n = 1, 2, 3.
  • a1 = 2(1) + 5 = 7
  • a2 = 2(2) + 5 = 9
  • a3 = 2(3) + 5 = 11
First three terms: 7, 9, 11. Solution (ii): \(a_n = \dfrac{n-3}{4}\). Put n = 1, 2, 3.
  • \(a_1 = \dfrac{1-3}{4} = \dfrac{-2}{4} = -\dfrac{1}{2}\)
  • \(a_2 = \dfrac{2-3}{4} = -\dfrac{1}{4}\)
  • \(a_3 = \dfrac{3-3}{4} = 0\)
First three terms: \(-\dfrac{1}{2}, -\dfrac{1}{4}, 0\).
Tip: For “first few terms”, substitute n = 1, 2, 3, … carefully, and simplify signs/fractions fully.

Example 2: Finding a specific term

Question: Find the 20th term when an = (n − 1)(2 − n)(3 + n). Solution: Put n = 20.
  • a20 = (20 − 1)(2 − 20)(3 + 20)
  • = 19 × (−18) × 23
  • = −7866
Common mistake: Dropping the negative sign from (2 − n). Evaluate each bracket first, then multiply.

Example 3: Sequence given by recurrence; writing the series

Question: a1 = 1, and an = an−1 + 2 for n ≥ 2. Find first five terms and write the corresponding series. Solution: Build term-by-term.
  • a1 = 1
  • a2 = a1 + 2 = 1 + 2 = 3
  • a3 = a2 + 2 = 3 + 2 = 5
  • a4 = a3 + 2 = 5 + 2 = 7
  • a5 = a4 + 2 = 7 + 2 = 9
First five terms: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. Corresponding series: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + …
If a sequence is recursive, you must find terms in order: a1 → a2 → a3 → …

8.4 Geometric Progression (G.P.)

Recognising constant ratio

Look at these sequences:
  • (i) 2, 4, 8, 16, …
  • (ii) \(-1, \dfrac{1}{3}, -\dfrac{1}{9}, \dfrac{1}{27}, -\dfrac{1}{81}, \dfrac{1}{243}, \ldots\)
  • (iii) 0.1, 0.001, 0.00001, …
How terms move: compare each term with the one just before it.
  • In (i): \(\dfrac{a_2}{a_1} = 2, \dfrac{a_3}{a_2} = 2, \dfrac{a_4}{a_3} = 2, \ldots\) ⇒ constant ratio = 2.
  • In (ii): \(\dfrac{a_2}{a_1} = -\dfrac{1}{3}, \dfrac{a_3}{a_2} = -\dfrac{1}{3}, \dfrac{a_4}{a_3} = -\dfrac{1}{3}, \ldots\) ⇒ constant ratio = \(-\dfrac{1}{3}\).
  • In (iii): each term = (previous term) × 0.01 ⇒ constant ratio = 0.01.
Geometric progression (G.P.) / geometric sequence: a sequence a1, a2, a3, … is a G.P. when each term is non-zero and the ratio (next ÷ previous) stays constant.
Common ratio definition: \(\dfrac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} = r\) (constant), for k ≥ 1
  • ak = kth term
  • ak+1 = next term
  • r = common ratio (same throughout)
  • k ≥ 1 = start checking from 1st → 2nd onward

Standard form of a G.P.

If a1 = a, then the G.P. looks like: a, ar, ar2, ar3, …
  • a = first term
  • r = common ratio
Common ratios for the examples:
  • (i) r = 2
  • (ii) \(r = -\dfrac{1}{3}\)
  • (iii) r = 0.01
Exam caution: G.P. definition needs every term non-zero, so \(\dfrac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\) is always defined.

Notations for Geometric Progression (G.P.) Formulae

Standard symbols you’ll see in G.P. formulae:
  • a = first term
  • r = common ratio
  • l = last term
  • n = number of terms
  • Sn = sum of first n terms

8.4.1 General Term of a G.P.

A sequence is a G.P. if every term after the first is found by multiplying the previous term by a fixed non-zero number r (the common ratio).
For a G.P. with first non-zero term a and common ratio r:
  • 2nd term: a2 = ar
  • 3rd term: a3 = a2r = ar2
  • 4th term: a4 = ar3
nth term of a G.P.: an = arn−1
  • an = nth term
  • a = first term
  • r = common ratio
  • n = term number (positive integer)
So a G.P. can be written as:
  • Finite (n terms): a, ar, ar2, ar3, …, arn−1
  • Infinite: a, ar, ar2, ar3, … (goes on forever)
Geometric series:
  • Finite geometric series: a + ar + ar2 + … + arn−1
  • Infinite geometric series: a + ar + ar2 + … (no last term)
Quick pattern for terms: 1st = ar0, 2nd = ar1, …, nth = arn−1. So 16th term = ar15.

Example 4: 10th and nth terms

G.P. 5, 25, 125, … so a = 5, r = 5.
  • a10 = 5·59 = 510
  • an = 5·5n−1 = 5n

Example 5: Identify which term equals a given value

G.P. 2, 8, 32, … so a = 2, r = 4. Let 131072 be the nth term. Use an = arn−1:
  • 131072 = 2·4n−1 ⟹ 65536 = 4n−1
  • Since 65536 = 48, n − 1 = 8 ⟹ n = 9
So 131072 is the 9th term.

Example 6: Find r and a from two given terms

Given: 3rd term = 24 and 6th term = 192. Using an = arn−1:
  • a3 = ar2 = 24 … (1)
  • a6 = ar5 = 192 … (2)
Divide (2) by (1): \(r^3 = \dfrac{192}{24} = 8 \Rightarrow r = 2\). Sub into (1): a·22 = 24 ⟹ a = 6. 10th term: a10 = ar9 = 6·29 = 3072.
Common exam slip: when dividing terms, subtract powers properly: ar5 ÷ ar2 = r3 (NOT r7 or r2).

8.4.2 Sum to n Terms of a G.P.

Let first term = a, common ratio = r. Sum of first n terms: Sn = a + ar + ar2 + … + arn−1 … (1)

Case 1: r = 1

If r = 1, every term is a, so: Sn = na
  • Sn = sum of first n terms
  • n = number of terms
  • a = first term

Case 2: r ≠ 1

Multiply (1) by r: rSn = ar + ar2 + ar3 + … + arn … (2) Subtract (2) from (1): (1 − r)Sn = a − arn = a(1 − rn)
Sum of first n terms of a G.P. (r ≠ 1): \(S_n = \dfrac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}\)
  • Sn = sum of first n terms
  • a = first term
  • r = common ratio (r ≠ 1)
  • n = number of terms
Equivalent form: \(S_n = \dfrac{a(r^n - 1)}{r - 1}\). Same answer — pick the one that avoids sign slips.

Example 7: Sum to n terms and sum of first 5 terms

G.P.: \(1 + \dfrac{2}{3} + \dfrac{4}{9} + \ldots\)
  • a = 1
  • \(r = \dfrac{2}{3}\)
Use \(S_n = \dfrac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}\): \(S_n = \dfrac{1 - (2/3)^n}{1 - 2/3} = 3\left[1 - \left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^n\right]\) So: \(S_n = 3 - 3\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^n\) For n = 5: \(S_5 = 3\left[1 - \left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^5\right] = 3\left[1 - \dfrac{32}{243}\right] = 3\cdot\dfrac{211}{243} = \dfrac{211}{81}\)

Example 8: Find number of terms from a given sum

G.P.: \(3, \dfrac{3}{2}, \dfrac{3}{4}, \ldots\) so \(a = 3, r = \dfrac{1}{2}\). Given \(S_n = \dfrac{3069}{512}\). Use \(S_n = \dfrac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}\): \(S_n = \dfrac{3(1 - (1/2)^n)}{1 - 1/2} = 6(1 - (1/2)^n)\) Set equal to \(\dfrac{3069}{512}\):
  • \(6(1 - (1/2)^n) = \dfrac{3069}{512}\)
  • \(1 - (1/2)^n = \dfrac{3069}{3072}\)
  • \((1/2)^n = 1 - \dfrac{3069}{3072} = \dfrac{3}{3072} = \dfrac{1}{1024}\)
  • 2n = 1024 = 210 ⟹ n = 10

Example 9: Use sum and product of first three terms to find r and the terms

Let first three terms be: \(\dfrac{a}{r}, a, ar\). Given:
  • \(\dfrac{a}{r} + a + ar = \dfrac{13}{12}\) … (1)
  • \(\dfrac{a}{r}\cdot a\cdot(ar) = -1\) … (2)
From (2): a3 = −1 ⟹ a = −1 (real root only). Sub into (1): \(-\dfrac{1}{r} - 1 - r = \dfrac{13}{12}\) Multiply by 12r, simplify → quadratic: 12r2 + 25r + 12 = 0 Solve: \(r = -\dfrac{3}{4}\) or \(r = -\dfrac{4}{3}\). So the terms:
  • If \(r = -\dfrac{3}{4}\): \(\dfrac{a}{r} = \dfrac{-1}{-3/4} = \dfrac{4}{3}\), \(a = -1\), \(ar = (-1)(-3/4) = \dfrac{3}{4}\)
  • If \(r = -\dfrac{4}{3}\): \(\dfrac{a}{r} = \dfrac{-1}{-4/3} = \dfrac{3}{4}\), \(a = -1\), \(ar = (-1)(-4/3) = \dfrac{4}{3}\)
In \(\dfrac{a}{r}, a, (ar)\): product is \(\dfrac{a}{r}\cdot a\cdot(ar) = a^3\) (the r cancels!). Don’t wrongly keep an r factor.

Example 10: Sum of 7, 77, 777, … to n terms

This is not a G.P. directly — rewrite using powers of 10, then link to a G.P.
Let: Sn = 7 + 77 + 777 + … (n terms) Rewrite terms: \(7 = 7\cdot\dfrac{9}{9}, 77 = 7\cdot\dfrac{99}{9}, 777 = 7\cdot\dfrac{999}{9}, \ldots\) So: \(S_n = \dfrac{7[9 + 99 + 999 + \ldots \text{(n terms)}]}{9}\) And 9 = 10 − 1, 99 = 102 − 1, …, nth term = 10n − 1: \(S_n = \dfrac{7}{9}\left[(10-1) + (10^2-1) + \ldots + (10^n-1)\right]\) \(= \dfrac{7}{9}\left[(10 + 10^2 + \ldots + 10^n) - (1 + 1 + \ldots + 1)\right]\) (1 + 1 + … + 1) has n terms ⇒ equals n. (10 + 102 + … + 10n) is a G.P. with a = 10, r = 10, n terms: Sum \(= \dfrac{10(10^n - 1)}{10 - 1} = \dfrac{10(10^n - 1)}{9}\) Therefore:
Sum of 7, 77, 777, … (n terms): \(S_n = \dfrac{7}{9}\left[\dfrac{10(10^n - 1)}{9} - n\right] = \dfrac{7}{81}\left[10(10^n - 1) - 9n\right]\)
  • Sn = sum of first n terms of 7, 77, 777, …
  • n = number of terms

Example 11: Ancestors over 10 generations

Ancestors per generation: 2, 4, 8, … is a G.P.
  • a = 2, r = 2, n = 10
Sum: \(S_{10} = \dfrac{a(r^{10} - 1)}{r - 1} = 2(2^{10} - 1) = 2(1024 - 1) = 2046\) So ancestors in the ten preceding generations = 2046.

8.4.3 Geometric Mean (G.M.)

Geometric mean (G.M.) of two positive numbers a and b: G = √(ab)
Example: G.M. of 2 and 8 = √(16) = 4, and 2, 4, 8 are consecutive terms of a G.P.

Inserting n geometric means between two positive numbers

Given positive a and b. Insert n numbers G1, G2, …, Gn so that: a, G1, G2, …, Gn, b is a G.P. b is the (n+2)th term, so: \(b = ar^{n+1} \Rightarrow r = (b/a)^{1/(n+1)}\)
Common ratio when inserting n G.M.s: \(r = (b/a)^{1/(n+1)}\)
  • a, b = given positive numbers (end terms)
  • n = number of G.M.s inserted
  • r = common ratio of the new G.P.
Inserted terms:
  • \(G_1 = ar = a(b/a)^{1/(n+1)}\)
  • \(G_2 = ar^2 = a(b/a)^{2/(n+1)}\)
  • …
  • \(G_n = ar^n = a(b/a)^{n/(n+1)}\)

Example 12: Insert three numbers between 1 and 256 to form a G.P.

Need: 1, G1, G2, G3, 256 in G.P. a = 1 and 256 is the 5th term, so: 256 = ar4 = r4 ⟹ r = ±4 (real roots only) If r = 4:
  • G1 = ar = 4
  • G2 = ar2 = 16
  • G3 = ar3 = 64
If r = −4:
  • G1 = −4, G2 = 16, G3 = −64
So one valid insertion is 4, 16, 64 (also: −4, 16, −64).
“Insert n numbers between a and b”: total terms = n+2, so b = arn+1. Count terms carefully.

8.5 Relationship Between A.M. and G.M.

For two positive real numbers a and b:
  • A.M.: \(A = \dfrac{a+b}{2}\)
  • G.M.: G = √(ab)
Consider: \(A - G = \dfrac{a+b}{2} - \sqrt{ab}\) \(= \dfrac{a + b - 2\sqrt{ab}}{2}\) \(= \dfrac{(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b})^2}{2} \ge 0\)
A.M.–G.M. inequality (positive reals): A ≥ G, with equality iff a = b.

Example 13: Given A.M. and G.M., find the numbers

Given A = 10 and G = 8 for positive a and b. So:
  • \(\dfrac{a+b}{2} = 10 \Rightarrow a + b = 20\) … (3)
  • √(ab) = 8 ⟹ ab = 64 … (4)
Use: (a − b)2 = (a + b)2 − 4ab = 202 − 4·64 = 400 − 256 = 144 So a − b = ±12 … (5) Solve with a + b = 20:
  • If a − b = 12: a = 16, b = 4
  • If a − b = −12: a = 4, b = 16
Thus the numbers are 4 and 16 (either order).

Miscellaneous Examples

Example 14: Condition implying four numbers are in G.P.

If an expression is a sum of squares and it’s given ≤ 0, then it must be exactly 0 ⇒ each square term = 0.
Given: a, b, c, d and p are different real numbers such that
(a2 + b2 + c2)p2 − 2(ab + bc + cd)p + (b2 + c2 + d2) ≤ 0 a, b, c, d, p: real numbers (given different)
Group the LHS: (a2p2 − 2abp + b2) + (b2p2 − 2bcp + c2) + (c2p2 − 2cdp + d2) Spot perfect squares: (ap − b)2 + (bp − c)2 + (cp − d)2 ≥ 0
Each square ≥ 0 ⇒ sum ≥ 0. But we’re given the same thing ≤ 0 ⇒ the only way is = 0.
So, (ap − b)2 + (bp − c)2 + (cp − d)2 = 0 Sum of squares = 0 ⇒ each square = 0:
  • ap − b = 0 ⇒ b = ap
  • bp − c = 0 ⇒ c = bp
  • cp − d = 0 ⇒ d = cp
Hence, \(\dfrac{b}{a} = p, \dfrac{c}{b} = p, \dfrac{d}{c} = p\)
Equal successive ratios ⇒ a, b, c, d are in G.P. (common ratio = p).
Exam tip: Main move = rewrite as sum of squares. Once you get “sum of squares ≤ 0” ⇒ instantly write “each square = 0”.

Summary (Sequences and Series)

Sequence: meaning and types

Sequence: numbers written in a fixed order, following some rule. Functional view: a sequence is a function with domain N or a finite starting set {1, 2, 3, …, k}.
  • Finite sequence: has finitely many terms.
  • Infinite sequence: goes on indefinitely (not finite).

Series: meaning and finite series

If a sequence is a1, a2, a3, … then a1 + a2 + a3 + … is a series. Finite series: a series with a finite number of terms.

Geometric Progression (G.P.)

A sequence is a G.P. if \(\dfrac{\text{term}}{\text{previous term}}\) is the same constant throughout. This constant is the common ratio. Convention:
  • first term = a
  • common ratio = r

nth term of a G.P.

an = arn−1 an: nth term of the G.P. a: first term r: common ratio n: term number (natural number)

Sum of first n terms of a G.P.

\(S_n = \dfrac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}\), if r ≠ 1 Equivalently, \(S_n = \dfrac{a(r^n-1)}{r-1}\), if r ≠ 1 Sn: sum of first n terms a: first term r: common ratio n: number of terms
Common mistake: Using the G.P. sum formula when r = 1. (If r = 1, every term = a, so Sn = na.)

Geometric Mean (G.M.)

The geometric mean of two positive numbers a and b is √(ab). Also: if G = √(ab), then a, G, b are in G.P.
G = √(ab) G: geometric mean between positive numbers a and b

Historical Note (Sequences and Series)

  • Evidence suggests Babylonians knew arithmetic + geometric sequences about 4000 years ago.
  • As per Boethius (510), arithmetic + geometric sequences were known to early Greek writers.
  • In India, Aryabhatta (476) first gave formulas for sum of squares and cubes of natural numbers in Aryabhatiyam (around 499).
  • Aryabhatta also gave a formula for the sum to n terms of an arithmetic sequence starting with the pth term.
  • Brahmgupta (598), Mahavira (850), and Bhaskara (1114–1185) also worked on sums of squares and cubes.
  • The Fibonacci sequence (useful in applications) was discovered by Leonardo Fibonacci (1170–1250).
  • The 17th century brought classification of series into specific forms.
  • In 1671, James Gregory used the term infinite series linked with infinite sequences.
  • With rigorous development of algebraic + set-theoretic tools, the modern form of sequences and series became possible.
