Ch-10 : Conic Sections

10.1 Introduction

Conic sections (conics) = curves formed when a plane cuts a double-napped right circular cone.
In this chapter (after equations of a line), we study: circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola. Names parabola + hyperbola were given by Apollonius. Conics come from slicing a cone with a plane; used in planetary motion, telescopes/antennas, reflectors (flashlights, car headlights), etc.

10.2 Sections of a Cone

Formation and parts of the cone

Let l be a fixed vertical line and m another line meeting it at fixed point V and making angle α with it. Rotate m about l, keeping α constant → forms a double-napped right circular hollow cone extending infinitely both ways.
  • Vertex: fixed point V
  • Axis: line l
  • Generator: rotating line m
  • Nappes: the 2 parts of the cone separated by the vertex

Conic section and the role of the cutting plane

Conic section = curve made by intersecting a right circular cone with a plane.
The type of conic depends on the plane’s position + the angle it makes with the cone’s vertical axis.
Let β be the angle between the cutting plane and the cone’s vertical axis. The plane may cut the cone at the vertex or elsewhere (on one nappe or both).

10.2.1 Circle, ellipse, parabola and hyperbola (non-degenerate cases)

If the plane cuts the cone at a point other than the vertex (hits a nappe away from the vertex), then the conic depends on how β compares to α:
  • Circle: β = 90°
  • Ellipse: α < β < 90°
  • Parabola: β = α
  • Hyperbola: 0 ≤ β < α
  • For circle, ellipse, parabola: plane cuts across one nappe only.
  • For hyperbola: plane cuts through both nappes.

10.2.2 Degenerated conic sections (cuts through the vertex)

If the plane cuts the cone at the vertex, the intersection can “degenerate” into simpler shapes:
  • Point: when α < β ≤ 90°
  • Straight line: when β = α (plane contains a generator). This is the degenerate parabola.
  • Pair of intersecting straight lines: when 0 ≤ β < α. This is the degenerate hyperbola.
Later: we get equations of these conics in standard form using their geometric definitions.

10.3 Circle

Definition and basic terms

Definition (Circle): set of all points in a plane at the same distance from a fixed point.
  • Fixed point = centre
  • Constant distance = radius

Equation of a circle with given centre and radius

Equation is simplest if the centre is at the origin, but we derive it for any centre. Centre C(h, k), radius r. For any point P(x, y) on the circle: |CP| = r Using distance formula:
(x − h)2 + (y − k)2 = r2 x, y: coordinates of any point on the circle h, k: coordinates of the centre r: radius (positive real number)

Worked examples on the circle

Example 1: centre at origin

Find an equation of the circle with centre (0,0) and radius r. Here h = 0, k = 0. Substitute into (x − h)2 + (y − k)2 = r2:
x2 + y2 = r2 x, y: coordinates of any point on the circle r: radius

Example 2: given centre and radius

Find the equation of the circle with centre (−3, 2) and radius 4. h = −3, k = 2, r = 4: (x − (−3))2 + (y − 2)2 = 42
(x + 3)2 + (y − 2)2 = 16 x, y: coordinates of any point on the circle Centre: (−3, 2) Radius: 4

Example 3: find centre and radius from general form (complete squares)

Find the centre and the radius of the circle x2 + y2 + 8x + 10y − 8 = 0. Move constant to RHS; group x-terms and y-terms: (x2 + 8x) + (y2 + 10y) = 8 Complete the squares:
  • x2 + 8x + 16 = (x + 4)2
  • y2 + 10y + 25 = (y + 5)2
Add the same constants to both sides: (x2 + 8x + 16) + (y2 + 10y + 25) = 8 + 16 + 25 So: (x + 4)2 + (y + 5)2 = 49
  • Match to (x − h)2 + (y − k)2 = r2. Note: (x + 4)2 = (x − (−4))2, similarly for y.
  • Centre = (−4, −5), radius = 7.
Completing the square: add (coefficient of x / 2)2 inside. For x2 + 8x add (8/2)2 = 16; for y2 + 10y add (10/2)2 = 25. Whatever you add on LHS, add on RHS too.

Example 4: circle through two points with centre on a given line

Find the equation of the circle which passes through (2, −2) and (3, 4) and whose centre lies on the line x + y = 2. Let centre be (h, k), radius r. General circle: (x − h)2 + (y − k)2 = r2 Turn each condition into an equation:
  • Through (2, −2): (2 − h)2 + (−2 − k)2 = r2 ...(1)
  • Through (3, 4): (3 − h)2 + (4 − k)2 = r2 ...(2)
  • Centre on x + y = 2: h + k = 2 ...(3)
Solving (1), (2), (3): h = 0.7, k = 1.3, and r2 = 12.58 So the circle is:
(x − 0.7)2 + (y − 1.3)2 = 12.58 (h, k) = (0.7, 1.3): centre r2 = 12.58: square of radius
Coordinate-geometry tip: convert every geometric condition into an equation, then solve the simultaneous equations (usually for h, k, r).

10.4 Parabola

Definition and basic terms

Parabola: locus of all points in a plane that are at the same distance from:
  • a fixed line = directrix, and
  • a fixed point F not on the line = focus.
Meaning note: “Para” = “for”, “bola” = “throwing” → path traced by a thrown ball.

Degenerate case of a parabola (focus lies on directrix)

If the fixed point lies on the fixed line, then points equidistant from them form the straight line through the fixed point and ⟂ to the fixed line. This is the degenerate parabola.

Axis and vertex of a parabola

  • Axis: line through the focus and ⟂ to the directrix.
  • Vertex: point where the parabola meets its axis.

10.4.1 Standard equations of parabola (set-up)

Parabola equations are simplest when the vertex is at the origin and the axis of symmetry is along the x-axis or y-axis. Then there are 4 orientations (Fig. 10.15 a–d): opening right, left, up, or down.

Equation of a Parabola (Focus on x-axis, Vertex at Origin)

Parabola (definition): locus of all points P such that distance P → focus equals the perpendicular distance P → directrix.

Derivation of the standard equation y2 = 4ax

Take focus F(a,0) with a > 0, and directrix x = −a (i.e. x + a = 0).
  • Distance between focus and directrix = 2a.
  • Let FM be ⟂ to the directrix, and O the midpoint of FM. Then O lies on the parabola = the vertex.
  • Choose O as origin, OX as x-axis, and OY ⟂ to it as y-axis.
Let P(x, y) be any point on the parabola. Drop PB ⟂ directrix, meeting it at B.
  • Directrix is x = −a ⇒ foot B = (−a, y).
  • Parabola condition: PF = PB.
Distance PF: PF = √((x − a)2 + y2) x, y = coordinates of point P a = x-coordinate of the focus (a > 0)
Perpendicular distance to x = −a: PB = |x + a| x = x-coordinate of point P a = positive constant locating the directrix
Equate PF and PB (points on parabola):
  • √((x − a)2 + y2) = |x + a|
  • Square: (x − a)2 + y2 = (x + a)2
  • x2 − 2ax + a2 + y2 = x2 + 2ax + a2
  • y2 = 4ax
Standard parabola (opens right): y2 = 4ax, a > 0 a = distance (vertex → focus) = distance (vertex → directrix)

Converse check (point satisfying the equation lies on the parabola)

If P(x,y) satisfies y2 = 4ax, then:
  • PF = √((x − a)2 + y2) = √((x − a)2 + 4ax) = √(x2 + 2ax + a2) = |x + a|
  • But PB = |x + a|
  • So PF = PB ⇒ P lies on the parabola.
So y2 = 4ax is exactly the parabola with vertex (0,0), focus (a,0), directrix x = −a, with a > 0.

Discussion: domain and quadrants for y2 = 4ax

  • y2 ≥ 0 and 4a > 0 ⇒ x ≥ 0.
  • No point on the curve has x < 0.
  • Curve goes infinitely into the 1st and 4th quadrants.
  • Axis is the positive x-axis.

Standard equations of parabolas (four orientations)

Opens right: y2 = 4ax a > 0; axis along x-axis; focus towards +x
Opens left: y2 = −4ax a > 0; axis along x-axis; focus towards −x
Opens up: x2 = 4ay a > 0; axis along y-axis; focus towards +y
Opens down: x2 = −4ay a > 0; axis along y-axis; focus towards −y
Note (what “standard” means here): in all above, vertex at origin, focus on a coordinate axis, and directrix ∥ the other axis. (Other focus/directrix positions are outside this scope.)

Observations from standard parabola equations (symmetry and opening)

  1. Symmetry: parabola is symmetric about its axis.
    • If equation has y2 ⇒ axis is along x-axis.
    • If equation has x2 ⇒ axis is along y-axis.
  2. Axis along x-axis (equations in y2):
    • Opens right if coefficient of x is +.
    • Opens left if coefficient of x is −.
  3. Axis along y-axis (equations in x2):
    • Opens up if coefficient of y is +.
    • Opens down if coefficient of y is −.
Exam tip: squared variable (x2 or y2) gives the axis; sign of the non-squared term gives the opening direction.

Latus Rectum of a Parabola

Latus rectum (parabola): a line segment ⊥ to the axis, passing through the focus, with its endpoints on the parabola.

Length of the latus rectum for y2 = 4ax

For y2 = 4ax (axis = x-axis, focus = (a,0)):
  • Using parabola property: for endpoints A and C on the latus rectum, AF = AC.
  • Distance focus → directrix: FM = 2a ⇒ AC = FM = 2a.
  • So AF = 2a.
  • By symmetry about x-axis: AF = FB.
  • Total latus rectum length: AB = AF + FB = 2a + 2a = 4a.
Length of latus rectum (standard parabola): 4a a = distance from vertex to focus

Example 5: Focus, axis, directrix, latus rectum for y2 = 8x

  • Has y2 ⇒ axis of symmetry is the x-axis.
  • x coefficient is positive ⇒ opens to the right.
  • Compare y2 = 8x with y2 = 4ax ⇒ 4a = 8 ⇒ a = 2.
  • Focus = (a,0) = (2,0).
  • Directrix: x = −a ⇒ x = −2.
  • Length of latus rectum = 4a = 8.

Example 6: Equation given focus (2,0) and directrix x = −2

  • Focus on x-axis ⇒ axis is x-axis ⇒ form: y2 = 4ax or y2 = −4ax.
  • Directrix x = −2 with focus (2,0) ⇒ opens right ⇒ pick y2 = 4ax.
  • a = 2 ⇒ y2 = 4(2)x = 8x.

Example 7: Equation given vertex (0,0) and focus (0,2)

  • Focus on y-axis ⇒ axis is y-axis.
  • Vertex at origin ⇒ standard form x2 = 4ay.
  • a = 2 ⇒ x2 = 4(2)y = 8y.

Example 8: Parabola symmetric about y-axis and passing through (2, −3)

  • Symmetric about y-axis + vertex at origin ⇒ x2 = 4ay or x2 = −4ay.
  • (2, −3) has y < 0 ⇒ opens downwards ⇒ x2 = −4ay.
  • Sub (2,−3): 22 = −4a(−3) ⇒ 4 = 12a ⇒ a = 1/3.
  • Equation: x2 = −4(1/3)y = −(4/3)y ⇒ 3x2 = −4y.
Common mistake: picking x2 = 4ay vs x2 = −4ay without checking if the point is above/below the x-axis (sign of y).

Ellipse: Basic Definitions and Axes

Ellipse (definition): locus of points such that the sum of distances from two fixed points is constant. Those fixed points are the foci.
Note: this constant sum is always greater than the distance between the two foci.

Centre, major axis, minor axis, vertices

  • Centre: midpoint of the segment joining the foci.
  • Major axis: segment passing through both foci.
  • Minor axis: segment through the centre, ⊥ to the major axis.
  • Vertices: endpoints of the major axis.

Standard ellipse notation (lengths)

  • Major axis length = 2a ⇒ semi-major = a.
  • Minor axis length = 2b ⇒ semi-minor = b.
  • Distance between foci = 2c ⇒ centre → each focus = c.

Ellipse: Relationship Among a, b, c

Relationship between semi-major axis, semi-minor axis and focus distance

Let centre be O, foci be F1, F2. Pick two special points:
  • P: endpoint of major axis (a vertex).
  • Q: endpoint of minor axis.
At P (end of major axis):
  • F1P + F2P = (F1O + OP) + F2P
  • F1O = c, OP = a, and F2P = a − c (P is a from centre; focus is c from centre on same line)
  • Sum = (c + a) + (a − c) = 2a
At Q (end of minor axis):
  • By symmetry: Q is equally far from each focus.
  • Each distance = √(b2 + c2)
  • So F1Q + F2Q = 2√(b2 + c2)
Same ellipse ⇒ same constant sum:
  • 2√(b2 + c2) = 2a
  • √(b2 + c2) = a
  • a2 = b2 + c2
  • c = √(a2 − b2)
Ellipse relation: a2 = b2 + c2 a = semi-major axis b = semi-minor axis c = distance from centre to a focus
Focus distance: c = √(a2 − b2) a = semi-major axis b = semi-minor axis

Eccentricity of an Ellipse

Eccentricity (ellipse): ratio = (centre → focus) ÷ (centre → vertex).
Eccentricity: e = c/a e = eccentricity c = distance from centre to focus a = distance from centre to vertex (semi-major axis)
Since c = ae, each focus is ae from the centre along the major axis.

Standard Equations of an Ellipse (Orientation)

When the equation is simplest

  • Equation is simplest when the centre is at the origin.
  • Foci lie on either the x-axis or the y-axis (2 orientations).

Next step (setup for derivation with foci on x-axis)

Next, we derive the ellipse equation with its foci on the x-axis (as in the referenced figure).

Equation of an Ellipse with Centre at Origin and Major Axis along the x-axis

Set-up (standard position):
  • Foci: F1(−c, 0), F2(c, 0)
  • O is midpoint of F1F2 (so centre at origin)
  • x-axis along the line through the foci; y-axis ⟂ to it through O
Ellipse (focus definition): points P(x, y) such that PF1 + PF2 = 2a, with 0 < c < a.
Distances to foci PF1 = √((x + c)2 + y2) PF2 = √((x − c)2 + y2) Symbols: P(x, y) on ellipse; c = focus distance from origin; a = semi-major axis.
Using PF1 + PF2 = 2a: √((x + c)2 + y2) + √((x − c)2 + y2) = 2a …(1) Rearrange (1) then square: √((x + c)2 + y2) = 2a − √((x − c)2 + y2) Squaring: (x + c)2 + y2 = 4a2 − 4a√((x − c)2 + y2) + (x − c)2 + y2 Simplify → get an expression that can be squared again; after the second squaring and simplification:
Intermediate form (a2 − c2)x2 + a2y2 = a2(a2 − c2) Symbols: a = semi-major axis; c = focus distance; x, y = coordinates of P.
Let b2 = a2 − c2 (so c2 = a2 − b2). Then:
Standard equation (major axis along x-axis) x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1 Symbols: a = semi-major axis; b = semi-minor axis; foci (±c, 0) with c2 = a2 − b2.

Converse: Equation ⇒ Focus Property

Assume P(x, y) satisfies: x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1, with 0 < c < a and b2 = a2 − c2. Then: y2 = b2(1 − x2/a2) Compute PF1: PF1 = √((x + c)2 + y2) = √((x + c)2 + b2(1 − x2/a2)) Using b2 = a2 − c2, this simplifies to: PF1 = a + (cx)/a Similarly: PF2 = a − (cx)/a Hence: PF1 + PF2 = (a + cx/a) + (a − cx/a) = 2a
This proves the equivalence:
  • Geometric definition (sum of distances to foci = 2a) ⇔ standard equation x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1 (with foci at (±c, 0) and c2 = a2 − b2).

Discussion/Consequences from the Standard Equation

From x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1: y2/b2 = 1 − x2/a2 ≤ 1 ⇒ x2 ≤ a2 ⇒ −a ≤ x ≤ a. So the ellipse lies between (and touches) x = −a and x = a. Similarly, y2 ≤ b2 ⇒ −b ≤ y ≤ b, so it lies between (and touches) y = −b and y = b.
Standard equation (major axis along y-axis) x2/b2 + y2/a2 = 1 Symbols: a = semi-major axis (along y); b = semi-minor axis (along x).
Standard ellipses:
  • Centre at origin
  • Major/minor axes along coordinate axes
Ellipses with centre not at origin and/or rotated axes are beyond the present scope.

Observations from the Standard Equations

  1. Symmetry: symmetric about both coordinate axes. If (x, y) is on the ellipse, then (−x, y), (x, −y), (−x, −y) are also on it.
  2. Location of foci: foci always lie on the major axis.
  3. How to spot the major axis from the equation:
    • Use intercepts on axes of symmetry, or
    • In standard form, the axis with the larger denominator is the major axis:
    • If a2 is under x2 (larger denom under x2) → major axis along x-axis.
    • If a2 is under y2 (larger denom under y2) → major axis along y-axis.
Exam pitfall: don’t pick major axis by the bigger coefficient. Pick by bigger denominator (or rewrite into standard form first).

Latus Rectum of an Ellipse

Definition (latus rectum): a line segment perpendicular to the major axis, through a focus, with endpoints on the ellipse.

Length of Latus Rectum for x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1

Take focus F2(c, 0). Let A be an endpoint of the latus rectum through F2. Since it’s ⟂ to the major axis (x-axis), it’s a vertical line through x = c, so: A(c, l) = (ae, l) (since c = ae) Because A lies on the ellipse: (ae)2/a2 + l2/b2 = 1 ⇒ e2 + l2/b2 = 1 ⇒ l2 = b2(1 − e2) But: e = c/a, and c2 = a2 − b2 ⇒ e2 = (a2 − b2)/a2 ⇒ 1 − e2 = b2/a2 Therefore: l2 = b2·(b2/a2) = b4/a2 ⇒ l = b2/a By symmetry about the x-axis, the other endpoint B is (c, −l), so latus rectum length: AB = 2l
Length of latus rectum (standard ellipse) L = 2b2/a Symbols: L = length of latus rectum; a = semi-major axis; b = semi-minor axis.
Exam pitfall: l = b2/a is the semi-latus-rectum (focus to one endpoint). Full length is 2b2/a.

Worked Examples (Ellipse)

Example 9: x2/25 + y2/9 = 1

Since 25 (under x2) > 9 (under y2) ⇒ major axis along x-axis. Compare with: x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1
  • a = 5, b = 3
  • c2 = a2 − b2 = 25 − 9 = 16 ⇒ c = 4
  • Foci: (−c, 0), (c, 0) = (−4, 0), (4, 0)
  • Vertices: (±a, 0) = (−5, 0), (5, 0)
  • Length of major axis: 2a = 10
  • Length of minor axis: 2b = 6
  • Eccentricity: e = c/a = 4/5
  • Latus rectum length: 2b2/a = 2·9/5 = 18/5

Example 10: 9x2 + 4y2 = 36

Convert to standard form (divide by 36): x2/4 + y2/9 = 1 Since 9 (under y2) is larger ⇒ major axis along y-axis. Compare with: x2/b2 + y2/a2 = 1
  • b = 2, a = 3
  • c = √(a2 − b2) = √(9 − 4) = √5
  • e = c/a = √5/3
  • Foci: (0, ±c) = (0, √5) and (0, −√5)
  • Vertices: (0, ±a) = (0, 3) and (0, −3)
  • Length of major axis: 2a = 6
  • Length of minor axis: 2b = 4
  • Eccentricity: √5/3

Example 11: Vertices (±13, 0), foci (±5, 0)

Vertices on x-axis ⇒ major axis along x-axis ⇒ use: x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1
  • a = 13
  • c = 5
  • c2 = a2 − b2 ⇒ b2 = a2 − c2 = 169 − 25 = 144 ⇒ b = 12
Required ellipse x2/169 + y2/144 = 1 Symbols: a = 13, b = 12.

Example 12: Major axis length 20; foci (0, ±5)

Foci on y-axis ⇒ major axis along y-axis ⇒ use: x2/b2 + y2/a2 = 1
  • Major axis length = 2a = 20 ⇒ a = 10
  • c = 5
  • c2 = a2 − b2 ⇒ b2 = a2 − c2 = 100 − 25 = 75
Required ellipse x2/75 + y2/100 = 1 Symbols: a = 10 (along y), b2 = 75, c = 5.

Example 13: Major axis along x-axis; passes through (4, 3) and (−1, 4)

Major axis along x-axis ⇒ start with standard form: x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1 Sub (4, 3): 16/a2 + 9/b2 = 1 …(1) Sub (−1, 4): 1/a2 + 16/b2 = 1 …(2) Solve (1) & (2):
  • a2 = 247/7
  • b2 = 247/15
So: x2/(247/7) + y2/(247/15) = 1 Multiply through by 247:
Required equation 7x2 + 15y2 = 247 Symbols: (a2, b2) chosen so that both given points satisfy the standard ellipse equation.
Exam pitfall: For an ellipse through points, keep a2, b2 unknown and form 2 linear equations in 1/a2 and 1/b2. Faster + fewer algebra slips.

Hyperbola (Start of Next Section)

Definition: A hyperbola = set of points where the difference of distances from two fixed points is constant.

Hyperbola: basic terminology and parameters

Hyperbola (focus definition): “difference” means: (distance to the farther focus) − (distance to the nearer focus) = constant.
  • Foci: the two fixed points.
  • Centre: midpoint of the segment joining the foci.
  • Transverse axis: the line through the foci.
  • Conjugate axis: line through the centre ⟂ transverse axis.
  • Vertices: intersection points of the hyperbola with the transverse axis.
Standard parameters + relation: Distance between foci = 2c Distance between vertices (transverse axis length) = 2a b is defined by: b = √(c² − a²) (so b² = c² − a²) Length of conjugate axis = 2b a: semi-transverse axis length b: semi-conjugate axis length c: distance from centre to each focus

Constant difference equals 2a (why the constant is 2a)

To get the constant (difference of distances) for a hyperbola in standard position:
  • Pick point P at the vertices A and B on the transverse axis.
  • Using “difference of distances is constant”: BF₁ − BF₂ = AF₂ − AF₁
  • Split along the axis and rearrange: BA + AF₁ − BF₂ = AB + BF₂ − AF₁ So AF₁ = BF₂.
  • Then: BF₁ − BF₂ = BA + AF₁ − BF₂ = BA = 2a
The constant difference in the focus-definition is 2a (i.e. the transverse axis length = distance between vertices).

Eccentricity of a hyperbola

Eccentricity (hyperbola): e = c/a
  • Since c ≥ a, hyperbola eccentricity is never < 1 ⇒ e ≥ 1.
  • In terms of e, each focus is ae from the centre.
e = c/a e: eccentricity c: distance from centre to focus a: semi-transverse axis length

Standard equations of a hyperbola

Case 1: Transverse axis along x-axis

Assume centre at origin + foci on the x-axis.
  • Centre: O(0,0)
  • Foci: F₁(−c,0), F₂(c,0)
  • Any point P(x,y) on the hyperbola satisfies: PF₁ − PF₂ = 2a
Distance forms: PF₁ = √((x + c)² + y²) PF₂ = √((x − c)² + y²) x, y: coordinates of P c: distance of each focus from origin
Start with: √((x + c)² + y²) − √((x − c)² + y²) = 2a Rearrange → square once → simplify → square again; final result:
Standard form (transverse axis along x-axis): x²/a² − y²/b² = 1 a > 0: semi-transverse axis b > 0: semi-conjugate axis c: satisfies c² = a² + b²

Converse check (every point on the equation satisfies the distance definition)

Let P(x,y) satisfy x²/a² − y²/b² = 1 with 0 < a < c. Then:
  • From x²/a² − y²/b² = 1, rewrite: y² = b²((x² − a²)/a²)
  • Compute: PF₁ = √((x + c)² + y²) Sub in y² and use b² = c² − a²; simplifies to: PF₁ = a + (c/a)x
  • Similarly: PF₂ = a − (c/a)x, but on the right branch x > a and c > a, so a − (c/a)x is negative; distance must be positive ⇒ PF₂ = (c/a)x − a
  • So for the right branch: PF₁ − PF₂ = (a + (c/a)x) − ((c/a)x − a) = 2a
  • For the left branch (left of x = −a): PF₂ − PF₁ = 2a
The equation x²/a² − y²/b² = 1 gives two branches:
  • Right branch: PF₁ − PF₂ = 2a
  • Left branch: PF₂ − PF₁ = 2a

Equilateral hyperbola

Equilateral hyperbola: hyperbola with a = b.

Region where the curve cannot lie (no points between x = −a and x = +a)

From: x²/a² − y²/b² = 1 implies x²/a² = 1 + y²/b² ≥ 1 So |x|/a ≥ 1 ⇒ |x| ≥ a ⇒ x ≤ −a or x ≥ a.
No part of the hyperbola lies between x = −a and x = +a. So there is no real intercept on the conjugate axis (here: the y-axis).

Case 2: Transverse axis along y-axis

If the transverse axis is along the y-axis (centre at origin), then:
Standard form (transverse axis along y-axis): y²/a² − x²/b² = 1 a: semi-transverse axis (along y-axis) b: semi-conjugate axis (along x-axis)
Standard equations assume:
  • Centre at the origin
  • Transverse + conjugate axes along the coordinate axes
Hyperbolas can have any perpendicular pair of axes (done later).

Observations from the standard equations

  1. Symmetry: symmetric about both axes. If (x,y) lies on it, then (−x,y), (x,−y), (−x,−y) also lie on it.
  2. Foci lie on the transverse axis: the transverse axis matches the positive term in the standard equation (the variable with the + sign).
    • Example: x²/9 − y²/16 = 1 ⇒ transverse axis along x-axis; length 2a = 6.
    • Example: y²/25 − x²/16 = 1 ⇒ transverse axis along y-axis; length 2a = 10.
Exam tip: Transverse axis = the squared variable with the positive sign. That denominator is a² ⇒ a = √(denominator).

Latus rectum of a hyperbola

Latus rectum: line segment ⟂ transverse axis, through a focus, endpoints on the hyperbola.
Length of latus rectum (hyperbola): 2b²/a a: semi-transverse axis b: semi-conjugate axis

Worked examples on hyperbola

Example 14: foci, vertices, eccentricity, latus rectum

Find foci, vertices, eccentricity, and latus rectum length for: (i) x²/9 − y²/16 = 1   (ii) y² − 16x² = 16 (i) Compare x²/9 − y²/16 = 1 with x²/a² − y²/b² = 1:
  • a = 3, b = 4
  • c = √(a² + b²) = √(9 + 16) = 5
  • Foci: (±c, 0) = (±5, 0)
  • Vertices: (±a, 0) = (±3, 0)
  • Eccentricity: e = c/a = 5/3
  • Latus rectum length: 2b²/a = 2·16/3 = 32/3
(ii) Given y² − 16x² = 16. Divide by 16: y²/16 − x²/1 = 1 Compare with y²/a² − x²/b² = 1:
  • a = 4, b = 1
  • c = √(a² + b²) = √(16 + 1) = √17
  • Foci: (0, ±c) = (0, ±√17)
  • Vertices: (0, ±a) = (0, ±4)
  • Eccentricity: e = c/a = √17/4
  • Latus rectum length: 2b²/a = 2·1/4 = 1/2
Common slip: For a hyperbola, c² = a² + b² (NOT a² − b²). So compute c using a plus.

Example 15: equation from foci and vertices

Given: foci (0, ±3), vertices (0, ±11/2). Find the equation.
  • Foci + vertices on y-axis ⇒ use: y²/a² − x²/b² = 1
  • Vertices (0, ±a) ⇒ a = 11/2
  • Foci (0, ±c) ⇒ c = 3
  • b² = c² − a² = 9 − (121/4) = (36 − 121)/4 = 25/4
Equation: y²/(121/4) − x²/(25/4) = 1 Multiply through to get the stated simplified form: 100y² − 44x² = 275

Example 16: equation from foci and latus rectum length

Given: foci (0, ±12), latus rectum length = 36. Find the equation.
  • Foci on y-axis ⇒ use y²/a² − x²/b² = 1
  • c = 12
  • Latus rectum: 2b²/a = 36 ⇒ b²/a = 18 ⇒ b² = 18a
  • Use c² = a² + b²: 144 = a² + 18a
  • Solve a² + 18a − 144 = 0 ⇒ a = −24, 6
  • Since a > 0, take a = 6
  • Then b² = 18a = 108
Equation: y²/36 − x²/108 = 1, i.e. 3y² − x² = 108.
Exam tip: If you get a quadratic in a, reject negative a (since a is a length).

Miscellaneous example (parabola application)

Example 17: parabolic mirror depth and aperture

Given: Focus is 5 cm from vertex, mirror is 45 cm deep. Find aperture width AB.
  • Parabola with vertex at origin + axis along +x: y² = 4ax
  • Focus 5 cm from vertex ⇒ a = 5
  • So equation: y² = 4(5)x = 20x
  • Depth 45 cm ⇒ at opening plane x = 45
  • y² = 20·45 = 900 ⇒ y = ±30
  • Full width AB = 2|y| = 2·30 = 60 cm
Parabola (axis along +x, vertex at origin): y² = 4ax a: vertex-to-focus distance (x,y): any point on the parabola

Worked Examples (Applications of Conics)

Parabolic deflection of a beam (finding where deflection is 1 cm)

Beam bends into a parabola → pick axes so:
  • vertex = lowest point
  • axis of parabola = vertical
Standard form (opens up, vertex at origin): x2 = 4ay
x2 = 4ay a = parameter (distance vertex → focus) (x, y) = any point on parabola (vertex at origin, axis along +y)
Given: max (central) deflection = 3 cm
  • horizontal distance from centre = 6 m
  • vertical rise from vertex = 3 cm = 3/100 m
So parabola passes through (6, 3/100).
Step 1: Find a using point (6, 3/100). Use x2 = 4ay: 62 = 4a(3/100) 36 = (12a)/100 a = (36 × 100)/12 = 300 m Step 2: Find x when deflection is 1 cm.
Deflection 1 cm ⇒ vertical rise from vertex = 1/100 m. In the textbook’s coordinate choice, point B for “deflection 1 cm” has y = 2/100 m. So take B = (x, 2/100).
Substitute y = 2/100 and a = 300 into x2 = 4ay: x2 = 4 × 300 × (2/100) = 24 x = √24 = 2√6 m
Always keep units consistent: 1 cm = 1/100 m, 3 cm = 3/100 m.

Example 19: Locus of a point on a rod between coordinate axes (ellipse)

Segment AB has ends on the axes (A on x-axis, B on y-axis). If point P divides AB in a fixed ratio (fixed AP and PB), then the locus of P is an ellipse (standard form).
Given: AB = 15 cm, A on x-axis, B on y-axis. P(x, y) on rod with AP = 6 cm. So PB = AB − AP = 15 − 6 = 9 cm.
Geometric setup (as in figure):
  • Rod AB makes angle θ with +x-axis (OX).
  • Drop perpendiculars: PR ⟂ x-axis, PQ ⟂ y-axis.
Step 1: Write cos θ in terms of x. From right △PBQ (PB hypotenuse, horizontal component = x): cos θ = x/9 Step 2: Write sin θ in terms of y. From right △PRA (AP hypotenuse, vertical component = y): sin θ = y/6 Step 3: Use cos2θ + sin2θ = 1. (x/9)2 + (y/6)2 = 1 x2/81 + y2/36 = 1
Locus is an ellipse: x2/81 + y2/36 = 1
Common slip: writing cos θ = 9/x or sin θ = 6/y. Stick to: cos θ = (adjacent)/(hypotenuse), sin θ = (opposite)/(hypotenuse).

Chapter Summary (Conic Sections)

Circle

Circle: set of points in a plane at a fixed distance from a fixed point (centre).
(x − h)2 + (y − k)2 = r2 (h, k) = centre r = radius (x, y) = any point on circle

Parabola

Parabola: set of points equidistant from a fixed point (focus) and a fixed line (directrix).
y2 = 4ax (axis along x-axis) Focus = (a, 0), with a > 0 Directrix: x = −a (x, y) = any point on parabola
Latus rectum (parabola): chord ⟂ axis, through focus, endpoints on parabola.
Length of latus rectum for y2 = 4ax is 4a a = parameter of parabola

Ellipse

Ellipse: set of points where the sum of distances from two fixed points (foci) is constant.
x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1 (foci on x-axis) a = semi-major axis b = semi-minor axis (x, y) = any point on ellipse
Latus rectum (ellipse): chord ⟂ major axis, through a focus, endpoints on ellipse.
Length of latus rectum for x2/a2 + y2/b2 = 1 is (2b2)/a a = semi-major axis b = semi-minor axis
Eccentricity (ellipse): (distance centre → focus) / (distance centre → vertex on major axis).

Hyperbola

Hyperbola: set of points where the difference of distances from two fixed points (foci) is constant.
x2/a2 − y2/b2 = 1 (foci on x-axis) a = semi-transverse axis b = semi-conjugate axis (x, y) = any point on hyperbola
Latus rectum (hyperbola): chord ⟂ transverse axis, through a focus, endpoints on hyperbola.
Length of latus rectum for x2/a2 − y2/b2 = 1 is (2b2)/a a = semi-transverse axis b = semi-conjugate axis
Eccentricity (hyperbola): (distance centre → focus) / (distance centre → vertex on transverse axis).

Historical Note (Analytical Geometry and Conics)

Origins and key contributors

  • Geometry = one of the oldest branches of maths; Greek geometers studied curves with both theory + real-life use.
  • Euclid (c. 300 B.C.): wrote a treatise setting up geometry from axioms based on physical ideas.
  • Early geometry (by ancient Indians and Greeks) was mainly synthetic (almost no algebra). This style (Euclid, Sulbasutras, etc.) continued for ~ 1300 years.
  • Apollonius (c. 200 B.C.): wrote ‘The Conic’; results on conic sections stayed unmatched for ~ 18 centuries.
  • Modern analytic geometry = Cartesian (named after Rene Descartes (1596–1650)); his ‘La Geometrie’ came out in 1637.
  • But the core method was already found by Pierre de Fermat (1601–1665). His Ad Locus Planos et Solidos Isagoge was published after his death in 1679, which helped Descartes get seen as the “single inventor”.
  • Isaac Barrow: avoided the Cartesian method.
  • Newton: used undetermined coefficients to get curve equations; worked with multiple coordinate systems incl. polar + bipolar.
  • Leibnitz: introduced the terms abscissa, ordinate, coordinate.
  • L’ Hospital (about 1700): wrote an important analytic geometry textbook.
  • Clairaut (1729): first gave the distance formula (clumsy version) + the intercept form of a line.
  • Cramer (1750): formally used the two axes; gave circle equation as (y − a)2 + (b − x)2 = r (as stated in the text).
  • Monge (1781): gave the modern point-slope form of a line + the perpendicularity condition for two lines.
  • S.F. Lacroix (1765–1843): gave the two-point form, the length of perpendicular from a point to a line, and a formula for the angle between two lines (noting it took a long time historically to reach these formulas).
  • C. Lame (1818) (civil engineer): gave the combined equation mE + m′E′ = 0 for the curve through intersection points of loci E = 0 and E′ = 0.
  • Conics connect to many discoveries in maths + science: Greeks studied them for beauty (esp. Archimedes (287–212 B.C.) + Apollonius), and today they appear in outer space exploration and in research on atomic particle behaviour.

Named line formulas and conditions mentioned

Point-slope form (Monge, 1781): y − y′ = a (x − x′) (x′, y′) = fixed point on the line a = slope of the line (historical notation) (x, y) = general point on the line
Condition of perpendicularity (Monge): aa′ + 1 = 0 a = slope of one line a′ = slope of the other line
Two-point form (Lacroix): (y − β)/(y′ − β) = (x − α)/(x′ − α) (text’s notation) (α, β) = one fixed point (x′, y′) = second fixed point (x, y) = general point on the line
Perpendicular distance from (α, β) to y = ax + b (Lacroix): |β − aα − b| / √(1 + a2) (text’s notation) (α, β) = point from which perpendicular is drawn y = ax + b = given line a = slope, b = y-intercept
Angle between two lines with slopes a and a′ (Lacroix): tan θ = (a − a′)/(1 + aa′) (as stated) θ = angle between the two lines a, a′ = slopes of the lines
Curve through intersection of E = 0 and E′ = 0 (Lame, 1818): mE + m′E′ = 0 E = 0 and E′ = 0 = two given loci m, m′ = arbitrary constants
⚠ Exam tip: don’t mix these up — ellipse: sum of distances = constant; hyperbola: difference of distances = constant.
