Ch-11 : Introduction to Three Dimensional Geometry

11.1 Introduction

Why we need 3D coordinates: In a plane, to fix a point we need 2 perpendicular axes ⇒ 2 numbers. In space (thrown ball / aeroplane / tip of ceiling fan), we need 3 perpendicular distances from 3 mutually perpendicular planes (think: floor + two adjacent walls). So: a point in space is written using three coordinates = its perpendicular distances from the three coordinate planes.
This chapter starts 3D geometry by extending 2D coordinates to a rectangular (orthogonal) 3D coordinate system.

11.2 Coordinate Axes and Coordinate Planes in Three Dimensional Space

Rectangular coordinate system in space

Construction: Take 3 planes intersecting at a point O, with the planes mutually perpendicular.
  • Their intersection lines are 3 mutually perpendicular lines: X′OX, Y′OY, Z′OZ → called x-axis, y-axis, z-axis.
  • These 3 perpendicular axes make the rectangular coordinate system.
  • The 3 planes are the coordinate planes:
    • XOY = XY-plane
    • YOZ = YZ-plane
    • ZOX = ZX-plane
  • O is the origin.

Orientation and sign convention

  • Take XOY (XY-plane) as the plane of the paper; Z′OZ is perpendicular to it (vertical if paper is horizontal).
  • z-sign: measured up along OZ is +; measured down along OZ′ is −.
  • y-sign: to the right of ZX-plane along OY is +; to the left along OY′ is −.
  • x-sign: in front of YZ-plane along OX is +; to the back along OX′ is −.

Octants

The 3 coordinate planes split space into 8 regions called octants. Named as: XOYZ, X′OYZ, X′OY′Z, XOY′Z, XOYZ′, X′OYZ′, X′OY′Z′, XOY′Z′ and denoted by I, II, III, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII respectively.

11.3 Coordinates of a Point in Space

Finding coordinates of a given point (projection method)

Given a point P in space:
  1. Drop perpendicular PM to the XY-plane; M is the foot on XY-plane.
  2. From M, draw perpendicular ML to the x-axis, meeting it at L.
  3. Let OL = x, LM = y, MP = z.
Then x, y, z are the x-, y-, z-coordinates of P ⇒ write P(x, y, z).
If P lies in octant XOYZ (1st octant), then x, y, z are all positive. In other octants, the signs change as per the octant.

Locating a point from a given ordered triplet (x, y, z)

Given (x, y, z):
  1. Mark L on x-axis so that OL = x.
  2. In the XY-plane, locate M with coordinates (x, y) such that LM ⟂ x-axis (i.e. LM ∥ y-axis).
  3. Through M, draw perpendicular MP to the XY-plane and mark P so that MP = z (use sign +/− by direction).
We get the point P(x, y, z).
There is a one-to-one correspondence between points in space and ordered triplets (x, y, z) of real numbers.

Alternative description using planes parallel to coordinate planes

Through a point P, draw 3 planes parallel to the coordinate planes:
  • Plane ∥ YZ-plane meets x-axis at A; take OA = x.
  • Plane ∥ ZX-plane meets y-axis at B; take OB = y.
  • Plane ∥ XY-plane meets z-axis at C; take OC = z.
Then P has coordinates (x, y, z). Conversely: given x, y, z, mark A, B, C on axes and draw planes through them ∥ YZ, ZX, XY respectively; their intersection gives the required point P.
If P(x, y, z) is any point in space, then:
  • x = perpendicular distance from YZ-plane
  • y = perpendicular distance from ZX-plane
  • z = perpendicular distance from XY-plane

Special cases (origin, axes, planes)

  • Origin: O(0, 0, 0)
  • Point on x-axis: (x, 0, 0)
  • Point in YZ-plane: (0, y, z)

Octant identification by signs (Table 11.1)

Remark: the octant is fixed by the signs of x, y, z.
  • Octant I: (+, +, +)
  • Octant II: (−, +, +)
  • Octant III: (−, −, +)
  • Octant IV: (+, −, +)
  • Octant V: (+, +, −)
  • Octant VI: (−, +, −)
  • Octant VII: (−, −, −)
  • Octant VIII: (+, −, −)
Exam tip: don’t “eyeball” octants from a sketch—match the sign pattern of (x, y, z) exactly with the list.

  • (−3, 1, 2) has signs (−, +, +) ⇒ Octant II
  • (−3, 1, −2) has signs (−, +, −) ⇒ Octant VI

11.4 Distance between Two Points

3D distance setup using a rectangular parallelepiped

Let P(x1, y1, z1) and Q(x2, y2, z2) be two points w.r.t. rectangular axes OX, OY, OZ. Draw planes through P and Q parallel to coordinate planes → forms a rectangular parallelepiped with PQ as a diagonal. In right triangle PAQ (right angle at A):
Relation (1) from right △PAQ: PQ2 = PA2 + AQ2 PQ = distance between points P and Q PA, AQ = perpendicular edges of the right triangle inside the parallelepiped
Also, triangle ANQ is right-angled with ∠ANQ = 90°. (Next step: use this to write AQ using coordinate differences.)

Distance Formula in Three Dimensions

For points in 3D: P(x1, y1, z1) and Q(x2, y2, z2), distance PQ comes from Pythagoras applied step-by-step along perpendicular directions.
  • In a plane: if PA ⟂ AQ, then PQ2 = PA2 + AQ2.
  • In 3D: if AN ⟂ NQ, then AQ2 = AN2 + NQ2.
  • Combine: PQ2 = PA2 + AN2 + NQ2.
Distance between two points in 3D PQ=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2+(z2−z1)2
  • x1, y1, z1 = coordinates of P
  • x2, y2, z2 = coordinates of Q
  • PQ = distance from P to Q
Distance from origin to a point IfPisoriginO(0,0,0)andQ(x,y,z),thenOQ=x2+y2+z2.
  • O = origin (0, 0, 0)
  • Q(x, y, z) = any point in space
  • OQ = distance from origin to Q
Distance formula tip: subtract in a consistent order for each coordinate: (x2−x1), (y2−y1), (z2−z1). Squaring removes minus signs, but consistency avoids later algebra slips (esp. when expanding).

Example 3: Distance Between Two Given Points

Find the distance between P(1, −3, 4) and Q(−4, 1, 2). Solution
  • PQ=(−4−1)2+(1−(−3))2+(2−4)2
  • =(−5)2+42+(−2)2
  • =25+16+4=45=35units

Example 4: Collinearity of Three Points Using Distances

Show that P(−2, 3, 5), Q(1, 2, 3) and R(7, 0, −1) are collinear.
Collinear points = points on the same straight line. Distance test: if one point lies between the other two, then PR = PQ + QR (or similar ordering).
Solution
    • PQ = (1−(−2))2 + (2−3)2 + (3−5)2 = 32 + (−1)2 + (−2)2 = 9+1+4 =14
    • QR = (7−1)2 + (0−2)2 + (−1−3)2 = 62 + (−2)2 + (−4)2 = 36+4+16 =56 =214
    • PR = (7−(−2))2 + (0−3)2 + (−1−5)2 = 92 + (−3)2 + (−6)2 = 81+9+36 =126 =314
    • PQ+QR = 14 + 214 =314 =PR
    Hence, P, Q and R are collinear.
    Using PQ + QR = PR only works when Q is between P and R (on segment PR). If the order is different, compare the largest distance to the sum of the other two.

    Example 5: Testing Whether a Triangle is Right-Angled

    Check whether A(3, 6, 9), B(10, 20, 30), C(25, −41, 5) form a right-angled triangle.
    Right-angled triangle test (some ordering): (largest side)2 = (other side)2 + (third side)2.
    Solution (compute squared lengths)
    • AB2 = (10−3)2 + (20−6)2 + (30−9)2 = 49 + 196 + 441 = 686
    • BC2 = (25−10)2 + (−41−20)2 + (5−30)2 = 225 + 3721 + 625 = 4571
    • CA2 = (3−25)2 + (6+41)2 + (9−5)2 = 484 + 2209 + 16 = 2709
    • Check: CA2 + AB2 = 2709 + 686 = 3395 ≠ 4571 = BC2
    Hence, triangle ABC is not right-angled.
    Use squared distances (AB2, etc.) to avoid square roots. Always identify the largest squared length first, then compare.

    Example 6: Locus of a Point with Condition PA² + PB² Constant

    Find the equation of the set of points P such that PA2 + PB2 = 2k2, where A(3, 4, 5) and B(−1, 3, −7).
    A locus = all points satisfying a given condition. Here: the condition is written using squared distances to two fixed points.
    Solution
    • Let P(x, y, z).
    • PA2 = (x−3)2 + (y−4)2 + (z−5)2
    • PB2 = (x+1)2 + (y−3)2 + (z+7)2
    • Given: PA2 + PB2 = 2k2
    • (x−3)2 + (y−4)2 + (z−5)2 + (x+1)2 + (y−3)2 + (z+7)2 = 2k2
    • On simplification: 2x2 + 2y2 + 2z2 − 4x − 14y + 4z = 2k2 − 109
    Expansion sign trap: (x−a)2 = x2 − 2ax + a2, but (x+a)2 = x2 + 2ax + a2.

    Parallelogram and Rectangle in 3D Using Distances

    Example 7: Proving a Parallelogram and Showing It Is Not a Rectangle

    Show that A(1, 2, 3), B(−1, −2, −1), C(2, 3, 2), D(4, 7, 6) form a parallelogram ABCD, but not a rectangle.
    • Quadrilateral is a parallelogram if both pairs of opposite sides are equal (⇒ parallel).
    • A parallelogram is a rectangle if (one test) its diagonals are equal in length.
    Solution: show opposite sides equal
    • AB = (−1−1)2 + (−2−2)2 + (−1−3)2 = 4+16+16 =36 =6
    • BC = (2−(−1))2 + (3−(−2))2 + (2−(−1))2 = 9+25+9 =43
    • CD = (4−2)2 + (7−3)2 + (6−2)2 = 4+16+16 =36 =6
    • DA = (1−4)2 + (2−7)2 + (3−6)2 = 9+25+9 =43
    • So AB=CD and BC=AD ⇒ ABCD is a parallelogram.
    Solution: show it is not a rectangle (diagonals unequal)
    • AC = (2−1)2 + (3−2)2 + (2−3)2 = 1+1+1 =3
    • BD = (4−(−1))2 + (7−(−2))2 + (6−(−1))2 = 25+81+49 =155
    • Since AC≠BD , ABCD is not a rectangle.
    Alt parallelogram test: diagonals bisect each other (midpoints of AC and BD are the same). Can use this instead of side-length checking.

    Equation of the Perpendicular Bisector Plane (Equidistant Points)

    Example 8: Locus of Points Equidistant from Two Fixed Points

    Find the equation of the set of points P such that distances from A(3, 4, −5) and B(−2, 1, 4) are equal.
    All points equidistant from two fixed points A and B in 3D form a plane: the perpendicular bisector plane of AB.
    Solution
    • Let P(x, y, z), with PA = PB.
    • PA2 = (x−3)2 + (y−4)2 + (z+5)2
    • PB2 = (x+2)2 + (y−1)2 + (z−4)2
    • Set equal: (x−3)2+(y−4)2+(z+5)2=(x+2)2+(y−1)2+(z−4)2
    • On simplification: 10x + 6y − 18z − 29 = 0
    Equidistant locus: skip square roots—use PA2 = PB2 directly so the algebra stays clean.

    Centroid of a Triangle in 3D

    Example 9: Finding a Vertex Using the Centroid

    Centroid of △ABC is G(1, 1, 1). Given A(3, −5, 7) and B(−1, 7, −6). Find C.
    Centroid of a triangle in 3D If A(x1, y1, z1), B(x2, y2, z2), C(x3, y3, z3), then G=(x1+x2+x33,y1+y2+y33,z1+z2+z33)
    • G = centroid = intersection point of medians
    • (xi, yi, zi) = vertex coordinates
    Solution
    • Let C(x, y, z) and G(1, 1, 1).
    • (3+(−1)+x)/3=1⇒x=1
    • ((−5)+7+y)/3=1⇒y=1
    • (7+(−6)+z)/3=1⇒z=2
    Hence, C = (1, 1, 2).
    In centroid questions: solve x-part, then y-part, then z-part. Biggest trap = sign errors when adding negatives.

    Chapter Summary (Key Facts to Revise)

    • In 3D rectangular Cartesian coordinates, the x-, y-, and z-axes are mutually perpendicular.
    • Coordinate planes:
      • XY-plane: z = 0
      • YZ-plane: x = 0
      • ZX-plane: y = 0
    • These 3 coordinate planes split space into 8 regions called octants.
    • A point is written as an ordered triplet (x, y, z), where:
      • x = directed distance from the YZ-plane
      • y = directed distance from the ZX-plane
      • z = directed distance from the XY-plane
    • Special forms (points on axes):
      • On x-axis: (x, 0, 0)
      • On y-axis: (0, y, 0)
      • On z-axis: (0, 0, z)
    Distance between two points (recalled) PQ=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2+(z2−z1)2
    • P(x1, y1, z1), Q(x2, y2, z2) = given points
    • PQ = distance between them

    Historical Note (Development of 3D Coordinate Geometry)

    • René Descartes (1596–1650): “father” of analytical geometry; mainly plane geometry (1637). He had the 3D idea but didn’t fully develop it.
    • Pierre Fermat (1601–1665) and La Hire (1640–1718): also mostly plane analytic geometry; 3D hints appear but not fully detailed.
    • J. Bernoulli (1667–1748): (1715 letter to Leibnitz) introduced the idea of the 3 coordinate planes used today.
    • Antoine Parent (1666–1716): first systematic analytical solid geometry (paper to French Academy, 1700).
    • L. Euler (1707–1783): treated 3D coordinate geometry systematically (1748) in an appendix (Chapter 5) to Vol. 2 of “Introduction to Geometry”.
    • Geometry beyond 3D grew mainly from mid-19th century; famous application: space-time continuum in Einstein’s Theory of Relativity.
