Ch-09 : Straight Lines

9.1 Introduction

Coordinate (analytical) geometry = geometry done using algebra. First developed systematically by René Descartes (1637, La Géométrie) → idea: represent curves using equations.
Basics already done (used throughout this chapter): coordinate axes + plane, plotting points, distance between points, section formula, midpoint, and area of a triangle using coordinates. This chapter focuses on the simplest figure: the straight line — mainly its algebraic form. Key idea needed: slope.
Reading coordinates: A point (x,y) is
  • x units from the y-axis along x-direction (right if x>0, left if x<0)
  • y units from the x-axis along y-direction (up if y>0, down if y<0)

Recall: Key formulae from earlier classes

I. Distance formula PQ=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2
  • P(x1,y1): first point
  • Q(x2,y2): second point
  • PQ: distance between P and Q
Example: Distance between (6,−4) and (3,0) PQ=(3−6)2+(0−(−4))2=(−3)2+42=9+16=25=5 units.
II. Section formula (internal division) If a point divides the line segment joining A(x1,y1) and B(x2,y2) internally in the ratio m:n, then coordinates are: (mx2+nx1m+n,my2+ny1m+n)
  • m:n: ratio in which the point divides AB (A-side : B-side)
  • x1,y1,x2,y2: coordinates of endpoints
Example: Point dividing A(1,−3) and B(−3,9) internally in ratio 1:3 x=1(−3)+3(1)1+3=−3+34=0 y=1(9)+3(−3)1+3=9−94=0 So the point is (0,0).
III. Midpoint formula (special case of section formula with m=n) Midpoint of A(x1,y1) and B(x2,y2): (x1+x22,y1+y22)
  • x1,y1,x2,y2: coordinates of endpoints
IV. Area of triangle (coordinate formula) Area of triangle with vertices (x1,y1),(x2,y2),(x3,y3): Area=12|x1(y2−y3)+x2(y3−y1)+x3(y1−y2)|
  • x1,y1,x2,y2,x3,y3: coordinates of the three vertices
Example: Area of triangle with vertices (4,4), (3,−2), (−3,16) Area=12|4((−2)−16)+3(16−4)+(−3)(4−(−2))| =12|4(−18)+3(12)+(−3)(6)|=12|−72+36−18| =12|−54|=27 square units.
Collinearity test: If the area of the triangle formed by three points A, B, C is 0, then A, B, C lie on the same straight line (are collinear).

9.2 Slope of a Line

A line in the coordinate plane makes two supplementary angles with the x-axis. The angle θ made with the positive x-axis, measured anticlockwise, is called the inclination of the line.
Inclination: θ satisfies 0∘≤θ≤180∘. Line ∥ (or same as) x-axis ⇒ inclination 0∘. Vertical line ∥ (or same as) y-axis ⇒ inclination 90∘.
Definition (Slope/Gradient): If θ is the inclination of line l, then tanθ is the slope (gradient) of l.
Slope from inclination m=tanθ,θ≠90∘ m: slope of the line θ: inclination with the +x-axis
Slope of x-axis = 0 (since θ=0∘⇒tan0∘=0). Slope of y-axis = not defined (since θ=90∘⇒tan90∘ is undefined).

9.2.1 Slope of a line through two given points

A non-vertical line is fixed by any two points on it. Let P(x1,y1) and Q(x2,y2) lie on a non-vertical line l with inclination θ. For a non-vertical line, we must have x1≠x2 (otherwise it’s vertical and slope is undefined). θ can be acute or obtuse, but you still get the same slope formula:
Slope through two points m=y2−y1x2−x1, provided x2≠x1 m: slope of the line through P(x1,y1) and Q(x2,y2) x1,y1,x2,y2: coordinates of the two points
Common exam pitfall: If x2=x1, denominator =0 ⇒ vertical line ⇒ slope undefined. (Don’t write “infinite” unless your syllabus allows it.)

9.2.2 Conditions for parallelism and perpendicularity (in terms of slopes)

Let l1 and l2 be two non-vertical lines with slopes m1,m2, and inclinations α,β respectively.
Parallel lines: l1∥l2⇔α=β⇔tanα=tanβ⇔m1=m2.
Perpendicular lines: l1⊥l2⇔β=α+90∘. Then tanβ=tan(α+90∘)=−cotα=−1tanα. So m2=−1m1, i.e. m1m2=−1.
Conditions using slopes (non-vertical lines) Parallel: m1=m2 Perpendicular: m2=−1m1 or m1m2=−1 m1,m2: slopes of the two lines
These slope tests are for non-vertical lines. A vertical line has undefined slope, so treat that case separately.

Example 1: Finding slopes in common situations

Find the slope of the lines: (a) Through (3, −2) and (−1, 4) (b) Through (3, −2) and (7, −2) (c) Through (3, −2) and (3, 4) (d) Making inclination 60° with positive x-axis Solution (a) m=4−(−2)−1−3=6−4=−32. (b) m=−2−(−2)7−3=04=0. (Horizontal line) (c) m=4−(−2)3−3=60, not defined. (Vertical line) (d) α=60∘⇒m=tan60∘=3.
Horizontal line ⇒m=0. Vertical line ⇒ slope undefined. Inclination given ⇒m=tan(inclination) (except 90∘).

9.2.3 Angle between two lines

For two lines in a plane: either parallel or intersecting. If they intersect, the angle(s) between them can be written using slopes. Let L1,L2 be two non-vertical lines with slopes m1,m2 and inclinations α1,α2, so m1=tanα1, m2=tanα2. If θ and ϕ are adjacent angles at the intersection, then θ+ϕ=180∘. Taking θ=α2−α1 (with α1,α2≠90∘):
tan of the angle between two lines (slopes form) tanθ=tan(α2−α1)=tanα2−tanα11+tanα1tanα2=m2−m11+m1m2, provided 1+m1m2≠0. θ: one angle between the two lines m1,m2: slopes of L1,L2
The adjacent angle is ϕ=180∘−θ, hence
tan of the adjacent angle tanϕ=tan(180∘−θ)=−tanθ=m1−m21+m1m2, provided 1+m1m2≠0. ϕ: adjacent angle between the lines (ϕ=180∘−θ) m1,m2: slopes of the lines
If 1+m1m2=0, don’t use the tanθ formula: the lines are perpendicular (m1m2=−1), so θ=90∘ directly.

Angle Between Two Lines (in Terms of Slopes)

For two non-vertical lines L1, L2 with slopes m1, m2, the angle(s) between them come from the tangent of the angle between their directions.

Sign cases and identifying acute/obtuse angle

Look at:
(m2−m1)(1+m1m2)m1, m2 = slopes of the two lines
  • Case I: If (m2 − m1) / (1 + m1m2) > 0, then one angle has tan positive and the other has tan negative → one angle is acute, the other is obtuse. (Name them θ and φ: tan θ > 0 ⇒ θ acute; tan φ < 0 ⇒ φ obtuse.)
  • Case II: If (m2 − m1) / (1 + m1m2) < 0, then tan θ is negative and tan φ is positive → θ is obtuse and φ is acute.

Formula for the acute angle between two lines

tanθ=(m2−m1)(1+m1m2),whereθistakenastheacuteangleθ = acute angle between the lines m1, m2 = slopes of the lines Condition: 1 + m1m2 ≠ 0 (if = 0, lines are perpendicular and tan θ is undefined)
Obtuse angle comes from the acute one: φ = 180° − θ

Given: Line through (−2, 6) and (4, 8) is perpendicular to the line through (8, 12) and (x, 24). Find x. Step 1: Slope of first line

9.3 Various Forms of the Equation of a Line

A line has infinitely many points. The equation of a line is an algebraic condition in x and y that is:
  • true for points (x, y) on the line
  • false for points not on the line
We pick different forms depending on what info is given.

9.3.1 Horizontal and vertical lines

Horizontal line: ∥ x-axis ⇒ y-coordinate (ordinate) stays constant. Vertical line: ∥ y-axis ⇒ x-coordinate (abscissa) stays constant.
Horizontal line at distance a from x-axis:
  • Above x-axis: y = a
  • Below x-axis: y = −a
Vertical line at distance b from y-axis:
  • Right of y-axis: x = b
  • Left of y-axis: x = −b
Sign comes from position:abovebelowfory=±a,rightleftforx=±b.

Example 4: Lines parallel to axes through a given point

Given: Find equations of lines ∥ axes through (−2, 3).
  • ∥ x-axis ⇒ y constant = 3 ⇒ y = 3
  • ∥ y-axis ⇒ x constant = −2 ⇒ x = −2

9.3.2 Point-slope form

For a non-vertical line with slope m through fixed point P0(x0, y0), a point P(x, y) lies on it ⇔ slope between P and P0 is m.
From slope definition:
m=(y−y0)(x−x0)m: slope (x, y): general point on line (x0, y0): fixed point on line
Rearrange ⇒ point-slope form:
y − y0 = m(x − x0) m: slope (x0, y0): known point (x, y): any point on the line
This equation fully defines the line: every on-line point satisfies it; every off-line point doesn’t.

Example 5: Equation through a point with given slope

Given: Line through (−2, 3) with slope −4. Use y − y0 = m(x − x0), with (x0, y0) = (−2, 3), m = −4: y − 3 = −4(x − (−2)) = −4(x + 2) ⇒ y − 3 = −4x − 8 ⇒ 4x + y + 5 = 0

9.3.3 Two-point form

If a line passes through two distinct points P1(x1, y1) and P2(x2, y2), then P(x, y) is on the line ⇔ slopes P1P and P1P2 are equal (collinearity).
So: (y−y1)(x−x1)=(y2−y1)(x2−x1)
(y−y1)(x−x1)=(y2−y1)(x2−x1)(Two−pointform)(x1, y1), (x2, y2): given points (x, y): general point
If x2 = x1, line is vertical ⇒ slope form fails; equation is just x = x1.

Example 6: Equation through two points

Given: Line through (1, −1) and (3, 5). Use two-point form: (y−(−1))/(x−1)=(5−(−1))/(3−1)=62=3So y + 1 = 3(x − 1) ⇒ y + 1 = 3x − 3 ⇒ −3x + y + 4 = 0

9.3.4 Slope-intercept form

Case I: Given slope and y-intercept

y-intercept = directed distance c from origin to where line meets y-axis. Intercept point is (0, c) (c can be + or −).
Line with slope m through (0, c) (via point-slope):
y = mx + c (Slope-intercept form) m: slope c: y-intercept (y when x = 0) (x, y): any point on the line
c > 0 if line cuts y-axis above origin; c < 0 if it cuts below.

Case II: Given slope and x-intercept

x-intercept = directed distance d from origin to where line meets x-axis. Intercept point is (d, 0).
Line with slope m and x-intercept d:
y = m(x − d) m: slope d: x-intercept (x when y = 0) (x, y): any point on the line

Given:tanθ=12,whereθistheinclination.
Slope for inclination θ:m=tanθ⇒herem=12.
(i)y−interceptis−23c=−23,m=12.Usey=mx+c:y=(12)x−23⇒0=(12)x−y−23(equivalentrearrangement)(ii) x-intercept is 4 d=4,m=12.Usey=m(x−d):y=(12)(x−4)⇒0=(12)x−y−2(equivalentrearrangement)
Exam move: if inclination θ is given, first do m = tan θ, then pick the right form: y = mx + c (y-intercept) or y = m(x − d) (x-intercept).

9.3.5 Intercept Form of a Line

Intercepts: If line cuts x-axis at x-intercept = a and y-axis at y-intercept = b, then it passes through (a, 0) and (0, b).
Using two-point form through (a, 0) and (0, b) ⇒ intercept form:
Intercept form:xa+yb=1 x: x-coordinate of any point on line y: y-coordinate of any point on line a: x-intercept (meets x-axis at (a, 0)) b: y-intercept (meets y-axis at (0, b))
If a = 0 or b = 0, intercept form fails (division by zero). Then the line passes through origin—use slope form/general form instead.

Example 8: Line with given intercepts

Problem: Find equation of the line with intercepts –3 (x-axis) and 2 (y-axis). Given: a = –3, b = 2 Use:xa+yb=1 So,x(–3)+y2=1Multiply by 6: –2x + 3y = 6 General form: 2x – 3y + 6 = 0 (any equivalent form ok)
General linear equation (general equation of a line): Any equation Ax + By + C = 0, where A and B are not both zero, represents a straight line.
Don’t write “A and B are nonzero”. Correct: A and B are not zero simultaneously (at least one is nonzero).

9.4 Distance of a Point from a Line

Distance of a point from a line = length of the perpendicular from the point to the line.
Line: L: Ax + By + C = 0, Point: P(x1, y1). Drop perpendicular PM to the line ⇒ distance d = PM. Derivation idea (as in text):
  • Line cuts x-axis at Q(−C/A, 0) and y-axis at R(0, −C/B).
  • Area: area(ΔPQR) = (1/2)·PM·QR ⇒ PM = 2·area(ΔPQR)/QR.
  • Find area(ΔPQR) (coordinate area formula) + find QR (distance formula), then substitute into PM to get the standard formula.
Perpendicular distance (point → line): d=|Ax1+By1+C|A²+B²d = perpendicular distance from point to line (x1, y1) = given point Ax + By + C = 0 = line (general form) A, B, C = real constants; A and B not both zero
Put absolute value in the numerator so distance is never negative.

9.4.1 Distance between Two Parallel Lines

Parallel lines ⇒ same slope. So they can be written as: y = mx + c1 and y = mx + c2
Idea of derivation: Take point A where y=mx+c1 meets the x-axis. Setting y=0 , x=− c1 m , so A ( − c1 m ,0 ) The distance between the lines is the perpendicular distance from A to y=mx+c2 . Use the point-to-line distance formula.
Distance between parallel lines (slope–intercept form): d=|c1−c2|1+m²d = distance between the two parallel lines m = common slope c1, c2 = y-intercepts
Distance between parallel lines (general form): If Ax + By + C1 = 0 and Ax + By + C2 = 0, then d=|C1−C2|A²+B²A, B = same in both lines C1, C2 = constants of the two lines
For the general-form parallel distance formula, coefficients of x and y must be identical (same A, same B). If not, rewrite first (or confirm they’re actually parallel).

Miscellaneous Examples

Example 11: Concurrency of three lines

Concurrent lines: three lines pass through a common point. Equivalent idea: intersection of any two lies on the third.
Problem: If the lines
  • 2x + y − 3 = 0
  • 5x + ky − 3 = 0
  • 3x − y − 2 = 0
are concurrent, find k. Step 1: Intersection of two lines (use (1) and (3)): (1) 2x + y = 3 (3) 3x − y = 2 Add: 5x = 5 ⇒ x = 1 Put in (1): 2(1) + y = 3 ⇒ y = 1 Intersection point = (1, 1) Step 2: Concurrency condition ⇒ (1,1) lies on line (2): 5(1) + k(1) − 3 = 0 ⇒ 2 + k = 0 ⇒ k = −2
Concurrency: don’t solve all 3 at once. Find intersection of any 2, then substitute into the 3rd.

Distance of a Line from a Point Measured Along a Given Direction (Worked Idea)

Distance of a line from a point measured along another line: Through the point, draw the line in the given direction → meet the given line → required distance = distance between the point and that intersection.
Method (always 3 steps):
  1. Equation of the direction line through the point using slope m = tan θ.
  2. Find intersection with the given line (solve the pair).
  3. Use distance formula between the point and intersection point.

Example 12: Distance of 4x − y = 0 from P(4, 1) measured along a line at 135°

m = tan 135° = −1. Line through P(4,1):

Image of a Point in a Line (Plane Mirror Concept)

If a line behaves like a plane mirror, then the image Q of a point P is such that:
  • Mirror line is the perpendicular bisector of segment PQ.
  • So: (i) PQ ⟂ mirror line, and (ii) midpoint of PQ lies on the mirror line.

20. Line equidistant from two parallel lines

Two ∥ lines ⇒ locus of points equidistant from them = the two angle bisectors (internal + external). For ∥ lines, these bisectors are also ∥ to the given lines and lie halfway between them.
Step 1: Make both lines have same coeffs of x, y (same A, B).
Step 2: Use “equal perpendicular distances” (distance-from-line formula).
Given: L1: 9x + 6y − 7 = 0 L2: 3x + 2y + 6 = 0 Make coeffs same ⇒ multiply L2 by 3: L2: 9x + 6y + 18 = 0 Equidistant condition for any point (x, y): |9x+6y−7|9²+6²=|9x+6y+18|9²+6²Same denominator ⇒ |9x + 6y − 7| = |9x + 6y + 18| Cases:
  • 9x + 6y − 7 = 9x + 6y + 18 (impossible since −7 ≠ 18)

Summary: Straight Lines (Must-Know Results)

Slope of a line

m=(y2−y1)(x2−x1),providedx2≠x1m = slope of the non-vertical line through (x1, y1) and (x2, y2) x1, y1, x2, y2 = coordinates of two distinct points on the line
m = tan α, α ≠ 90° m = slope of a line making angle α with the positive x-axis α = inclination of the line with +x direction
Horizontal line ⇒ slope = 0. Vertical line ⇒ slope undefined (division by 0 in slope formula).

Angle between two lines

tanθ=|(m2−m1)(1+m1m2)|,with1+m1m2≠0θ = acute angle between lines L1 and L2 m1, m2 = slopes of L1, L2

Parallelism, perpendicularity, and collinearity

  • Parallel lines ⇔ slopes equal: m1 = m2.
  • Perpendicular lines ⇔ product of slopes = −1: m1m2 = −1.
  • Points A, B, C collinear ⇔ slope(AB) = slope(BC).

Equations of special lines at a fixed distance from axes

y = a or y = −a y = horizontal line at distance a from the x-axis a = perpendicular distance from x-axis (a > 0)
x = b or x = −b x = vertical line at distance b from the y-axis b = perpendicular distance from y-axis (b > 0)

Point–slope form

y − y0 = m(x − x0) (x, y) = any point on the line (x0, y0) = fixed point the line passes through m = slope of the line

Two-point form

(y−y1)(y2−y1)=(x−x1)(x2−x1)(x, y) = any point on the required line (x1, y1), (x2, y2) = two given distinct points on the line

Slope–intercept form

y = mx + c m = slope of the line c = y-intercept (value of y where x = 0)

Form using x-intercept

y = m(x − d) m = slope of the line d = x-intercept (value of x where y = 0)

Intercept form

xa+yb=1a = intercept made on x-axis b = intercept made on y-axis (x, y) = any point on the line

General equation of a line

Any equation Ax + By + C = 0 (A and B not both 0 at the same time) is the general linear equation (general equation) of a straight line.

Perpendicular distance from a point to a line

d=|Ax1+By1+C|A²+B²d = perpendicular distance from point (x1, y1) to line Ax + By + C = 0 A, B, C = coefficients of the line (x1, y1) = given point

Distance between two parallel lines

d=|C2−C1|A²+B²d = distance between Ax + By + C1 = 0 and Ax + By + C2 = 0 A, B = same coefficients for both lines (parallel condition) C1, C2 = constants of the two lines
The parallel-lines distance formula works only if both lines have identical A and B. If not, first multiply one (or both) equations so A and B match.
